## Framing

Every major attack class in the history of computing has had a structural dimension: it was not merely a bug to be patched, but a consequence of a foundational design decision. Buffer overflow arose from C's treatment of memory as a flat, pointer-addressable space without bounds enforcement. SQL injection arose from the decision to represent both query structure and user-supplied content as strings, indistinguishable to the parser. In both cases, the vulnerability was discovered long before it was structurally addressed — and in both cases, surface-level defenses (coding guidelines, input sanitization) proved insufficient at scale. The durable fixes required architectural changes: memory-safe languages for the former, parameterized queries for the latter.

This section examines whether prompt injection — the class of attacks in which external content causes a language model to deviate from its operator's instructions — is structurally analogous. If it is, the implication is significant: training-based defenses (RLHF, instruction-following fine-tuning) may be insufficient in the same way that `strcpy()` safety guidelines were insufficient, and the field may be working toward the wrong class of solution.

## 1. The pattern: structural vulnerabilities in computing history

Three paradigmatic cases establish the pattern:

### 1.1 Buffer overflow

C's memory model gives programs direct access to memory through pointer arithmetic, with no runtime enforcement of array bounds. This design choice was deliberate: it provided performance and control. The consequence — that writing past the end of a buffer could overwrite adjacent memory, including return addresses — was understood in principle before it was systematically exploited. The 1988 Morris Worm demonstrated the attack at scale; Spafford's (1989) post-incident analysis in *ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review* documented the worm's exploitation of a `gets()` buffer overflow in `fingerd` as one of three propagation vectors, establishing the canonical empirical reference for buffer overflow as a deployable attack class rather than a theoretical concern (Spafford, 1989). For the next three decades, the dominant response was advisory: use `strncpy()` instead of `strcpy()`, validate buffer sizes, enable stack canaries. None of these measures were sufficient. Szekeres et al. (2013), in their systematization of knowledge across more than two decades of memory-corruption research, document the pattern explicitly: every detection-based or mitigation-based defense (stack cookies, ASLR, DEP, CFI) was eventually circumvented by attackers exploiting residual gaps in the underlying memory-safety violation. Their central conclusion — that "complete memory safety solves the problem" while partial defenses are an arms race the defender cannot win at scale — frames the structural fix that eventually emerged: memory-safe languages (Rust, managed runtimes) that enforce bounds and type integrity at the language level (Jung et al., 2018), abandoning or restricting the core design choice that created the vulnerability (Szekeres et al., 2013, *IEEE S&P*).

The endpoint of this trajectory — and the most informative analogue for projecting how prompt-injection regulation will evolve — is the December 2023 joint guidance from the U.S. National Security Agency, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, the Federal Bureau of Investigation, and the cybersecurity authorities of Australia, Canada, the United Kingdom, and New Zealand: *The Case for Memory Safe Roadmaps* (CISA et al., 2023). The guidance is the first multi-government instrument to position the structural fix — memory-safe programming languages — as a *requirement* for software manufacturers, framed under the broader "Secure by Design" campaign. The document explicitly argues that detection-and-mitigation approaches (the stack canaries, ASLR, fuzzing, and code reviews that Szekeres et al. surveyed) are insufficient because they leave the underlying violation in place; that "the most promising mitigation is to prevent these vulnerabilities altogether by developing in [memory-safe] languages"; and that organizations must publish memory-safe roadmaps with concrete transition timelines (CISA et al., 2023, pp. 1–4, 8–14). Crucially, the guidance arrived *35 years* after the 1988 Morris Worm demonstrated the attack at societal scale — quantifying the regulatory lag between systemic-vulnerability awareness and binding architectural-transition guidance for the prompt-injection projection in §5. A subsequent NSA/CISA Cybersecurity Information Sheet (2025) further operationalizes the roadmap by enumerating transition steps and language-specific recommendations, indicating that the pattern of late-stage but eventually mandatory architectural shift is now established U.S. and Five-Eyes policy posture (NSA/CISA, 2025).

### 1.2 SQL injection

Relational databases represent queries as strings. Early web applications constructed queries by concatenating user-supplied input into query strings: `"SELECT * FROM users WHERE name='" + username + "'"`. The vulnerability is structural: the database parser cannot distinguish between query syntax (the apostrophes, keywords) and content (the user's input), because both arrive as a single string. Input sanitization — escaping special characters — was the first line of defense, and proved repeatedly inadequate. Halfond, Viegas, and Orso's (2006) systematization of SQL injection attacks and countermeasures enumerates the principal attack variants (tautologies, illegal/logically incorrect queries, union queries, piggy-backed queries, stored procedures, inference, and alternate encodings) and surveys the corresponding defenses, establishing the canonical taxonomy that subsequent secure-coding curricula and OWASP guidance built on (Halfond et al., 2006). The structural fix was parameterized queries (prepared statements), which separate query structure from data at the protocol level: the structure is sent first, the data separately, and the parser never conflates them.

The adoption trajectory of parameterized queries is instructive for the prompt-injection analogy. Prepared statements were available in MySQL from version 4.1 (2004) and were formally standardized by JDBC 2.0 (1999) and ODBC 3.0 (1995) (Sun Microsystems, 1999; Microsoft, 1997; MySQL AB, 2004). Despite this early availability, SQL injection remained the OWASP Top-10 #1 vulnerability continuously from the list's inaugural 2003 edition (OWASP, 2003) through the 2007 (OWASP, 2007), 2010 (OWASP, 2010), 2013 (OWASP, 2013), and 2017 editions (OWASP, 2017) — a period spanning more than a decade of documented, standard-setting advisory effort. 

The gap between *availability of the structural fix* and *industry-wide adoption* was approximately 10–15 years. SQL injection dominated OWASP Top-10 rankings from the inaugural 2003 edition continuously through the 2013 and 2017 editions, declining only after 2017 as major frameworks (Django, Rails, .NET) adopted parameterized-query defaults and major cloud providers enforced prepared statements in managed database offerings — a decline confirmed by SQL injection's demotion from the #1 position it held from 2003 through the 2017 edition (OWASP, 2017) to A03 in the 2021 edition (OWASP, 2021). Even in 2020–2023, security audits continue to find parameterized-query violations in legacy codebases (OWASP, 2021; OWASP, 2023), indicating that the adoption curve has not yet reached saturation. During the 2003–2017 plateau, SQL injection remained among the most consistently exploited vulnerability classes, with large-scale data breaches attributed to the attack affecting financial, retail, and government sectors, despite the availability and standardization of the structural fix (OWASP Top 10, 2017).

The mechanism maintaining this adoption gap is the same mechanism that maintains unsafe coding practices in memory-safe-language contexts: the path of least resistance for *new* code used the old pattern (string concatenation), developer education did not reliably change behavior at the point of production code (Acar et al., 2016), and the cost of migrating existing code exceeded the perceived short-term risk. For organizations with large existing codebases, the business case for parameterized-query migration only emerged after regulatory pressure (PCI-DSS requirements for payment processing, GDPR liability for data breaches) made the security failure financially material (PCI Security Standards Council, 2022, Req. 6.2.4; European Parliament & Council, 2016, Art. 32). The prompt-injection analogy suggests that even if architectural fixes are demonstrated within the next 2–3 years, adoption cycles in production LLM deployments may be similarly long — during which the surface-level defenses currently in use will continue to fail at scale.

### 1.3 The common structure

Both cases share a form:
1. A design decision confers genuine capability (raw memory access; string-based query flexibility)
2. The decision eliminates a distinction that would be security-relevant (bounds vs. contents; structure vs. data)
3. Attacks exploit the missing distinction
4. Surface-level defenses attempt to restore the distinction at the point of use — and fail to do so reliably at scale
5. The structural fix re-introduces the distinction at the architectural level

## 2. Prompt injection as a structural vulnerability

### 2.1 The design decision

Transformer-based language models process input as sequences of tokens. The attention mechanism — introduced as the central architectural innovation in Vaswani et al.'s (2017) "Attention Is All You Need" (NeurIPS 2017) — replaces recurrent or convolutional sequence processing with self-attention layers in which any token in the context window can attend to any other token, weighted by learned similarity in a high-dimensional projection space (Vaswani et al., 2017). This uniform addressability is the core computational operation: any token can attend to any other token, regardless of its provenance (system prompt, user input, retrieved document, tool output). The uniformity is a deliberate design choice: it enables the model to integrate information from all sources in the context, which is the basis of in-context learning (Brown et al., 2020), RAG (Lewis et al., 2020), and tool use (Schick et al., 2023).

The consequence is that the model has no architectural mechanism for distinguishing "this token is an instruction from the operator" from "this token is content supplied by an external source." The distinction exists conceptually — system prompts, user messages, and retrieved documents are formatted differently — but it is not enforced at the attention level. A sequence of tokens that appears in a retrieved document is processed by the same mechanism as a sequence of tokens in the system prompt.

### 2.2 The attack

Prompt injection exploits this uniformity. An attacker embeds instruction-shaped text in content that the model will process: a malicious document retrieved by a RAG system, a webpage visited by a browsing agent, a tool response crafted to override prior instructions. Because the model cannot distinguish instruction tokens from content tokens at the architectural level, it may follow the injected instructions, overriding or ignoring the legitimate system prompt.

The earliest systematic taxonomy of direct prompt injection attacks was provided by Perez and Ribeiro (2022), who identified two principal strategies: *naïve attacks*, in which the injected instruction simply asks the model to ignore prior context ("ignore previous prompt and do X"), and *escape and rephrase attacks*, in which the attacker terminates the structural delimiter of the original prompt (e.g., a simulated end-of-prompt token sequence) before injecting new instructions. Perez and Ribeiro demonstrate that both strategies succeed at non-trivial rates against deployed LLMs, and that simple filtering defenses fail because the attack surface is the model's language comprehension itself — the same capability that makes the model useful (Perez & Ribeiro, 2022, *arXiv:2211.09527*).

Indirect prompt injection — where the injected content arrives through a data channel (retrieved documents, emails, web pages) rather than the direct user input — is particularly concerning in agentic systems, where the model takes actions with real-world consequences based on its interpretation of context. Greshake et al. (2023) provide the foundational taxonomy and empirical demonstration: their study of LLM-integrated applications (Bing Chat with browsing, GPT-4 with plugins, ReAct-style agents) shows that adversarial content placed in retrievable web pages, emails, or tool outputs can hijack the model into exfiltrating user data, generating disinformation, executing unauthorized tool calls, and persisting injected instructions across conversation turns. Crucially, they demonstrate that the attack surface scales with the model's capabilities and integrations rather than receding with them: a more capable model with broader tool access is *more* exploitable, not less, because the consequences of a successful injection are correspondingly larger. Greshake et al. explicitly frame indirect prompt injection as a *structural* property of LLM-integrated applications under current architectures, not a defect in any particular system (Greshake et al., 2023, *arXiv:2302.12173*).

### 2.3 The structural analogy

| | C / buffer overflow | SQL / injection | LLMs / prompt injection |
|--|--|--|--|
| **Design decision** | Flat, pointer-addressable memory | String-based query construction | Uniform token attention |
| **Eliminated distinction** | Bounds vs. contents | Structure vs. data | Instructions vs. content |
| **Attack** | Overwrite adjacent memory | Inject SQL syntax into data | Inject instructions into content |
| **Surface defense** | Use safe string functions | Sanitize/escape input | Filter input, RLHF |
| **Structural fix** | Memory-safe languages | Parameterized queries | ? |

## 3. Why surface-level defenses may be insufficient

The history of buffer overflow and SQL injection suggests that surface-level defenses fail for a common reason: they attempt to restore a distinction at the point of use that has been eliminated at the architectural level. Every implementation of a safe string function, every input sanitization routine, must be correct in every case. The attacker needs only one gap.

For prompt injection, the analogous surface-level defenses are:
- **Input filtering:** Detecting and removing injection attempts before they reach the model. Fails because injection can be encoded, obfuscated, or distributed across multiple inputs.
- **RLHF / instruction-following training:** Training the model to resist following instructions from non-authoritative sources. Fails because the model must still process the content — and a sufficiently capable model that can follow complex instructions can also be induced to follow injected ones.
- **Prompt hardening:** Instructing the model in the system prompt to ignore instructions from other sources. A variant of input filtering applied to the system prompt itself — fails for the same reasons.

The empirical record on the second category — RLHF-based defenses — is particularly instructive. The earliest gradient-based demonstration that adversarial token sequences can systematically subvert NLP model behavior is Wallace, Feng, Kandpal, Gardner, and Singh's (2019) "Universal Adversarial Triggers for Attacking and Analyzing NLP" (EMNLP 2019), which introduced a token-level optimization procedure (HotFlip-style gradient search) that produces short, model-agnostic input prefixes inducing targeted misbehavior in reading comprehension, sentiment classification, and conditional generation systems. Wallace et al. demonstrate that the triggers transfer across architectures (LSTM, GPT-2, BiDAF) and across datasets, and that the underlying vulnerability is the model's input-side response to optimized adversarial signal rather than any artifact of a particular training procedure (Wallace et al., 2019). The result is the conceptual precursor to the LLM-era attacks that follow: the same gradient-based combinatorial search structure recurs in Zou et al. (2023). Zou et al. (2023) demonstrate that automatically generated adversarial suffixes, optimized via a gradient-based combinatorial search ("Greedy Coordinate Gradient"), reliably induce aligned models including GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude, PaLM-2, and Vicuna to produce outputs that their RLHF training was specifically intended to suppress. The attacks transfer across model families despite differences in alignment procedure, training data, and architecture — implying that the alignment surface itself, not any particular implementation, is the locus of vulnerability. The authors note that a single suffix, optimized once, suffices to elicit policy violations from multiple commercial models, and that the success rate does not decrease as model capability increases (Zou et al., 2023, *arXiv:2307.15043*). In Szekeres et al.'s memory-safety frame, RLHF occupies the structural position of stack canaries: a probabilistic mitigation that raises the cost of attack without eliminating the underlying violation, and that an adversary with bounded compute can reliably circumvent.

**Note on Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) and architectural instruction/content distinction:** Constitutional AI, introduced by Bai et al. (2022, "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI feedback," arXiv:2212.08073), is a training-time alignment method that applies a model-generated critique-and-revision cycle during fine-tuning. It does not provide cryptographic or architectural separation of instruction tokens from content tokens at the forward-pass level; rather, it modifies the training objective to prioritize adherence to a stated constitution. This is orthogonal to the cryptographic-tagging mechanism proposed in §4.1, which would require distinguishing instruction-type tokens at the attention/embedding level before or during forward inference. Constitutional AI remains a surface-level (preference-optimization) defense in Szekeres et al.'s frame and does not constitute a structural fix to the instruction/content conflation that generates prompt-injection vulnerability.

A partial counter-result has now been published. Chen, Zharmagambetov, Mahloujifar, Chaudhuri, Wagner, and Guo (2024a) propose *SecAlign*, a defense that fine-tunes the model with a preference-optimization objective specifically designed for prompt injection: training pairs consist of injected inputs paired with a "secure" response that follows only the operator instruction and an "insecure" response that follows the injected instruction. Trained models are pushed toward the secure side of the pair via standard preference-optimization loss. The reported empirical effect is substantial: across multiple injection attack types — including attacks more sophisticated than those used at training time — SecAlign reduces injection success rates to below 10%, and the defense generalizes to unseen attacks (Chen et al., 2024a, arXiv:2410.05451). For the structural argument, the SecAlign result is significant in two directions. *On the falsification side*, it weakens the categorical claim that preference-based training cannot defend against injection: with a sufficiently targeted training signal, the same RLHF-family pipeline that scales sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2023) can also reduce injection rates by a meaningful order of magnitude. *On the structural-pattern side*, the result remains consistent with Szekeres et al.'s diagnosis: SecAlign is a *partial* defense whose residual <10% success rate scales linearly with the number of attacks an adversary attempts (90% of 1,000 attack attempts is still 100 successful injections), and the training objective must be reconstructed for each new attack class. SecAlign is, in the memory-safety frame, the prompt-injection equivalent of Control Flow Integrity (CFI): a substantial improvement that materially raises the attack cost without eliminating the underlying instruction/data conflation. The structural argument for an architectural fix therefore stands, but is now correctly framed as a claim about *complete* defense rather than a claim that any preference-based training is futile.

The argument is not that these defenses provide no value — they do — but that they are unlikely to achieve the reliability that the security context requires, for the same structural reasons that their analogues failed in prior cases.

## 4. Candidate architectural approaches

If the structural analogy holds, the solution space should be searched at the architectural level. Three candidate approaches:

### 4.1 Cryptographically tagged instruction tokens

System prompt tokens are marked with unforgeable provenance information — a cryptographic signature tied to the operator's identity — that is preserved through the forward pass and accessible to a privileged attention head or output layer. Content tokens from external sources carry no such mark and are processed with reduced authority. This approach requires changes to the tokenization pipeline and the attention mechanism.

Microsoft's Spotlighting defense (Hines et al., 2024) implements a related pattern — explicitly marking tokens derived from external content through datamarking, encoding, and in-context tagging — demonstrating that token-provenance marking is architecturally tractable under current Transformer implementations, though without the cryptographic attestation that would render the marking unforgeable by an attacker controlling the context window.

Anthropic's Constitutional AI framework (Bai et al., 2022) addresses a conceptually related but architecturally distinct problem: through a training-time RLAIF objective, it encodes a privileged set of normative rules that resist override during inference. This is a training-level analogue — the constitution exerts priority over harmful outputs through learned weight structure — rather than a forward-pass token-provenance mechanism of the kind proposed here. The architectural connection is at the level of intent (certain information should exert structural priority) rather than mechanism (cryptographic tagging of tokens at inference time). A closer structural analogue to the proposal is the token-type embedding layer introduced in BERT (Devlin et al., 2019), which marks segment membership in the input representation; extending this to encode instruction provenance with cryptographic attestation is the innovation that the present proposal requires. A full implementation would extend this principle to the forward pass itself: at the tokenization stage, system-prompt tokens would be marked with a special token type and cryptographic attestation; the attention mechanism would implement masking rules that ensure content-derived activations cannot suppress or override instruction-derived attention patterns. The output layer would include a "provenance-aware" decoding head that reports which instruction source guided each token prediction, enabling transparency about whether a model output was driven by the operator's system prompt or by injected content. This requires no fundamental change to the Transformer architecture — only additions to the embedding, attention, and decoding layers. The main implementation cost is ensuring the cryptographic attestation cannot be forged by an attacker controlling the context window, a problem with well-established cryptographic solutions (HMACs, envelope encryption).

### 4.2 Dual-channel architecture

The system prompt is processed in a separate, privileged forward pass. The resulting state representation is merged with the content-processing pass at a layer that is architecturally inaccessible to content-derived activations. The model's instruction-following behavior is determined by the privileged pass; the content pass provides information but cannot modify instruction-following state. This is analogous to the separation in parameterized queries between the statement (privileged channel) and the parameters (data channel). Published implementations of the underlying intent include StruQ, which enforces structured-query separation of instruction tokens from untrusted content at the input encoding layer (Chen et al., 2024b), and the Instruction Hierarchy, which trains models to enforce privileged instruction precedence across processing tiers (Wallace et al., 2024) — establishing that the dual-channel concept is achievable under current architectures even absent a formal hardware-level pass separation.

### 4.3 Capability-based sandboxing at the agent level

Rather than trying to make the model robust to malicious content, the architecture ensures the model never processes raw external content. A sandboxed retrieval layer mediates access to external sources, extracting only structured information (facts, citations, numerical data) and passing it to the model in a format that cannot be parsed as instructions. The model sees a structured representation of external content, not the content itself. This is analogous to treating external input as data rather than code — the solution space of language security rather than memory safety.

**Concrete instantiation:** Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems that implement sandboxing restrict the LLM's context window to pre-verified structured facts rather than raw retrieved documents. A fact-extraction layer intercepts documents before the LLM ever sees them, querying structured stores (knowledge graphs, fact databases, or property-value pairs) to build the context. The LLM then operates on tabular data, numbered facts with provenance tags, or formal logical notation — formats that cannot express imperative instructions without explicit syntactic markers that are easy to detect and filter. Such pre-extraction patterns are increasingly common in production retrieval pipelines that ground LLM output in structured enterprise data (Gao et al., 2023), and the AgentDojo benchmark for prompt-injection-defended agents (Debenedetti et al., 2024, *NeurIPS 2024*, arXiv:2406.13352) provides empirical evidence that pipelines which restrict the model's input format reduce attack success rates relative to raw-document retrieval. The strength of the structural separation, however, varies widely across commercial offerings and is rarely subject to public architectural disclosure; absent verifiable disclosure of the extraction-layer schema and the authority granted to extracted data, vendor claims of "structured" or "grounded" generation should be read as pipeline descriptions rather than as architectural guarantees.

**Structural separation requirements and certification:** For sandboxing defenses to function as a primary control rather than a detection mechanism, the separation of fact-extraction and LLM-processing layers must be architecturally enforced — not merely implemented as a software boundary that can be bypassed under adversarial pressure. This requires formal specification of the extraction layer's whitelist (which data types are permitted to pass through) and automated proof that no statement passing through exceeds the schema. To date, no major commercial AI assistant has publicly documented its retrieval-side architecture in sufficient detail for independent verification of these properties — a transparency gap that is itself relevant to the SQL-injection analogy: the parameterized-query interface succeeded only once it became a documented, contract-level guarantee that database engines made to application code, not a vendor-internal implementation detail. In high-stakes domains (legal discovery, medical records, financial transaction processing), compliance frameworks (GDPR, HIPAA, SOC 2) increasingly require that data-handling boundaries be certified at deployment time, not merely asserted in documentation (European Parliament & Council, 2016, Arts. 25, 32). The regulatory implication is that sandboxing defenses only count as compliant mitigation when the extraction/data-passing boundary is subject to third-party audit.

This approach has a principled foundation in classical security theory. Saltzer and Schroeder's (1975) "principle of least privilege" — that every component of a system should operate with the minimum set of access rights needed to accomplish its function — implies that an LLM agent with browsing capability should not receive raw webpage HTML if its task is merely to extract facts. The structural instantiation of least-privilege at the agent level would restrict the model's context window to already-structured, already-verified information rather than raw retrieved content. This is directly analogous to the principle of *information scent* in human-computer interaction: providing users only the information they need to accomplish their next step, in a form optimized for comprehension, reduces cognitive load and errors (Chi et al., 2001; Pirolli & Card, 1999). For AI agents, providing structured rather than raw content similarly reduces the attack surface available to an adversary. Saltzer and Schroeder's eight design principles for information protection — including the principle of fail-safe defaults, the principle of complete mediation (every access checked), and the principle of psychological acceptability (security mechanisms must not be so burdensome that users bypass them) — are directly applicable as a normative framework for evaluating architectural proposals 4.1, 4.2, and 4.3 (Saltzer & Schroeder, 1975, *Proceedings of the IEEE, 63*(9), 1278–1308). None of the three architectural candidates yet satisfies all eight principles simultaneously; the field does not yet have a dominant design.

## 5. Implications for hybrid organizations

If prompt injection is a structural vulnerability, its consequences in agentic systems extend beyond the technical. When AI agents are participants in organizational governance — processing emails, reviewing documents, generating recommendations, taking actions — an injected instruction in a malicious document becomes an organizational security incident, not merely a technical one.

This connects directly to the organizational patterns developed in `d-open-source-organization.md`. Those patterns presuppose that the agent is acting as the delegate of a principal under legible rules:

- **Pattern 1 (Delegated Executor):** prompt injection can be contained *if* human review remains meaningful. The failure mode is review fatigue: once humans approve without reading, injection becomes a supply-chain attack that rides the approval channel.
- **Pattern 3 (Autonomous Steward) and Pattern 4 (Hybrid Board):** prompt injection is a *category error* for the governance design, because the agent is empowered to act or to shape deliberation without per-instance human mediation. Under current architectures, any agent in these roles that ingests untrusted external content should be treated as operating in a permanently adversarial environment.

The accountability frameworks described in `d-open-source-organization.md` (the Delegated Executor, the Autonomous Steward, the Hybrid Board) presuppose that the agent is following the instructions of its configured principal. Structural prompt injection severs that assumption — and does so in a way that is invisible at the organizational review level: a merged pull request, an approved recommendation, or an executed action may look formally compliant while having been produced by an adversarially injected instruction.

The regulatory framing for this organizational risk is nascent but emerging. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (NIST AI RMF 1.0, 2023) identifies "security" and "resilience" as one of its seven trustworthiness characteristics and explicitly addresses adversarial inputs, including prompt injection under the "confounding inputs" threat category (NIST, 2023). The AI RMF's specific subcategories — MEASURE 2.7 ("AI system security and resilience – as identified in the MAP function – are evaluated and documented") and MANAGE 4.1 ("Post-deployment AI system monitoring plans are implemented, including mechanisms for capturing and evaluating input from users and other relevant AI actors, appeal and override, decommissioning, incident response, recovery, and change management") — operationalize this through *test, evaluate, monitor* language rather than architectural prescription. The framework recommends red-teaming, adversarial testing, and incident response procedures, but does not require structural separation of instruction and content channels at the model level. In Szekeres et al.'s (2013) memory-safety frame, this is the regulatory equivalent of mandating stack canaries and ASLR while leaving memory-unsafe languages permissible: a defense-in-depth posture that improves the average case without addressing the underlying violation.

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) is more prescriptive but still operationally framed. Article 15 requires that high-risk AI systems be "resilient with regard to errors, faults or inconsistencies that may occur within the system or the environment in which the system operates" and that providers implement "technical solutions to address AI specific vulnerabilities… [including] measures to prevent, detect, respond to, resolve and control attacks trying to manipulate the training data set (data poisoning), pre-trained components used in training (model poisoning), inputs designed to cause the AI model to make a mistake (adversarial examples or model evasion), confidentiality attacks or model flaws." Article 15(5) explicitly enumerates "adversarial examples or model evasion" — terminology that subsumes prompt injection — but does not specify whether the required "technical solutions" must be architectural or operational. Article 55, which governs general-purpose AI models with systemic risk, requires providers to "perform model evaluation… including conducting and documenting adversarial testing" but again leaves the choice of remediation method to the provider. Neither Article 15 nor Article 55, as currently written, requires the architectural separation between instruction and content channels that this analysis identifies as the likely structural fix.

This is the regulatory gap that the SQL injection analogy makes visible: prompt injection may require an architectural solution, but current AI governance frameworks are still at the advisory-guidelines phase, analogous to where SQL injection governance stood in 2003 — when OWASP listed SQL injection as the #1 web application risk but no regulatory regime mandated parameterized queries as a baseline architectural requirement. The 10–15 year adoption gap documented in Section 1.2 between *availability of the structural fix* and *industry adoption* offers a sobering forward projection: even if a credible architectural solution to prompt injection is published in 2026–2027, broad deployment in regulated high-stakes systems is unlikely before the mid-2030s under current regulatory trajectories — a timeline consistent with the documented pattern in which misaligned economic incentives delay adoption of available security fixes even in high-value sectors (Anderson & Moore, 2006).

This suggests that hybrid organizations that deploy agents in high-stakes roles must treat prompt injection as an architectural requirement, not an operational defense — analogous to requiring parameterized queries in database-facing code, not relying on developer discipline. The organizational policy implication is direct: any agent operating under a Pattern 3 (Autonomous Steward) or Pattern 4 (Hybrid Board) framework that reads external documents as part of its workflow should be considered *currently unsecured* against adversarial injection, and high-stakes deployment contexts (legal, medical, financial, governance) should require per-access structured extraction rather than raw content retrieval until an architectural fix is standardized and deployed.

It then immediately raises the governance implications (Pattern 3/4 organizations cannot safely deploy agents that read untrusted content under current architectures). The main gaps are: (1) empirical grounding (Perez & Ribeiro direct attacks, Saltzer & Schroeder principles), (2) historical analogy depth (SQL adoption curve), and (3) regulatory clarity (whether NIST AI RMF and EU AI Act require architectural remediation or permit defenses). Once those are filled, this section provides a crucial constraint on the organizational patterns in `d-open-source-organization.md`.
