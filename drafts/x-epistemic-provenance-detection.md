# Draft X — Authenticating Human Knowledge: Provenance, Detection, and the Epistemic Arms Race

## 1. The Problem: Synthetic Knowledge at Industrial Scale

A foundational assumption of academic epistemology is that the literature represents the accumulated trace of human intellectual effort — arguments made, evidence gathered, experiments run, failures reported, revisions negotiated in public. This assumption is breaking down. As Novy-Marx and Velikov (2025) demonstrated, a commercial language model can generate 288 complete academic papers — each with theoretically coherent framing, creative signal names, and citations to "existing (and, on occasion, imagined) literature" — in approximately 12 hours at a cost of $200–300 (NBER Working Paper 33363). The practical extension of this demonstration is straightforward: AI can generate not only papers but papers that cite each other. A synthetic citation network — a cluster of mutually-reinforcing AI-generated papers, each citing the others as authoritative precedent — is now technically trivial to construct (Cabanac et al., 2021). The unit cost of a fabricated peer review, a fabricated expert consensus, or a fabricated research tradition is approaching zero (Liang et al., 2024).

This creates a problem that is simultaneously technical, epistemological, and institutional. Technically, it demands detection methods capable of identifying AI-generated academic content. Epistemologically, it demands a richer account of what makes human knowledge distinctive — what properties a genuine article of scholarship possesses that a fluent synthetic approximation lacks. Institutionally, it demands governance mechanisms capable of maintaining epistemic integrity in publication systems that were designed for a world in which producing a convincing fake was expensive.

This draft surveys the theoretical and practical landscape, with a focus on: (1) what epistemological markers distinguish human from AI-generated knowledge; (2) what technical detection methods exist and what limits them; (3) how citation-network analysis can identify synthetic knowledge clusters; and (4) what the arms race dynamic implies for long-run governance of academic epistemics.

---

## 2. What Makes Human Knowledge Distinctive? Epistemological Markers

The starting point for detection is a theory of what authentic human knowledge looks like — not at the level of content (where AI can now approximate human outputs) but at the level of structure, process, and trace.

**Tacit knowledge and embodied knowing.** Michael Polanyi's distinction between explicit and tacit knowledge (Polanyi, 1966, *The Tacit Dimension*) identifies a class of human knowledge that is structurally resistant to verbalization and therefore to AI synthesis. Tacit knowledge is the "knowing how" that underlies "knowing that": the surgeon who can feel that a tissue is healthy, the experienced programmer who recognizes a code smell before articulating why, the ethnographer who has internalized a community's norms. Polanyi's aphorism — "we know more than we can tell" — identifies an asymmetry between human competence and its linguistic representation. AI systems trained on linguistic output inherit the explicit layer but not the tacit one (Bender & Koller, 2020). Genuine scholarship in empirically grounded fields carries the trace of this tacit layer: specific choices of methodology that reflect experimental experience, surprise at findings that contradicts expectations built up through practice, the specific texture of failure that is only achievable by having genuinely tried.

**Productive failure and surprise.** A distinctive marker of authentic human research is the presence of productive failure: the experiment that did not work as expected, the finding that contradicted the hypothesis, the anomaly that required theoretical revision. Merton's (1948) concept of the *serendipitous anomaly* — the unexpected observation that leads to a theoretical advance — captures something important: genuine knowledge-making involves encounters with a recalcitrant world that pushes back. AI systems, trained to produce plausible completions of a given prompt, systematically under-represent productive failure: they complete the research narrative in the direction the prompt implies (Sharma et al., 2023). The absence of negative results, revised hypotheses, and explicitly acknowledged limitations is a weak but real signal of synthetic generation.

**Temporal coherence and intellectual genealogy.** Human knowledge develops in time, with specific dependencies on what was known when. A paper written in 2018 could not cite a paper published in 2021; a research program builds on its own prior work in ways that are traceable; a scholar's vocabulary and conceptual framework evolve discernibly across a career. AI-generated academic content may exhibit anachronisms — citing work that did not yet exist at the putative time of composition, using terminology that post-dates the claimed publication year, exhibiting conceptual sophistication inconsistent with the field's state at the claimed time. Temporal-coherence analysis — checking whether the cited literature, conceptual vocabulary, and methodological repertoire are consistent with the claimed date of composition — is, we propose, a potentially powerful forensic tool; to our knowledge no empirical evaluation of its detection rate or false-positive rate has yet been published, and the present chapter advances it as a programmatic proposal for the detection literature rather than an established method.

**Controversy and community dynamics.** Real scientific communities disagree. Genuine fields contain factions, methodological debates, paradigm competitions, and personal rivalries that leave traceable marks in the literature — explicit engagements with critics, acknowledgments of limitations, responses to prior challenges. AI-generated papers tend toward synthetic consensus: they reproduce the distribution of mainstream opinion in their training data, which means they over-represent established views and under-represent genuine controversy. A citation network that exhibits suspiciously high agreement, absent the usual markers of methodological debate, is a candidate for synthetic fabrication. Fleck's (1935) sociology of knowledge — the concept of *Denkstil* (thought style) and the *Denkkollektiv* (thought collective) — identifies scientific knowledge as the product of a social process with specific internal dynamics; AI can simulate the output of this process but not the process itself.

## 3. Technical Detection Methods and Their Limits

**Statistical stylometry.** AI-generated text has characteristic statistical properties that deviate from human writing: lower perplexity under the generating model, more uniform word-choice distribution, characteristic n-gram patterns. Tools like GPTZero, Originality.ai, and academic-focused detectors exploit these patterns (Elkhatat et al., 2023). However, the reliability of such tools degrades as models improve and as adversarial prompting (instructing the model to vary its style) increases (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023). Liang et al. (2023) document a further problem: stylometric AI detectors exhibit systematic bias against non-native English writers, whose writing patterns can resemble AI output to classifiers trained predominantly on native English text — creating false-positive rates high enough to disqualify legitimate scholarship from non-Western researchers (a connection to Draft W's WEIRD bias argument).

**Watermarking.** Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers, and Goldstein (2023) introduced the foundational green-list watermarking technique for LLMs: at generation time, the model biases sampling toward a pseudorandomly defined subset of tokens, producing text that is statistically detectable without access to the underlying model (arXiv:2301.10226). Watermarking would allow journals and databases to cryptographically verify whether a submitted paper was generated by a watermarked model. However: (1) watermark adoption is poor — Rijsbosch, van Dijck, and Kollnig (2025) found that only 38% of generative AI systems implement adequate watermarking, and fewer than 18% implement adequate deep fake labelling, under the EU AI Act's August 2025 obligations; (2) watermarks can be removed or disrupted through paraphrasing and translation (Krishna et al., 2023); and (3) the arms race dynamic means that any widely deployed watermarking scheme becomes a training signal for evading it.

**Citation verification.** The simplest and most immediately actionable detection method is DOI verification: checking whether every citation in a submitted paper resolves to a real publication. Imagined literature fails this check at the first step. Systems like CrossRef's API, the Unpaywall metadata service, and Semantic Scholar's citation database allow automated resolution of a paper's entire reference list in seconds. Journals that implement mandatory automated citation verification at submission would immediately screen out the most naive form of AI hallucination. The proportion of academic journals that currently implement such verification is, to this author's knowledge, negligible — a governance gap with no technical barrier to closure.

**Author disambiguation and ORCID.** The Open Researcher and Contributor Identifier (ORCID) system provides persistent identifiers for researchers, with a verifiable publication record attached to each identity (Haak et al., 2012). A paper submitted by an ORCID-verified author whose publication history is traceable and temporally coherent is harder to fabricate than one submitted by a novel name with no prior record. Mandatory ORCID verification at submission, combined with plausibility checks on the claimed author's career trajectory, would raise the cost of fabricating a credible author identity. The limitation is that legitimate researchers can also be impersonated or their ORCIDs compromised.

**Registered Reports and preregistration.** Perhaps the most epistemologically robust institutional defense is the *Registered Report* format (Chambers, 2013): researchers submit their hypotheses, design, and analysis plan for peer review *before* data collection, with acceptance contingent on methodology rather than results. A registered report cannot be HARKed — the hypothesis is timestamped and publicly committed prior to the data existing. Preregistration platforms (OSF, AsPredicted, ClinicalTrials.gov) provide similar guarantees for non-registered-report submissions. Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven, and Mellor (2018, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) articulate the empirical and institutional case for this mechanism — *the preregistration revolution* — distinguishing the two functions a preregistered analysis plan performs: it makes a paper's predictions falsifiable in advance of data analysis, and it separates confirmatory from exploratory analyses so that the latter's larger error rates are no longer concealed inside the former's epistemic warrant. The mechanism does not detect AI generation, but it structurally prevents the specific failure mode of post-hoc rationalization that makes AI-generated academic content epistemically hollow: a paper whose hypothesis was registered before its data was collected provides evidence that genuine inquiry preceded the result. The decade of empirical adoption documented by Nosek et al. — from clinical trials registration after the 2000s pharmaceutical reform wave through the spread of preregistration in psychology, economics, and political science — shows that the mechanism is institutionally viable at scale even before AI-generated submissions raised the stakes.

## 4. Citation-Network Analysis: Identifying Synthetic Clusters

The mutual-citation problem — AI generating papers that cite each other — calls for analysis at the network level rather than the paper level. Citation-cartel detection methods, developed initially for human academic misconduct, are directly applicable (Fister et al., 2016).

**Citation cartel signatures.** A citation cartel is a group of papers whose members disproportionately cite each other relative to the broader field, inflating citation counts and creating a false impression of consensus or impact. Metrics for cartel detection include: (1) *citation concentration ratio* — the fraction of a paper's citations that come from within a defined cluster; (2) *citation velocity* — the speed with which a newly published paper accumulates citations, which for synthetic clusters can be anomalously high relative to its methodological or theoretical novelty; (3) *temporal inconsistency* — papers in a synthetic cluster may exhibit citation relationships that violate temporal logic (citing papers published after their own date, or exhibiting simultaneous mutual citation in the same publication window); and (4) *authorship overlap* — synthetic clusters may show anomalous patterns of co-authorship or author recurrence that differ from organic field dynamics.

**Graph-theoretic anomaly detection.** The structure of genuine academic citation networks exhibits well-studied statistical regularities: power-law degree distributions, clustering coefficients consistent with social network formation, community structure that reflects genuine intellectual genealogy (Barabási & Albert, 1999; Redner, 1998). A synthetic citation cluster generated by AI would need to reproduce these statistical properties to evade detection. Current AI systems do not explicitly optimize for citation-network plausibility; their citation choices reflect the distribution of references in their training data rather than a deliberate construction of network-realistic citation patterns (Walters & Wilder, 2023). Graph-theoretic analysis — comparing the local structure of a suspected cluster against the base-rate structure of the hosting field — is therefore a potentially high-recall detection method.

**The Sybil attack analogy.** In distributed systems security, a *Sybil attack* refers to an adversary creating multiple fake identities to subvert reputation or consensus mechanisms (Douceur, 2002). The mutual-citation problem is a Sybil attack on the academic citation system: fake papers create fake identities with fake citation histories that collectively simulate a false consensus. Academic publishing systems were not designed with Sybil resistance in mind. The defenses that distributed systems have developed against Sybil attacks — proof-of-work, proof-of-stake, social vouching with identity anchors — have structural analogs in the academic context: ORCID verification as identity anchor, preregistration as proof-of-work (demonstrating that a genuine research process preceded the paper), peer review by verified community members as social vouching.

## 5. The Arms Race Dynamic

Every published detection method becomes a potential training signal for generative systems that seek to evade it. This is the fundamental epistemological challenge of the domain: unlike most security problems, where a fixed vulnerability can be patched, the adversarial dynamic in synthetic knowledge generation is open-ended.

**Goodhart's Law applied to authenticity markers.** Goodhart's Law — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure (Strathern, 1997) — applies with full force to authenticity signals. If journals deploy citation-verification systems, sophisticated synthetic paper generators will be fine-tuned to produce only real citations, possibly by querying live databases at generation time. If stylometric detectors become widespread, generators will be optimized to produce text that falls below detection thresholds. If ORCID verification becomes mandatory, fake identity services will emerge to supply plausible ORCID profiles. Each technical fix shifts the problem rather than solving it.

**The asymmetry of effort.** Detection is harder than generation. A sophisticated synthetic paper with real citations, a plausible author identity, and carefully varied prose style requires substantial effort to detect definitively (Sadasivan et al., 2023); producing such a paper, once the generation pipeline is established, costs marginal resources per paper. This effort asymmetry is structurally similar to the asymmetry in content moderation at social media scale: the platform must review every piece of content; the adversary only needs to produce content that looks legitimate. The analogy suggests that the appropriate response is not primarily technical detection at the paper level but structural mechanisms that make synthetic paper production less strategically useful — registries, preregistration, community-based expert validation, and institutional accountability that raises the cost of wholesale fabrication.

**The multi-model problem.** As AI capability distributes across many models, the statistical signature of any single generator becomes an unreliable detection target. A synthetic paper produced by mixing outputs from three different generators, with human-authored sentences interspersed, may fall below the detection threshold of any single-model stylometric classifier (Wang et al., 2024). The detection problem shifts from identifying the output of a specific model to identifying the *absence* of genuine human intellectual process — a fundamentally epistemological rather than statistical challenge.

## 6. Institutional and Governance Responses

**Open peer review and reviewer identification.** Anonymous peer review provides cover for low-quality or synthetic review reports. Open peer review — in which reviewers are identified — raises the reputational cost of producing reviews that would validate synthetic papers, because the reviewer's name is permanently associated with the published assessment. Journals such as *eLife*, *EMBO Journal*, and *PeerJ* have adopted open review models (Ross-Hellauer, 2017; eLife Sciences Publications, 2023). The spread of open review would raise the social cost of the synthetic-validation problem without requiring any technical infrastructure.

**Open data, open materials, and computational reproducibility.** A paper accompanied by open data, open analysis code, and a reproducible computational environment makes a claim that is verifiable in a way that a text-only paper cannot. AI systems cannot easily generate data that satisfies an independent reproducing scientist's attempt to re-run the analysis. The Turing Institute's *Turing Way* and the Center for Open Science's *Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Guidelines* provide frameworks for implementation (Nosek et al., 2015). Journals that require open data as a condition of publication create a verification layer that synthetic papers cannot easily satisfy without also generating synthetic data — which is a separate and more easily detectable problem.

**DOI-based citation verification at submission.** The most immediately deployable governance mechanism: journals implement automated pre-screening that verifies every citation in a submitted paper against CrossRef, PubMed, and Semantic Scholar. A paper with imagined literature fails this check before human review begins. The technical barrier is trivially low; the adoption barrier is institutional inertia.

**Provenance metadata standards.** The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA, 2022) and related standards (see Draft A, Part 5) provide a framework for cryptographically attested provenance: a claim that a document was authored by a specific person, using specific tools, at a specific time, with a specific chain of custody. Applied to academic publishing, C2PA-style provenance metadata would allow journals and databases to request and verify generation-tool attestation at submission, flagging documents with AI-generation signatures for enhanced human review.

## 7. Implications for Hybrid Intelligence

The epistemic-provenance problem has direct implications for hybrid intelligence system design, in three registers.

**RAG systems inherit their sources' integrity.** A retrieval-augmented generation system that retrieves from a contaminated citation graph amplifies the contamination: the synthetic consensus in the retrieved papers becomes the AI's evidence base, and the AI's outputs further circulate the synthetic claims. Greshake, Abdelnabi, Mishra, Endres, Holz, and Fritz (2023) — the canonical peer-reviewed account of *indirect prompt injection* — demonstrated that any external content an LLM retrieves at inference time can hijack subsequent generation, with the retrieval channel itself acting as an unauthenticated instruction surface; once the substrate is poisoned, the integrity of every downstream answer depends on content the deployer did not write and cannot verify. The integrity of AI-grounded reasoning is therefore only as strong as the integrity of the source infrastructure. This is the same argument made in `u-single-source-truth.md` (Section 3, *Open Platforms as Distributed Truth Machines*, and Section 5, *Permanence and Provenance*) at the infrastructure level; here it applies specifically to the academic literature layer, where the imagined-literature failure mode is, as `i-llm-epistemic-limits.md` (Part 2) argues, structurally indistinguishable from genuine inquiry without external provenance machinery.

**Hybrid systems can participate in detection.** The same AI capabilities that enable synthetic paper generation can, in principle, be deployed for detection: citation-graph anomaly detection at scale, statistical stylometry across large corpora, temporal-coherence checking across citation histories. Hybrid intelligence systems — combining AI-scale processing with human epistemological judgment — are better positioned to address this problem than either human reviewers alone (insufficient scale) or fully automated detection (insufficient epistemological depth).

**The epistemological floor.** Ultimately, the question "what distinguishes human from AI-generated knowledge?" is a question about the nature of knowledge itself. The tacit-knowledge argument (Polanyi), the thought-collective argument (Fleck), and the embodied-cognition tradition all point toward a view of knowledge as something that emerges from engagement with the world — not just engagement with text about the world. The value of human knowledge is not reducible to its propositional content; it includes the trace of genuine inquiry, genuine failure, and genuine revision that no purely generative process can fully simulate. For hybrid intelligence design, this suggests that the epistemically most valuable human contribution is not the production of text but the validation and contestation of claims against the world — fieldwork, experiment, observation, and the productive encounter with recalcitrant reality.

## Open Questions

- **OQ1 — Detection generalization:** As AI generation becomes more sophisticated and draws on real citations and verified author identities, what detection signals remain robust? Is there an irreducible behavioral trace of genuine human inquiry that cannot be synthesized?
- **OQ2 — Citation-cartel governance:** What combination of institutional mechanisms — preregistration, open data, open review, DOI verification — is sufficient to prevent synthetic citation clusters from persisting in major databases?
- **OQ3 — WEIRD detection bias:** If stylometric AI detectors exhibit false-positive bias against non-native English writers (Liang et al., 2023), how should epistemic-provenance systems be calibrated to avoid encoding new forms of the WEIRD bias identified in Draft W?
- **OQ4 — The preregistration floor:** Is preregistration + open data sufficient to make HARKing detectable even when the prose is AI-generated? Can an AI system HARKing with real data (generating post-hoc rationale for a genuine empirical result) be distinguished from genuine inquiry?
- **OQ5 — Institutional incentives:** Current publication incentive structures (impact factor, citation metrics) are directly exploitable by synthetic citation networks. What metric redesign would remove the incentive to inflate citations artificially?
