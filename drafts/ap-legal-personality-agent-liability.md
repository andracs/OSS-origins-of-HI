## Abstract

When an autonomous AI agent causes harm — misdiagnoses a patient, executes a harmful trade, gives negligent legal advice, or drives a vehicle into a pedestrian — existing legal systems face a structural problem: liability frameworks were designed for human actors and legal persons, and autonomous agents are neither. This chapter examines three interrelated questions that arise when agents act with genuine autonomy in consequential domains: (1) Can an AI agent bear legal personality — rights, obligations, and standing — in the way that corporations do? (2) When an agent causes harm, who in the principal-agent chain is liable — the model developer, the deployer, the operator, the user, or no one? (3) What governance architecture — registration, insurance, audit trails, liability shields, and accountability-by-design — is needed to close the responsibility gap that autonomous agency opens? Drawing on Matthias's responsibility gap analysis, Elish's moral crumple zone concept, the EU AI Act's tiered liability framework, the (now-withdrawn) EU AI Liability Directive proposal, the revised Product Liability Directive 2024/2853, autonomous vehicle liability precedents, and the AlphaFold Nobel Prize attribution case as a real-world edge case, the chapter argues that the responsibility gap is not a temporary technical problem solvable by better explainability — it is a structural feature of systems designed to act without continuous human direction, requiring purpose-built legal infrastructure rather than extensions of existing tort doctrine.

## §1 The Liability Gap in Autonomous Systems

The conventional liability chain in product and professional services law is straightforward: a manufacturer produces a product; a professional provides a service; if either causes harm, negligence or strict liability doctrines assign responsibility and enable remedy. The chain assumes a human or corporate decision-maker whose choices can be evaluated, whose foresight can be assessed, and who can be held to account.

Autonomous agents break this chain at a specific point: the point of **contingent action**. A product liability claim against a car manufacturer depends on a design decision made by identifiable engineers. A malpractice claim against a doctor depends on a treatment decision made by an identifiable professional. An autonomous agent's harmful action may depend on a combination of:

- Training data choices made by a foundation model developer (often disclosed minimally or not at all)
- Fine-tuning decisions made by a downstream model developer
- Deployment configuration choices made by the operator
- In-context prompt choices made by the user
- Emergent behavior arising from none of the above individually — a property of the interaction between components that no single actor designed or foresaw

Matthias (2004) introduced the **responsibility gap** in this form: as machines learn from data and operate in environments their designers cannot fully foresee, there is progressively less space for meaningful human moral responsibility between the act of deploying the system and the harm it causes. Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021) developed the canonical contemporary taxonomy, distinguishing four interrelated gaps — culpability, moral accountability, public accountability, and active responsibility — caused by a mix of technical, organisational, legal, and societal factors. Floridi et al. (2018) frame the same concern through the AI4People principle of *explicability* (intelligibility plus accountability) as the precondition for any of the other ethical principles to be enforceable. The gap is not merely practical (it is hard to prove who was responsible) but structural: the system was *designed* to act autonomously, and autonomous action is by definition action not continuously directed by a human principal.

Danaher (2016) introduced the complementary concept of the **retribution gap**: when an autonomous system causes harm, society's intuitive demand for a target of blame and punishment is frustrated by the absence of a culpable human, while the temptation to scapegoat a nearby human supervisor (the *moral crumple zone* later named by Elish, 2019) remains strong even when that human had no meaningful control over the harmful action. The Uber autonomous vehicle fatality (2018), where the safety driver was charged with negligent homicide despite limited capacity to override the vehicle's decision-making in the available reaction window, illustrates the phenomenon in a real legal proceeding.

## §2 Legal Personality: Corporation as Precedent and its Limits

The standard response to legal vacuum around new entities is to ask whether existing legal person categories can be extended. The corporation is the relevant precedent: corporations are not natural persons, but legal systems grant them standing, the capacity to own property, to contract, and to be sued. Could AI agents — or certain classes of AI agents — be granted analogous legal personality?

The argument for AI legal personality rests on functional equivalence: an autonomous agent that enters contracts, manages resources, produces outputs that third parties rely on, and causes foreseeable harm behaves, in legally relevant respects, like a corporate actor. The most-cited European policy expression of this argument is the European Parliament's resolution of 16 February 2017 with recommendations to the Commission on Civil Law Rules on Robotics, paragraph 59(f) of which invited the Commission to explore "creating a specific legal status for robots in the long run, so that at least the most sophisticated autonomous robots could be established as having the status of electronic persons responsible for making good any damage they may cause" (European Parliament, 2017, P8_TA(2017)0051, ¶59(f)). The proposal drew immediate scholarly opposition: a coalition of European experts in robotics, law, medicine, and ethics signed an open letter to the Commission arguing that electronic personhood would be inappropriate from a legal, technical, and ethical standpoint (Nevejans et al., 2018). Academic analysis includes Solum (1992) — among the first legal scholars to take AI personhood seriously — and Bryson, Diamantis, and Grant (2017), who argued against AI legal personality on the grounds that synthetic persons would not solve the accountability problem but transfer responsibility from natural persons to a fictional entity unable to bear it, weakening rather than strengthening the rule of law.

The argument against is both philosophical and practical. Philosophical: legal personality is an instrumental fiction created to serve human interests; extending it to systems that have no interests of their own creates a fiction that serves no one except those who wish to escape liability through it. Practical: if an AI agent has legal personality and causes harm, it cannot be imprisoned, cannot experience financial penalty in any meaningful sense, and has no assets independent of its creators — making legal personality a liability shield for the humans behind the agent rather than a genuine accountability mechanism.

The corporate analogy cuts both ways: corporations *do* limit liability for shareholders, and this is generally understood as a feature rather than a bug. But corporate liability requires piercing the veil to reach the humans who made decisions; an AI agent granted legal personality with no equivalent mechanism would be an accountability black hole.

The mainstream legal consensus, reflected in the EU AI Act and the revised Product Liability Directive (with the AI Liability Directive proposal having been formally withdrawn in 2025), rejects AI legal personality in favor of liability chains through human and corporate principals — an approach that preserves accountability while acknowledging the complexity of distributed causation.

## §3 Current Liability Frameworks and Their Application to Agents

**Product liability.** Directive (EU) 2024/2853 on liability for defective products (the revised Product Liability Directive, published in the Official Journal on 18 November 2024, with a transposition deadline of 9 December 2026) holds manufacturers liable for defective products causing personal injury or property damage. Article 4(1) of the revised Directive expressly defines "product" to include software — whether stand-alone, embedded, or supplied as a service — bringing AI systems within its scope (European Parliament and Council, 2024a). Applied to AI agents, the central challenge is the definition of "defect": a model that produces a harmful output under an unforeseeable combination of inputs may not be defective in any conventional sense — it behaved within its design parameters. Article 10 of the revised Directive responds to this by establishing rebuttable presumptions that ease the claimant's burden of proof in four circumstances: (a) the defendant fails to comply with disclosure obligations; (b) the claimant demonstrates non-compliance with mandatory product-safety requirements of EU or national law (which, for high-risk AI systems, includes the obligations under the EU AI Act); (c) the damage was caused by an obvious malfunction during reasonably foreseeable use; or (d) the claimant has shown that defectiveness or the causal link is "likely" but proof is "excessively difficult" because of the product's technical or scientific complexity — a clause expressly intended to cover AI's "black-box" character (European Parliament and Council, 2024a, Art. 10(2)–(4)). Together, Articles 4 and 10 create a regulatory compliance–liability nexus that ties failure to meet AI Act safety duties directly to product-liability exposure.

**Negligence.** Professional negligence requires a duty of care, breach of that duty, causation, and damage. For AI-assisted professional services (medical diagnosis, legal advice, financial planning), the question is whether the professional's duty of care extends to supervising the agent's output. The consensus emerging from malpractice jurisprudence in the US and EU is that it does: a physician who relies on an AI diagnostic recommendation without adequate clinical judgment exercises the physician's duty, not the AI's. This is the "human in the loop" liability model — accountability anchors at the human who has professional standing, regardless of whether the human was meaningfully capable of overriding the AI's recommendation.

**Strict liability.** For ultrahazardous activities — and autonomous systems operating in physical environments (vehicles, industrial robots, medical devices) are increasingly classified as such — strict liability holds the operator responsible without requiring proof of negligence. The autonomous vehicle context has advanced this framework most rapidly: most US states and EU member states have enacted or are enacting strict liability for autonomous vehicle operators/owners, with subrogation rights against manufacturers for design defects. This model — strict liability for deployment, products liability against the supply chain — is likely to extend to high-risk AI deployments under the EU AI Act's high-risk category framework.

**The EU AI Liability Directive (proposed 2022, withdrawn 2025).** The Directive (COM(2022) 496 final), tabled by the Commission on 28 September 2022, would have introduced a targeted civil-liability instrument for AI: a disclosure mechanism enabling victims of AI harm to obtain information about high-risk systems to support negligence claims, and a rebuttable presumption of causal link where the defendant could not demonstrate that the system functioned correctly (European Commission, 2022). After protracted Council and Parliament negotiations failed to converge, the Commission listed the proposal for withdrawal in Annex IV of its 2025 Work Programme on 11 February 2025, citing "no foreseeable agreement" and a need to assess whether a different instrument should be tabled. The Commission formally adopted the withdrawal at its 2533rd meeting on 16 July 2025, and the withdrawal notice was published in the Official Journal as C/2025/5423 on 6 October 2025 (European Commission, 2025). The legislative gap left by the withdrawal is now partially covered by the revised Product Liability Directive (above), the EU AI Act's high-risk-system obligations, and existing national tort regimes — but the AI-specific procedural innovations (sector-tailored disclosure, presumption of causation) are no longer on the EU agenda.

## §4 The Principal-Agent Chain in Multi-Agent Systems

The liability analysis becomes substantially more complex in multi-agent architectures (cf. Draft AK) where multiple agents interact, delegate to each other, and take actions whose consequences are not traceable to any single agent's decision. The relevant legal concept is **respondeat superior** — the employer is vicariously liable for employee actions within the scope of employment. Its applicability to AI agents depends on whether the deploying organization can be understood as the "employer" of the agent and whether the harmful action was within the "scope" of the agent's authorized activities.

Draft AB (attribution and accountability when agents do the work) establishes the normative dimension of this problem. The legal dimension adds practical urgency: a legal system that cannot determine who to sue when an agent causes harm fails a basic test of rule of law.

Proposed frameworks for the multi-agent liability chain:

**Cascading strict liability.** Each entity in the deployment chain (developer → operator → user) is strictly liable for harms caused by agents they deployed or configured, with contribution rights up the chain. This model ensures victims can recover while preserving incentives for quality at each link.

**Lead deployer liability.** The entity that deploys the agent to end users bears primary liability, regardless of upstream component responsibility. This simplifies the victim's task at the cost of creating adverse selection in the deployer market — only entities with deep pockets can bear the liability risk of deploying powerful agents.

**Insurance pools.** Mandatory insurance for AI agent deployments above a risk threshold, with pooled liability funds analogous to nuclear liability conventions. The EU AI Act's high-risk classification system is compatible with mandatory insurance requirements, though these are not yet enacted.

## §5 Accountability-by-Design

The technical and governance design of agentic systems can either concentrate or distribute accountability. The agentic harness architecture described in Draft AR — with defined principal hierarchies, audit logs, permission scoping, and explicit tool authorization — constitutes an accountability-by-design approach that creates the evidentiary infrastructure for post-hoc liability analysis. Wieringa's (2020) systematic review of the algorithmic-accountability literature applies a relational framing in which accountability requires specifying who is held to account (the *actor*), before which institutional *forum*, on what *content*, under what *relationship*, and with what *consequences* — and shows that most algorithmic systems leave at least one of these dimensions underspecified, especially the forum and the consequences. Properly engineered agentic systems must therefore not merely *log* what happened (the content dimension) but designate which institutional forum will examine those logs and what consequences will follow — a design choice that is typically deferred and rarely made explicit in commercial deployments.

Key design properties for legally accountable agentic systems:

**Immutable audit trails.** Every agent action — tool call, API invocation, message sent, file modified — should be logged with timestamp, authorization chain, and triggering context. Audit trails create the causal evidence base that negligence and product liability claims require.

**Explicit authorization scope.** Agents operating within defined permission boundaries can be evaluated against those boundaries; actions outside the authorized scope create clear manufacturer/operator liability. Systems with no defined scope create the "moral crumple zone" problem.

**Human-in-the-loop checkpoints.** For high-stakes decisions — financial transactions above a threshold, medical recommendations, legal advice, physical actuation — mandatory human confirmation creates a documented point of human judgment that anchors liability. The checkpoints must be genuine (the human must have time and information to meaningfully evaluate) rather than nominal (rubber-stamping agent output under time pressure).

**Principal disclosure.** Users of AI agents should know: who deployed the agent, what model it runs on, what actions it is authorized to take, and who bears liability for its outputs. Disclosure requirements analogous to those for medical devices or financial advisors are a natural regulatory extension for high-risk agent deployments.

## §6 The AlphaFold Case and the Attribution–Accountability Distinction

Draft AB discusses the AlphaFold Nobel Prize attribution case as an instance of the credit question: do Demis Hassabis and John Jumper deserve the Nobel Prize for a model that did most of the work? The accountability question is different and harder: if AlphaFold's protein structure predictions are wrong — if a drug developed using its structures fails in trial due to a structural error — who is accountable?

The answer under current law is: the researchers who used the predictions bear professional responsibility; AlphaFold itself cannot be sued; DeepMind/Google's product liability exposure depends on how the tool was marketed (as a research aid or a medical-grade instrument). This allocation — human researchers bear accountability for an AI tool's errors that they could not reasonably have detected — is structurally problematic for science, medicine, and any domain where AI outputs are trusted at scales that preclude systematic human verification.

The attribution–accountability distinction generalizes: AI systems that receive credit for positive outcomes (patent attribution, authorship credit, prize recognition) should bear commensurate accountability for negative outcomes. The asymmetry — agents credited for breakthroughs but not held accountable for failures — represents a subsidy to AI developers at the expense of the downstream users who bear the deployment risk.

## §7 Open Questions

1. **Is the EU's principal-chain liability model sufficient for frontier agentic AI?** As agents become capable of genuinely novel actions — not just executing configured workflows but reasoning about new situations — the "within scope of deployment" test for liability may fail. An agent that discovers an exploitable vulnerability and takes an unauthorized action is not "within scope" in any useful sense, yet the harm is real and a victim deserves remedy.

2. **Can insurance markets price agentic liability without historical actuarial data?** Automotive insurance took decades to develop appropriate risk models. Novel agentic deployments have no actuarial history; insurance underwriting will either be prohibitively expensive (limiting deployment) or based on models that underestimate tail risks.

3. **What is the accountability architecture for open-source foundation models?** If a harmful agent is built on an open-source model (Llama, Mistral, Falcon), no single entity controls deployment. Product liability requires an identifiable manufacturer; the open-source ecosystem deliberately distributes that role. Open-source AI liability shields analogous to Section 230 (which shields platforms from liability for third-party content) have been proposed but not enacted.

4. **Does granting AI agents legal personality help or hurt accountability?** The corporate analogy suggests that limited liability plus piercing-the-veil mechanisms could work for AI agents — but the veil-piercing doctrine in corporate law is notoriously difficult to invoke. Would AI legal personality effectively insulate the humans behind agents from accountability?

5. **How does accountability architecture interact with competitive incentives?** Operators who implement comprehensive audit trails, genuine human-in-the-loop checkpoints, and mandatory disclosure reduce their competitive speed relative to operators who do not. Without mandatory standards, accountability-by-design creates adverse selection: careful operators bear costs that reckless operators avoid.

6. **Whose values does the agent's principal owe accountability to?** Gabriel (2020) distinguishes the technical alignment problem (getting an agent to do what its principal asks) from the normative alignment problem (deciding *whose* values the principal is entitled to encode and impose on third parties). The liability framing inherits this ambiguity: a deployer can be held to account for behaviour that conforms to their instructions yet imposes externalities on non-principals (users, bystanders, downstream populations). The principal-chain liability model presumed throughout this chapter resolves only the first question — it identifies the human who *authorised* the agent's action — without resolving the second, which is the substantive grounds on which any forum should judge that action's permissibility.
