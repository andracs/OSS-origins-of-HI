# Draft I — LLM Overconfidence and Epistemic Limits: What Models Don't Know They Don't Know

*Draft v0.30 — 2026-05-13. Iterative concept document for Springer Handbook chapter.*  
*Target: epistemic limits and calibration failure section for "Open source code as fuel for hybrid intelligence"*

## Overview and Chapter Position

The chapter's three-stage model (Accumulation → Mining & refinement → Augmentation & feedback) implicitly assumes that the AI component of a hybrid system delivers reliable outputs that the human partner can build on. This draft examines a structural limit on that assumption: current large language models are systematically poorly calibrated — their expressed confidence correlates imperfectly with their accuracy, and more troublingly, they have no reliable internal access to the boundary between what they know and what they do not know. A model that confabulates a false citation with the same fluency as a true one, and experiences no internal signal distinguishing the two, is a hybrid intelligence partner with a specific failure mode: it can pull the human partner toward confident error.

This is not a bug to be patched but a structural property of models trained to generate probable continuations of text. The implication for hybrid intelligence design is not "use AI less" but "design HI systems that treat AI epistemic limits as a known parameter" — which requires understanding those limits in detail.

Three analytical threads structure this draft:
- **Calibration failure**: the statistical mismatch between expressed confidence and accuracy
- **Hallucination as confabulation**: fluent generation of false content with no internal alarm
- **Unknown unknowns**: the Rumsfeld taxonomy applied to AI — models cannot reliably represent the second quadrant (what they don't know they don't know)

## Part 1: Calibration — The Gap Between Confidence and Accuracy

**Anchor:** Guo et al. (2017) [C]; Kadavath et al. (2022) [P]

**Calibration** in machine learning refers to the alignment between a model's expressed probability and its actual accuracy: a well-calibrated model that says "I am 80% confident" should be correct approximately 80% of the time across many such predictions. Guo et al. (2017) [C] demonstrated that modern deep neural networks are systematically *overconfident* — their expressed probabilities are higher than their empirical accuracies, a phenomenon they trace to overfitting during training. The standard remediation, temperature scaling, partially corrects this at the output layer but does not address the underlying representation.

**LLM self-knowledge**: Kadavath et al. (2022, "Language models (mostly) know what they know," Anthropic, *Anthropic Technical Report*) [P] examined whether large language models can accurately predict whether they will answer a question correctly — a form of meta-cognitive self-assessment. The finding is nuanced: models show *some* capacity for self-calibration on factual questions when explicitly prompted to produce probability estimates, but this capacity degrades for out-of-distribution questions, for questions near the model's knowledge boundary, and for questions where training data contains plausible but incorrect information. The paper states explicitly: "Models perform well at predicting P(IK) on diverse multiple choice and true/false questions when they are provided in the right format. They show some generalization across tasks, though they struggle with calibration of P(IK) on new tasks." The hedge "mostly" in the title is load-bearing: partial self-knowledge is not sufficient for the kind of reliable uncertainty communication that safe hybrid intelligence design requires.

**The fluency problem**: unlike human experts who typically experience uncertainty as hesitation, vagueness, or explicit caveats, LLMs produce fluent, grammatical, confidently-structured text regardless of underlying accuracy. The surface signal that humans use to infer competence (articulateness, specificity, absence of hedging) is decoupled from the underlying epistemic state. A model cannot stammer. This decoupling is not incidental to language model design — it is fundamental: models are trained on human-generated text that is *by definition* fluent and articulate, making that surface structure the strongest available signal in the training distribution regardless of whether the underlying claim is accurate or confabulated. The loss function that guides training (next-token prediction or conditional likelihood) rewards matching the form of authoritative discourse without constraining truth value. Consequently, a model's fluency increases along with its falsity on the same trajectory (Lin et al., 2022a; Min et al., 2023). The failure mode is not "AI makes a grammatical error"; it is "AI produces persuasively wrong outputs because wrong outputs that maintain discourse coherence receive the same training reward as correct ones." This is distinct from hallucination in retrieval-augmented settings (where an external document supplies grounding); it is parametric hallucination — the model's own parameters encode a distribution over plausible continuations that is fundamentally decoupled from factual accuracy.

**Frontier calibration benchmarks**: Nel (2025, "Do Large Language Models Know What They Don't Know? KalshiBench: A New Benchmark for Evaluating Epistemic Calibration via Prediction Markets," arXiv:2512.16030) [P; iCit: pending] evaluates multiple frontier LLMs on epistemic calibration using temporally-filtered prediction market questions — a methodology designed to eliminate training-data contamination. Frontier models show systematic overconfidence across all model families; critically, reasoning-enhanced variants exhibit *degraded* calibration relative to base models, and calibration accuracy and predictive accuracy are largely decoupled. The cleanest finding for hybrid intelligence design: no current frontier model reliably knows what it does not know, and scale and instruction-tuning do not reliably fix this. A convergent finding across a larger model set comes from Ulmer et al. (2025), who evaluate nine frontier LLMs spanning 8B to >1T parameters (dense and MoE, SFT and RLHF fine-tuned) and find widespread overconfidence across all model families and sizes, with larger models showing *degraded* calibration on simpler queries in some conditions — corroborating the KalshiBench result that scale does not reliably reduce epistemic overconfidence (Ulmer et al., 2025). [PDF saved: Kilder/KalshiBench2025_EpistemicCalibrationPredictionMarkets_arXiv2512.16030.pdf]

**Code-generation calibration**: Spiess et al. (2025, "Calibration and Correctness of Language Models for Code," *ICSE 2025*) [C] provide the most direct empirical treatment: they measure how well LLM confidence scores align with actual correctness across function synthesis and line-level completion tasks, finding systematic miscalibration — models express high confidence on incorrect outputs, and confidence rescaling only partially corrects the gap. The hybrid intelligence implication is concrete: when an LLM generates code with high expressed confidence, that signal does not reliably distinguish correct from plausible-but-wrong solutions. The human partner in a hybrid system cannot use model confidence as a triage signal; independent verification remains necessary regardless of confidence score. [PDF saved: Kilder/Spiess2024_CalibrationCorrectnessCodeLLMs_arXiv2402.02047.pdf]

## Part 2: Hallucination — Confabulation Without Internal Alarm

**Anchor:** Ji et al. (2023); Rawte et al. (2023)

**Hallucination** in language models refers to the generation of factually incorrect or fabricated content presented with the same fluency and apparent confidence as accurate content. Ji et al. (2023, "Survey of hallucination in natural language generation," *ACM Computing Surveys*, 55(12), 1–38) provide a taxonomy: intrinsic hallucination (contradicting the source material), extrinsic hallucination (adding information not present or verifiable in the source), and factual hallucination (generating false statements about the world). Huang et al. (2023, "A Survey on Hallucination in Large Language Models," arXiv:2311.05232) offer a complementary open-access taxonomy with equivalent coverage — they are co-anchors for this framework, and Huang et al. should be preferred where the Ji et al. ACM CSUR PDF remains unobtainable. In code generation, the analog is functional hallucination: generating syntactically correct code that does not implement the specified behavior, or invoking non-existent library functions (so-called "ghost APIs").

**Why hallucination is structural, not incidental**: language models are trained to generate probable continuations. When a model encounters a prompt about an unfamiliar topic, the most probable continuation is text that *looks like* authoritative discussion of that topic — because that is the distribution the model was trained on. The model has no separate "I don't know" signal; it has only the generative process. Rawte et al. (2023, "A survey of hallucination in large foundation models," arXiv:2309.05922) identify the core mechanism: *sycophantic generation* — models trained on human feedback learn to produce outputs that appear satisfying to evaluators, which is not the same as producing accurate outputs. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) may inadvertently reinforce fluent confabulation if evaluators cannot distinguish fluent falsehood from fluent truth (Sharma et al., 2023).

**Detection approaches**: SelfCheckGPT (Manakul et al., 2023, arXiv:2303.08896) exploits the observation that a model generating uncertain content will produce inconsistent completions across multiple samples — high variance signals low confidence even when no single completion signals it. This offers a detection mechanism that operates at the output level without requiring access to internal representations.

**Memory architecture as structural constraint**: Jia et al. (2026, "The AI Hippocampus: How Far are We From Human Memory?", arXiv:2601.09113) survey memory mechanisms across LLMs and multimodal systems, distinguishing *implicit memory* (parametric knowledge — the substrate of hallucination), *explicit memory* (RAG and external storage — the standard mitigation), and *agentic memory* (persistent structures in autonomous agents). Their taxonomy clarifies why hallucination is not uniformly distributed: it clusters in the implicit/parametric tier where there is no retrieval check and no mechanism for the model to distinguish well-attested from poorly-attested knowledge. Explicit memory (RAG) relocates part of the knowledge function to a retrievable store where quality can be governed; agentic memory adds continuity but inherits calibration failure if the agent relies on parametric inference at intermediate steps. For hybrid intelligence design, the Jia et al. taxonomy reframes the hallucination mitigation question as an architectural one: which knowledge should be parametric, which should be retrieval-grounded, and which requires persistent agent state? The answer differs by task domain and acceptable error rate.

**Code hallucination taxonomy**: Gao et al. (2025, "A Systematic Literature Review of Code Hallucinations in LLMs," arXiv:2511.00776) survey 60 papers on hallucination specifically in code-oriented LLMs, identifying primary causes (data noise, exposure bias, insufficient semantic grounding) and distinguishing code-specific aggravating factors: syntax sensitivity, strict type systems, and external library dependence. The review highlights that standard mitigation strategies (knowledge-enhanced generation, constrained decoding, post-editing) transfer imperfectly to code intelligence tasks, and that existing benchmarks prioritize compilation/execution correctness over hallucination-specific detection — a gap that understates the problem for hybrid human-AI development workflows.

**Package hallucination in code generation**: Spracklen et al. (2024, "We Have a Package for You! A Comprehensive Analysis of Package Hallucinations by Code Generating LLMs," arXiv:2406.10279) provide the most comprehensive empirical account of a specific, consequential form of code hallucination: the generation of references to non-existent software packages. Across 16 LLMs and multiple package ecosystems, the study finds hallucination rates of 21.7% for open-source models and at least 5.2% for commercial models; critically, the hallucinated package names are syntactically plausible and could be registered by adversaries to serve malicious code to developers who trust the AI-generated dependency list. This connects ghost-API hallucination to supply-chain risk (Draft E, Part 3): confident incorrectness in code generation is not merely a productivity hazard — it is a potential security attack surface.

**Static analysis as a partial detection floor for library hallucinations**: Miranda-Pena et al. (2026, "An Empirical Analysis of Static Analysis Methods for Detection and Mitigation of Code Library Hallucinations," arXiv:2604.07755) directly address the Gao et al. detection gap by evaluating static analysis tools — cheap, deterministic, logit-free — against library-specific hallucinations across multiple LLMs and NL-to-code benchmarks. The central empirical finding: LLMs generate code using non-existent library features in 8.1–40% of responses depending on model; static analysis can detect 16–70% of all errors and 14–85% of library hallucinations, but manual analysis reveals a structural ceiling of 48.5–77% — a sizeable fraction of library hallucinations cannot be caught even by a perfect static analyser because they involve semantically plausible but behaviourally wrong API use that passes all signature checks. For hybrid intelligence design, this finding is important in two directions: (1) static analysis is cheap and should be used as a baseline detection layer in any human-AI coding workflow; (2) the structural ceiling means that the remaining 23–52% of library hallucinations will always require human verification or dynamic testing — there is no purely automated path to closing the gap. The confident-incorrectness failure mode (Part 1) cannot be resolved by static tooling alone. [PDF saved: Kilder/StaticAnalysisCodeLibraryHallucinations_2026_arXiv2604.07755.pdf]

**Academic publishing as hallucination surface: imagined literature and HARKing.** The package hallucination phenomenon has a structural analog in academic text generation with higher-stakes epistemic consequences. Novy-Marx and Velikov (2025, NBER Working Paper 33363) ran a controlled experiment in which a commercial LLM generated 288 complete academic finance papers in approximately 12 hours at a cost of $200–300, each citing "existing (and, on occasion, imagined) literature." The phrase "imagined literature" names the failure precisely: citation strings that are syntactically and academically plausible — plausible authors, plausible journal names, plausible titles — but do not correspond to real publications. This is the ghost-API problem transposed into academic prose. The model has no internal signal distinguishing real from invented sources; both are equally high-probability completions of the form "Author (year) demonstrated that…". The papers were internally signed "I. M. Harking" by the human researchers — a deliberate marker of the epistemic failure the machine cannot perceive: **HARKing** (Hypothesizing After Results are Known), the construction of post-hoc theoretical rationales for empirically discovered results, is structurally indistinguishable from genuine inquiry when produced by a generative model trained on academic prose. The hallucination-without-internal-alarm failure mode of Part 2 is here instantiated in a socially consequential domain: fabricated citations, if submitted and accepted, corrupt the academic citation graph — the epistemic infrastructure on which subsequent research and AI training corpora build (cf. `u-single-source-truth.md`, Section 1). The feedback loop from academic hallucination to model training is the same recursive poisoning mechanism identified for code (Part 4 below; Draft A, Part 5).

**Hallucination propagation in agentic multi-step trajectories**: the single-turn hallucination failure mode studied in Parts 1–2 compounds in agentic settings where an LLM operates across sequential steps with tool use, retrieval, and planning. Liu et al. (2026, "AgentHallu: Benchmarking Automated Hallucination Attribution of LLM-based Agents," arXiv:2601.06818) introduce the task of *hallucination attribution* — identifying which step in a multi-step agent trajectory caused the initial divergence and explaining why. Evaluating 13 leading models across 693 trajectories spanning 7 agent frameworks and 5 domains, they find that even top-tier models (GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro) achieve only 41.1% step-localization accuracy; tool-use hallucinations are the hardest to attribute, at just 11.6%. The hybrid intelligence implication is direct and structurally distinct from the single-turn case: in agentic workflows, a hallucination at an intermediate step — a misretrieved fact, an incorrect tool call, a faulty planning decision — propagates forward through subsequent steps, compounding error without any internal signal. The human partner interacting with the final output receives a plausible-looking result whose error was introduced steps earlier and is invisible in the output itself. This is the calibration-failure problem (Part 1) extended into pipeline architectures: not only can the model not signal uncertainty in a single response, it cannot locate where in a multi-step chain the uncertainty was introduced. [PDF saved: Kilder/AgentHallu2026_HallucinationAttributionAgents_arXiv2601.06818.pdf]

**Calibration dichotomy in tool-use agents**: the agentic miscalibration problem is not uniform across tool types. Xuan et al. (2026, "The Confidence Dichotomy: Analyzing and Mitigating Miscalibration in Tool-Use Agents," arXiv:2601.07264) investigate verbalized calibration specifically in tool-integrated agents and find a fundamental split: *evidence tools* (web search, retrieval) that return noisy external content systematically induce severe overconfidence — the agent treats retrieved noise as confirmation; *verification tools* (code interpreters, calculators) that provide deterministic execution feedback produce better confidence–accuracy alignment. For hybrid intelligence design, this finding is structurally significant: the calibration failure mode in agentic systems is not a scalar property of the agent but depends on the tool type in the active loop. A hybrid system that uses retrieval as a grounding mechanism for an LLM agent may inadvertently worsen calibration at exactly the step intended to improve it. The proposed mitigation — joint RL training on accuracy and calibration rewards — is promising but not deployable without access to the agent's training regime, limiting applicability to off-the-shelf integrations. [PDF saved: Kilder/Xuan2026_ConfidenceDichotomyToolUseAgents_arXiv2601.07264.pdf]

The preceding findings converge on a consistent pattern: AI-generated code fails in ways that are non-trivially hard to detect, while AI confidence signals remain high. The resulting condition — developer trust exceeding actual reliability — has been directly documented in a controlled experiment: participants using AI coding assistants wrote significantly more security vulnerabilities than unassisted participants, and *rated their code as more secure* (Perry et al., 2023). This is the confident-incorrectness failure mode instantiated at the human-AI interface: fluent, high-confidence output suppresses the developer's verification instinct at precisely the moments when verification is most needed. Becker et al.'s (2025) finding that experienced developers slow down with AI tools may reflect the cost of partially recovering that verification behavior — an expensive workaround for a calibration deficit the tool cannot self-report.

## Part 3: Unknown Unknowns — The Rumsfeld Taxonomy Applied

**Anchor:** Dunning (2011); Kuhn et al. (2023)

**The Rumsfeld taxonomy** distinguishes: (1) known knowns — what we know we know; (2) known unknowns — what we know we don't know; (3) unknown unknowns — what we don't know we don't know. The governance challenge for hybrid intelligence is category (3): the failure mode where neither the human nor the AI component recognizes the knowledge gap.

**The Dunning-Kruger mechanism in LLMs**: Dunning (2011, "The Dunning-Kruger effect: On being ignorant of one's own ignorance," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 44, 247–296) explains the human version: incompetence in a domain removes the meta-cognitive tools needed to recognize that incompetence — you need skill to accurately assess skill. LLMs face an analog: the model's "competence" in a domain is the probability distribution over plausible continuations of text about that domain. A domain with large amounts of plausible-but-incorrect text in training data produces a model that generates fluent text *and* generates fluent errors — with no internal distinguishing signal. Ghosh & Panday (2026, "The Dunning-Kruger Effect in Large Language Models: An Empirical Study of Confidence Calibration," arXiv:2603.09985) test this directly: evaluating four frontier models across 24,000 trials, they find that poorly performing models exhibit disproportionate overconfidence — Kimi K2 achieves only 23.3% accuracy yet an Expected Calibration Error of 0.726, while Claude Haiku 4.5 shows best-in-class calibration. The pattern is not uniform across model families, but the structural finding holds: low-competence model states do not produce proportionally low expressed confidence, directly instantiating the Dunning-Kruger mechanism at scale. [PDF saved: Kilder/Ghosh2026_DunningKrugerLLMs_arXiv2603.09985.pdf]

**Semantic uncertainty**: Kuhn et al. (2023, "Semantic uncertainty: Linguistic invariances for uncertainty estimation in natural language generation," *ICLR 2023*, arXiv:2302.09664) propose measuring uncertainty not at the token level but at the semantic level: cluster model completions by meaning, and use the entropy of the cluster distribution as an uncertainty signal. A model generating semantically consistent completions across samples is probably drawing on reliable knowledge; a model generating semantically dispersed completions is probably near a knowledge boundary. This approach can identify *when* a model is uncertain, but it cannot tell the model what it doesn't know — it can only signal that the model's outputs are inconsistent.

**Aleatoric vs. epistemic uncertainty in LLMs**: Wang et al. (2025, "Measuring Aleatoric and Epistemic Uncertainty in LLMs: Empirical Evaluation on ID and OOD QA Tasks," arXiv:2511.03166) empirically distinguish two fundamentally different types of uncertainty that conflate in typical LLM output: *aleatoric* uncertainty (inherent ambiguity in the question, irreducible by more information) and *epistemic* uncertainty (the model's knowledge gap, reducible in principle). Evaluating twelve uncertainty estimation methods on in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) QA tasks, they find that information-theoretic methods perform best in-distribution, while density-based methods and P(True) metrics outperform on OOD data — exactly the regime corresponding to unknown unknowns. The practical implication for hybrid intelligence design: treating all model uncertainty as a single signal underestimates the knowledge-boundary problem; OOD conditions systematically reward different estimation strategies, and current best-practice methods do not generalize. [PDF saved: Kilder/Wang2024_AleatoricEpistemicUncertaintyLLMs_arXiv2511.03166.pdf]

**Discriminating data uncertainty from model uncertainty**: Ren et al. (2026, "Beyond 'I Don't Know': Evaluating LLM Self-Awareness in Discriminating Data and Model Uncertainty," arXiv:2604.17293) introduce UA-Bench — 3,500+ questions across six datasets spanning knowledge-intensive and reasoning-intensive tasks — to test whether 18 frontier LLMs can attribute their refusals correctly: to *data uncertainty* (input-level ambiguity, irreducible by better training) versus *model uncertainty* (capability limits, reducible in principle). Their central finding: even the strongest frontier models struggle to make this discrimination reliably, and high answer accuracy does not imply strong uncertainty attribution. The hybrid intelligence implication is direct: if an AI component cannot tell *why* it is uncertain — whether the question is inherently ambiguous or its knowledge is simply insufficient — the human partner receives an undifferentiated signal. A system that says "I don't know" without distinguishing "this question has no single answer" from "I lack the training to answer this" fails to give the human partner the information needed to route effort appropriately. [PDF saved: Kilder/Ren2026_BeyondIDontKnow_UncertaintyAttribution_arXiv2604.17293.pdf]

**Operationalizing the taxonomy for system design**: the three quadrants of the Rumsfeld taxonomy present distinct design problems for hybrid systems. (1) *Known knowns* — both human and AI agree on competence — require no special machinery; (2) *Known unknowns* — both parties recognize a gap — trigger explicit information-seeking (retrieval, tool use, escalation to specialist); (3) *Unknown unknowns* — neither party recognizes the gap — is where hybrid systems fail silently. The governance problem is that category (3) is structurally invisible: there is no output signal, no internal disagreement flag, nothing to trigger verification. Current mitigation strategies (uncertainty quantification, ensemble disagreement, tracing) operate by converting (3) into (2): if a model generates inconsistent outputs across multiple samples, that variance becomes a known-unknown signal that the system can act on (Liu et al., 2025). But this conversion is not complete — some unknown unknowns remain undetectable even with ensemble methods, particularly in domains where the model has learned a plausible-but-narrow distribution that answers consistently within that false confidence envelope. For hybrid intelligence design, the implication is architectural: systems cannot rely on the AI component to self-report when it has entered unknown-unknown territory. Instead, the system must embed external verification checkpoints at decision boundaries of known high-stakes consequence, treating lack of internal AI uncertainty as a flag for mandatory human review rather than a signal of reliability.

**Implication for hybrid intelligence**: if the AI component cannot reliably represent its own knowledge boundaries, the human component in a HI system must supply that function — but this requires the human to already have enough domain knowledge to recognize AI error. This creates a complementarity condition: hybrid intelligence works best when human expertise is concentrated exactly where AI confidence is misplaced. In the Becker et al. (2025) finding — experienced developers 19% *slower* with AI tools on mature codebases — one interpretation is that experienced developers are the ones who *notice* AI hallucinations and must spend time verifying and correcting them; less experienced developers, lacking the meta-cognitive reference point, accept errors and are therefore measured as faster.

## Part 4: Implications for Hybrid Intelligence Design

**Anchor:** Amershi et al. (2019); Bansal et al. (2021)

**Appropriate reliance and automation bias**: the human-factors literature on automation (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997; Lee & See, 2004) identifies two failure modes: *misuse* (over-relying on automation) and *disuse* (under-relying, failing to take advantage of correct AI outputs). LLM overconfidence structurally pushes toward misuse: because the model signals confidence regardless of accuracy, human partners calibrated to use expressed confidence as a trust signal will over-rely. Sun et al. (2025, "Large language models are overconfident and amplify human bias," arXiv:2505.02151) provide direct empirical support: across five LLMs, models overestimate the probability their answer is correct by 20–60%; critically, LLM input more than *doubles* human overconfidence while providing only modest accuracy gains — a finding consistent with the complacency and bias amplification mechanisms documented in peer-reviewed human-factors research on automation use (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). The asymmetry — substantial confidence inflation, modest accuracy lift — is precisely the misuse failure mode instantiated. Shaw & Nave (2026, "Thinking—Fast, Slow, and Artificial: How AI is Reshaping Human Reasoning and the Rise of Cognitive Surrender," PsyArXiv, DOI:10.31234/osf.io/yk25n_v1) provide preregistered behavioural evidence at scale: across three experiments (N = 1,372; 9,593 trials), participants adopted AI outputs on a majority of trials, with accuracy tracking AI correctness (+25/−15 percentage points) — the authors term this "cognitive surrender," the behavioural signature of System 1 and System 2 being bypassed in favour of AI output without scrutiny. Critically, cognitive surrender occurred regardless of whether the AI was accurate, meaning participants did not learn to distrust an unreliable AI within the experimental window — a direct empirical parallel to the overconfidence-propagation failure mode in hybrid intelligence systems. Amershi et al. (2019, "Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction," *CHI 2019*) include explicit guidelines about uncertainty communication (G17: "make clear why the system did what it did" and G18: "notify users when it is uncertain") — but current LLMs cannot reliably implement these guidelines because they cannot reliably detect their own uncertainty. Li et al. (2024, "Understanding the Effects of Miscalibrated AI Confidence on User Trust, Reliance, and Decision Efficacy," arXiv:2402.07632) provide direct empirical evidence for the design challenge: miscalibrated AI confidence impaired users' appropriate reliance and reduced decision-making efficacy; crucially, informing users about the calibration problem enabled better detection but paradoxically worsened outcomes by inducing excessive distrust — participants under-relied even on accurate AI outputs. This double bind constrains transparency-based interventions: disclosing that a model may be miscalibrated does not restore appropriate reliance if users cannot distinguish miscalibrated from well-calibrated outputs in context, and the correction overshoots. [PDF saved: Kilder/Li2024_MiscalibratedAIConfidenceTrustReliance_arXiv2402.07632.pdf]

**Complementarity and explanation**: Bansal et al. (2021, "Does the whole exceed its parts? The effect of AI explanations on complementary team performance," *CHI 2021*) find that AI explanations do not automatically improve human-AI team performance — in some conditions they reduce it, by making humans anchor too heavily on the AI's reasoning chain. If the AI's reasoning chain is confabulated (plausible but wrong), explanations propagate the error into human judgment. This is the "confident wrongness amplified" failure mode: not only does the AI generate a wrong answer, but it provides a plausible account of *why* the wrong answer is correct, and the human updates on this account.

**Design responses**: three structural approaches have emerged in the literature:
1. **Uncertainty quantification at output** (SelfCheckGPT, Kuhn semantic uncertainty) — flag outputs with high semantic variance. Liu et al. (2025, "Uncertainty Quantification and Confidence Calibration in Large Language Models: A Survey," arXiv:2503.15850) provide a recent taxonomy of UQ methods for LLMs, categorising them along computational efficiency and uncertainty dimensions (input, reasoning, parameter, prediction), and identify scalability and interpretability as the main open challenges — a useful map for hybrid intelligence practitioners choosing among available techniques. Yadkori et al. (2026, "Complementing Self-Consistency with Cross-Model Disagreement for Uncertainty Quantification," arXiv:2604.17112) extend the standard self-consistency approach by adding a cross-model semantic disagreement signal — comparing outputs across a small ensemble of scale-matched models — and find that combined total uncertainty improves ranking calibration and selective abstention relative to within-model consistency alone. For hybrid intelligence design, this method is practically significant: it requires no model fine-tuning or access to internal logits, and can be applied as a post-hoc layer over any generation pipeline to produce a more reliable "this output is uncertain" signal. [PDF saved: Kilder/Yadkori2025_CrossModelDisagreementUQ_arXiv2604.17112.pdf]
2. **External verification gates** — architectural separation between AI generation and human verification, with mandatory review for high-stakes outputs
3. **Calibrated communication training** — fine-tuning models to express calibrated uncertainty, including "I don't know" responses (Lin et al., 2022b, "Teaching models to express their uncertainty in words," *TMLR*)

**Operationalising appropriate reliance in software engineering**: Ferino et al. (2026, "Towards an Appropriate Level of Reliance on AI: A Preliminary Reliance-Control Framework for AI in Software Engineering," arXiv:2604.10530) translate the Parasuraman & Riley (1997) overreliance/underreliance distinction into a practitioner-facing framework grounded in interviews with 22 software developers. Their central construct — developer-controlled variation of AI autonomy level as a diagnostic instrument — operationalises "appropriate reliance" as something measurable and adjustable within a workflow, rather than a fixed property of the tool. The implication for hybrid intelligence design: the reliance calibration problem is not only a model-side issue (fixing overconfident AI output) but a workflow-side issue (giving developers mechanisms to modulate and monitor their own reliance). [PDF saved: Kilder/Ferino2026_AppropriateRelianceFrameworkSE_arXiv2604.10530.pdf]

**Trust dynamics across the LLM supply chain**: Balayn et al. (2024, "An empirical exploration of trust dynamics in LLM supply chains," arXiv:2405.16310) extend the appropriate-reliance question beyond the individual developer to the full actor network — data providers, model trainers, integrators, end-users — through in-situ empirical study. Their central finding: trust relationships between supply-chain actors are frequently *uncalibrated*, with downstream actors inheriting unverified trust assignments from upstream ones. For hybrid intelligence design, this adds a structural dimension: even if individual human-AI interactions were well-calibrated, the system-level trust distribution can accumulate miscalibration as outputs propagate through integration layers. The supply-chain framing also connects epistemic miscalibration to Draft E's structural vulnerability argument: a component that overexpresses confidence passes that signal downstream, where it may be treated as validated rather than provisional.

4. **Observability infrastructure** — structured tracing of agent trajectories as a prerequisite for empirical calibration measurement at scale. AgentTrace (Yuan et al., 2026, "AgentTrace: A Structured Logging Framework for Agent System Observability," arXiv:2602.10133) proposes a schema-based logging framework that instruments LLM agents at runtime without code modification, introducing a three-surface taxonomy (cognitive, operational, contextual) that integrates with OpenTelemetry. The practical implication for calibration: if agent output traces are stored alongside outcome labels, the empirical confidence–accuracy gap (Part 1) becomes measurable in production rather than only in benchmark settings — calibration auditing becomes a continuous operational practice rather than a one-off benchmark exercise. This is the design-response complement to uncertainty quantification methods: UQ techniques tell the system *when* to distrust an output; tracing infrastructure provides the historical record needed to *measure whether* distrust was warranted and to improve the system accordingly. [PDF saved: Kilder/AgentTrace2026_StructuredLoggingAgentObservability_arXiv2602.10133.pdf]

None of these fully solves the unknown-unknowns problem, because all four operate downstream of the generation process. The model still does not know what it does not know; the interventions add signals that help humans identify when to distrust the output.

**Model collapse and the feedback loop**: a fourth concern, operating at the ecosystem level, is raised by Shumailov et al. (2024, "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data," *Nature*, 631, 755–759, arXiv:2305.17493). When models are trained on data that itself includes AI-generated content — as increasingly occurs as LLM outputs enter the training commons — the distribution of training data degrades: the tails of the original human-generated distribution are underrepresented, errors and biases in generation propagate, and successive model generations show narrowing capability and increasing error rates. In the context of Open Question 3: if AI-generated code containing hallucinated patterns enters the commons (Stage 3 → Stage 1 feedback), each training iteration potentially amplifies those patterns. This is the data-quality degradation loop as an empirically documented mechanism, not a theoretical concern. Dohmatob et al. (2024) extend this analysis to the scaling regime, showing that model collapse under synthetic-heavy training data manifests as a systematic change in scaling law exponents: successive models trained on synthetic data show altered capability-scaling curves that diverge from human-data baselines, with the divergence accelerating in the distribution tails — precisely where rare but high-value code patterns (correct handling of edge cases, uncommon library idioms) cluster (Dohmatob et al., 2024, *ICML 2024*, arXiv:2402.07043). While neither Shumailov et al. nor Dohmatob et al. study code corpora specifically, the mechanism is domain-general: any sequence modality in which AI output can re-enter training data faces the same distributional collapse dynamic, making the code-commons feedback loop identified in Open Question 3 a concrete instantiation of an empirically characterised scaling failure mode rather than a hypothetical risk.

## Open Questions

1. **Does calibration improve with scale?** Current evidence is mixed. Some benchmarks suggest larger models are better calibrated on standard tasks; Kadavath et al. (2022) suggest partial improvement in self-knowledge. But calibration on out-of-distribution queries — exactly where unknown unknowns cluster — may not improve, or may worsen. A large-scale empirical study across nine LLMs (8B to >1T parameters, dense and MoE architectures, SFT and RLHF fine-tuning) finds widespread overconfidence across all model families and sizes, with *larger models showing degraded calibration on simpler queries* in some conditions — a counterintuitive result suggesting that scale does not reliably improve calibration and may introduce new miscalibration patterns (Ulmer et al., 2025, "Mind the Confidence Gap: Overconfidence, Calibration, and Distractor Effects in Large Language Models," arXiv:2502.11028). Li et al. (2025) survey so-called "System 2" reasoning models — OpenAI o1/o3, DeepSeek R1 — as the leading candidates for closing this gap through extended chain-of-thought, but this survey's findings converge with KalshiBench's empirical result that reasoning-enhanced variants exhibit *degraded* calibration relative to base models: longer chains of reasoning do not reliably repair the confidence–accuracy gap and may introduce new overconfidence failure modes (Li, J., Peng, H., Zhang, M., et al., 2025, "From System 1 to System 2: A Survey of Reasoning LLMs," arXiv:2502.17419). Ziabari et al. (2025, "Reasoning on a Spectrum: Aligning LLMs to System 1 and System 2 Thinking," arXiv:2502.12470) provide direct empirical grounding for this inflexibility: constructing a curated alignment dataset for both thinking modes, they find System 2-aligned models excel at deliberate reasoning (arithmetic, symbolic) while System 1-aligned models succeed on intuitive tasks but fail systematically on deliberative ones — no current model generalizes robustly across both regimes. The calibration implication is structural: a model tuned for fluent, fast output will underperform in slow-reasoning contexts without any internal signal of that mode mismatch, instantiating the unknown-unknowns failure mode at the level of reasoning strategy rather than factual knowledge. [PDF saved: Kilder/Ziabari2025_ReasoningSpectrumSystem1System2_arXiv2502.12470.pdf] A complementary avenue is training-time improvement: Stengel-Eskin et al. (2025, "Improving Metacognition and Uncertainty Communication in Language Models," arXiv:2510.05126) find that supervised fine-tuning on uncertainty-labelled datasets can improve a model's expressed calibration, but the gains are task- and domain-dependent and do not generalize reliably to out-of-distribution prompts — precisely the regime where unknown unknowns cluster. The result is encouraging as proof-of-concept but underscores that training-time calibration fixes remain local: they can shift a model's behavior on known task types without closing the fundamental gap on novel or boundary-region queries.

2. **Is the hallucination problem intrinsic to transformer architectures or to current training regimes?** RLHF and next-token prediction both reward fluent, plausible output without a separate reward signal for accuracy. Architectures that include explicit factual retrieval (RAG — retrieval-augmented generation) partially address this for factual queries but do not address reasoning hallucinations. Li et al. (2025, "Mitigating Hallucination in Large Language Models (LLMs): An Application-Oriented Survey on RAG, Reasoning, and Agentic Systems," arXiv:2510.24476) provide an application-oriented taxonomy that formalises this distinction: RAG reliably reduces *knowledge-based* hallucinations (factual retrieval gaps) but is largely ineffective against *logic-based* hallucinations (reasoning errors), which require separate mitigation strategies such as chain-of-thought and structured verification. The implication is that RAG and reasoning enhancement are complementary, not substitutable — a hybrid intelligence system relying on RAG alone remains exposed to the logic-based confabulation failure mode. [PDF saved: Kilder/Li2025_HallucinationMitigationRAGReasoning_arXiv2510.24476.pdf]

3. **What does overconfidence imply for the chapter's feedback loop?** If AI-generated code enters the training commons (Stage 3 → Stage 1 feedback), and if that code contains hallucinated patterns with no internal quality signal, what is the long-run effect on model quality? Is there a data-quality degradation mechanism in the loop?

4. **Does appropriate human oversight require domain expertise that is itself being eroded by AI augmentation?** The Draft H (human-augmentation-coevolution) argument about skill atrophy applies here: if AI tools reduce human developers' practice in catching errors, the human meta-cognitive check on AI overconfidence weakens over time. The two failure modes — AI cannot self-assess, human losing ability to assess AI — compound. Empirical evidence for the first side of this compound failure is now available: Shen & Tamkin (2026, "How AI Impacts Skill Formation," arXiv:2601.20245) conducted a randomized experiment in which developers learned a new programming library either with or without AI assistance. The AI group scored 50% on a subsequent comprehension quiz versus 67% in the hand-coding group (Cohen's d=0.738, p=0.01); the largest gap appeared on debugging questions — precisely the skill required to detect hallucinated or incorrect AI output. Crucially, efficiency gains from AI assistance were not significant on average, meaning the skill cost was not offset by productivity benefit. The hybrid intelligence implication is structurally significant: if AI coding assistance systematically degrades the debugging competence needed to catch AI errors, then the intended human oversight layer of a hybrid system is attenuated by the very tool it is supposed to supervise. [PDF saved: Kilder/Shen2026_HowAIImpactsSkillFormation_arXiv2601.20245.pdf]

5. **What is the legal and organizational liability when AI overconfidence propagates into high-stakes outputs?** If a hallucinated AI output causes harm (medical, legal, engineering), and the human partner could not distinguish it from accurate output, how does liability attach? Early regulatory frameworks for large generative AI models identify product liability, professional negligence, and vicarious liability as candidate doctrines but acknowledge significant gaps when harm is caused by model output that no individual designer or deployer specifically foresaw (Hacker et al., 2023). The regulatory landscape has advanced substantially since that analysis. The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, entered into force August 2024) addresses human oversight obligations directly in Article 14, requiring high-risk AI systems to be designed so that natural persons can effectively oversee outputs and intervene — a requirement that places the calibration failure modes documented throughout this draft squarely in the compliance frame: a system that produces overconfident outputs without surfacing uncertainty signals may fail the Article 14 human oversight standard. The revised Product Liability Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/2853, adopted October 2024) explicitly extends the product liability regime to AI systems and software, closing the prior exclusion under which software outputs fell outside the directive's scope; under the revised directive, defective AI output that causes harm can ground a product liability claim against the developer, regardless of whether the harm was specifically foreseeable at design time. Together, these instruments establish the first comprehensive EU liability framework for generative AI output harms — though significant interpretive questions remain around the standard of care applicable to probabilistic, non-deterministic generative systems whose output variance is architectural rather than the result of a specific design defect. This connects to Draft D (governance patterns) and to the broader hybrid intelligence accountability literature.
