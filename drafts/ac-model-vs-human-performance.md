## Overview and Chapter Position

The claim that AI systems have reached or surpassed human-level performance is made frequently and disputed frequently. Both the claims and the disputes are often poorly specified: *which* AI system, performing *which* task, relative to *which* humans, measured by *which* metric, under *which* conditions? The answers to these questions change the conclusion dramatically.

This draft surveys the empirical literature on AI-versus-human performance comparisons across major domains, examines the methodological challenges that make such comparisons difficult to interpret, and identifies what the literature does and does not establish. For hybrid intelligence design, the question is not merely "can AI match humans?" but "at which specific tasks, under which conditions, and with what failure modes?" — because these details determine how human and AI contributions should be combined.

The draft connects directly to `g-agentic-hi-collaboration.md` (complementarity hypothesis), `o-hi-ecosystems-studies.md` (empirical HI domain studies), `aa-ai-outperformance-psychology.md` (human response to being outperformed), `ab-credit-blame-agent-attribution.md` (accountability when agents outperform), and `v-ai-paradigms-development.md` (the paradigms that produce high-performance systems).

## Part 1: The Measurement Problem — What Does "Human-Level" Mean?

**1.1 Which humans?**
The choice of human baseline fundamentally determines the comparison outcome. The literature uses at least four different baselines:

- *Median human*: an average, untrained adult performing the task — typically the easiest standard to surpass
- *Trained non-expert*: a person with general education but no domain specialisation
- *Domain expert*: a credentialed specialist (board-certified physician, licensed attorney, software engineer with N years experience)
- *World-class expert*: the best humans in the domain (grandmaster chess players, Nobel Prize–winning scientists)

A system that "matches physician performance" on a medical task may mean it matches a first-year resident, a community practitioner, or a specialist — and these are very different benchmarks. Papers that claim human-level performance without specifying which level of human are uninterpretable.

**1.2 Which task?**
Performance comparisons are almost always task-specific, and task design drives outcomes. Four dimensions of task design matter:

- *Ecological validity*: does the benchmark task resemble the real-world professional task? Medical imaging benchmarks often use carefully curated, high-quality images; real clinical practice involves motion artefacts, suboptimal positioning, and clinical context
- *Scope*: a narrow subtask (classify this image as malignant or benign) versus the full professional workflow (take a history, examine the patient, order tests, diagnose, treat)
- *Closed vs. open domain*: tasks with fixed answer spaces (multiple choice, binary classification) are more tractable than open-ended generation tasks
- *Time constraint*: humans under time pressure perform differently from humans given unlimited review time; most AI benchmarks have no time constraint

**1.3 Which metric?**
Accuracy is the dominant metric in the literature, but accuracy alone is often insufficient:

- *Calibration*: does the system's confidence match its actual accuracy? AI systems that are accurate but poorly calibrated produce false confidence in hybrid workflows (see Draft I)
- *Error distribution*: a system with the same average accuracy as humans may make systematically different errors — failing on exactly the cases where human expertise is most valuable
- *Consistency*: AI systems are typically more consistent than humans (same input, same output) — which is an advantage in high-volume screening and a disadvantage in cases requiring contextual judgment
- *Speed*: AI systems typically produce outputs orders of magnitude faster than human experts — a comparison-relevant advantage for high-throughput tasks

**1.4 Benchmark contamination**
A pervasive methodological concern: AI systems trained on internet-scale data may have been trained on data that includes the test sets used to evaluate them. Benchmark contamination — models that have effectively "seen" the answers during training — inflates apparent performance on standardised benchmarks relative to genuinely novel tasks (Sainz, Campos, García-Ferrero, Etxaniz, & López de Lacalle, 2023, *Findings of EMNLP 2023*). Recent studies have quantified this effect directly: Golchin & Surdeanu (2023) demonstrate via temporal analysis that frontier LLMs exhibit systematic contamination across widely used benchmarks, detectable through their knowledge of data from specific time windows; Dong, Jiang, Liu & Jin (2024, *Findings of ACL 2024*) find that benchmark contamination produces measurable performance inflation, with genuinely held-out evaluation revealing substantially lower scores on contaminated benchmarks. This is particularly acute for knowledge-based benchmarks (bar exams, medical licensing exams, trivia) where the questions and answers circulate online. Performance on novel, held-out task variants is a more reliable indicator of genuine capability.

## Part 2: Games and Formal Domains — Unambiguous AI Superiority

**2.1 Chess and strategic games**
Chess is historically the paradigmatic human-AI comparison domain. Deep Blue's 1997 victory over Kasparov established AI's first unambiguous defeat of the world's best human in a high-status cognitive domain. Stockfish (current chess engine) plays at an estimated Elo of ~3600 (CCRL, 2026); Magnus Carlsen, the highest-rated human player, peaks around 2882 (FIDE, 2024). The performance gap is not marginal — it is categorical and has been since approximately 2005 (Campbell et al., 2002).

The significance for HI research is not that chess engines are "intelligent" in a general sense, but that the performance ceiling in a constrained formal domain was reached and decisively surpassed within decades of serious effort — and that human response to this surpassing followed the denial-reconceptualisation-engagement arc described in Draft AA.

**2.2 Go: AlphaGo and AlphaZero**
Silver et al. (2016) demonstrated that AlphaGo — trained on human expert games plus self-play — defeated 9-dan professional Go player Lee Sedol 4–1. AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., 2017) subsequently achieved superior performance using *no human data at all*, training purely through self-play against itself. AlphaZero (Silver et al., 2018) extended this to chess and shogi, reaching superhuman performance in hours of training from random initial play.

The significance is the demonstration that superhuman performance can be achieved without learning from human demonstrations — a result relevant to the "Era of Experience" thesis (Draft V) and to the question of whether human-generated training data is necessary for high performance.

**2.3 Real-time strategy: StarCraft II**
Vinyals et al. (2019) reported that AlphaStar reached grandmaster level in StarCraft II (defined as the top 0.2% of all active players) — a significantly harder problem than chess or Go due to real-time execution, imperfect information, and the large action space. AlphaStar defeated professional players while operating under constraints designed to match human reaction time limits, including an enforced cap on actions per minute (APM) calibrated to human professional rates. This result extended the domain of established AI superiority from turn-based perfect-information games to real-time partial-information strategy.

**2.4 Natural language strategy: Diplomacy**
Bakhtin et al. (2022) reported that Cicero, a system combining language models with strategic reasoning, scored more than twice the average human score and ranked in the top 10% of players across 40 anonymous online games of Diplomacy — a natural-language negotiation and strategy game requiring long-horizon planning, coalition building, and deceptive communication (Bakhtin et al., 2022). This result is significant because it extends demonstrated AI performance into a domain requiring natural language pragmatics and social reasoning, not merely pattern matching on formal state spaces.

## Part 3: Medicine — Performance at Expert Level on Specific Tasks

**3.1 Dermatology: Esteva et al. (2017)**
Esteva, Kuprel, Novoa et al. (2017, *Nature*, 542, 115–118) trained a convolutional neural network on 129,450 clinical images and compared its performance on skin lesion classification to 21 board-certified dermatologists. On two binary classification tasks (keratinocyte carcinoma vs. benign seborrheic keratosis; malignant melanoma vs. benign nevi), the CNN matched or exceeded the mean dermatologist performance on the test set. This was the first rigorously controlled demonstration that a deep learning system could match specialist physician performance on a clinical visual classification task.

The study is widely cited but subject to the methodological caveats above: the test images were high-quality biopsy-confirmed photographs, not the variable-quality images encountered in clinical dermatoscopy; and "matching dermatologist performance" meant the median of 21 dermatologists, not specialist consensus or a reference standard.

**3.2 Radiology: lung cancer screening**
Ardila, Kiraly, Bharadwaj et al. (2019, *Nature Medicine*, 25, 954–961) demonstrated that a deep learning model for lung cancer detection from low-dose CT scans achieved performance exceeding that of six radiologists when given only the current scan (without prior imaging history). When prior scans were available — the standard clinical workflow — performance was comparable to radiologists. Importantly, the model reduced both false positives (unnecessary follow-up) and false negatives (missed cancers).

**3.3 Ophthalmology: diabetic retinopathy at national scale**
The SELENA+ system deployed in Singapore (noted in Draft Y) represents the transition from research performance to national clinical deployment. Ting et al. (2017, *JAMA*, 318(22), 2211–2223) reported that a deep learning system for diabetic retinopathy grading achieved 90.5% sensitivity and 91.6% specificity — exceeding the performance of non-specialist graders and comparable to retinal specialists. The system has since been deployed across Singaporean polyclinics and adopted in several other countries (Xie et al., 2020).

**3.4 Medical knowledge: licensing examination performance**
The GPT-4 technical report (OpenAI, 2023) reported that GPT-4 passed the US Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) at approximately the 90th percentile of human test-takers — exceeding the passing threshold by a substantial margin. Nori, King, McKinney et al. (2023) independently validated GPT-4's performance on the USMLE, finding scores exceeding the passing threshold across all three USMLE steps — consistent with the 90th-percentile result reported by OpenAI (Nori et al., 2023). The "90th percentile" framing, however, merits the same methodological caveat applied to the bar-exam results in §4.1: Sung & Brin (2023) *PLOS Digital Health* conducted a methodological re-evaluation of USMLE percentile claims and found that ranking varies substantially depending on the reference cohort (actual test-takers vs. re-take subsets); the pass-level claim survives scrutiny, but the rank-order claim does not. Singhal, Azizi, Tu et al. (2023, *Nature*) introduced Med-PaLM, demonstrating that large language models can encode clinical knowledge at a level sufficient for high performance on structured medical QA. Med-PaLM 2, the successor system, achieved 86.5% on MedQA (USMLE-style questions) — well above the approximate 60% human passing threshold — and was preferred over unassisted physicians by clinician raters in open-ended question answering (Singhal, Tu, Gottweis et al., 2025, *Nature Medicine*).

The critical qualification: licensing examination performance measures declarative medical knowledge, not clinical judgment, diagnostic reasoning in context, or the full scope of physician practice. GPT-4 passing the USMLE establishes that AI systems can retrieve and apply codified medical knowledge at expert level; it does not establish that they can do medicine.

## Part 4: Legal and Professional Domains

**4.1 Bar examination performance**
GPT-4 passed the Uniform Bar Examination at approximately the 90th percentile (OpenAI, 2023). Katz, Bommarito, Gao & Arredondo (2024, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A*, 382, 20230254) documented GPT-4's performance across multiple components of the bar exam, finding performance in the passing range for all components and in the high-performing range for several. Earlier models (GPT-3.5) performed at the 10th percentile — below the passing threshold (Bommarito & Katz, 2022). The "approximately 90th percentile" framing has, however, been contested: Martínez (2024, *Artificial Intelligence and Law*) re-evaluated the same numerical results against more representative reference populations and concluded that, while GPT-4's pass-level performance is not in dispute, the 90th-percentile claim depends on the choice of comparison cohort and is closer to the 48th–69th percentile when benchmarked against the full population of bar-exam takers rather than a re-take subset. The qualitative claim (GPT-4 passes) survives the critique; the rank-order quantification does not.

The passage from failing to passing in a single model generation (GPT-3.5 → GPT-4) illustrates a general pattern: capability transitions in specific benchmarks can be rapid and non-linear, occurring within a single generation of model improvement.

**4.2 Legal reasoning and judgment**
Performance on bar examinations measures legal knowledge recall and application of doctrine to standardised scenarios. Performance on actual legal judgment — predicting case outcomes, drafting contracts that withstand adversarial scrutiny, conducting depositions — is less studied and more contested. Surden (2014) surveys the structure of legal tasks and argues that tasks requiring pattern recognition in large text corpora (document review, contract analysis, due diligence) are well-suited to AI; tasks requiring contextual judgment, client counselling, and novel argumentation are not.

## Part 5: Software Engineering — Nuanced and Task-Dependent

**5.1 Code generation benchmarks**
HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) are the dominant benchmarks for code generation. GPT-4 achieves approximately 85–90% pass rates on HumanEval with code-optimised prompting (OpenAI, 2023; Bubeck et al., 2023); human performance on the same benchmark stands at approximately 72% pass@1 for a qualified software engineer (Chen et al., 2021) — above GPT-4 0-shot (OpenAI, 2023: 67%) but below optimised-prompting configurations (Bubeck et al., 2023). SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., 2024) presents a harder challenge: fixing real GitHub issues in existing codebases. State-of-the-art systems achieve 40–50% resolution rates on SWE-bench Verified (SWE-bench, 2025) — below what experienced human engineers achieve on well-specified issues (Jimenez et al., 2024), but extraordinary relative to performance a year prior.

**5.2 Controlled RCT: Becker et al. (2025)**
The most methodologically rigorous productivity study to date: a 246-task randomised controlled trial in which professional open-source developers were assigned either frontier AI coding assistance or no assistance, on tasks drawn from their own active codebases (Becker et al., 2025). Results:
- Junior developers on new-feature tasks: 20–30% productivity increase
- Experienced developers on mature-codebase tasks: 19% productivity *decrease*
- The source of the decrease: the overhead of prompting, reviewing, and integrating AI output exceeded the AI's contribution for complex tasks requiring deep codebase context

This is the most important controlled result in the HI-coding literature: it refutes the simple story that AI assistance uniformly accelerates developer productivity and reveals that human expertise, task type, and codebase context all mediate the direction and magnitude of the effect.

**5.3 Skill formation effects: Shen & Tamkin (2026)**
Separate from productivity comparisons, Shen & Tamkin (2026, arXiv:2601.20245) find that developers who learn a new library with AI assistance show impaired conceptual understanding and debugging ability relative to developers who learn without assistance — without significant efficiency gains. The AI assistance substitutes for cognitive engagement that is necessary for skill formation. This is a performance comparison in the negative direction: human performance *with* AI is worse than human performance *without* AI on the learning-phase dimension.

**5.4 Autonomous ML pipeline implementation: Sherwood et al. (2026)**
A different class of software-engineering benchmark measures not whether agents can write isolated functions but whether they can autonomously implement complete machine-learning systems end-to-end. Sherwood, Aybar & Kaplan (2026, arXiv:2604.25067) benchmarked frontier coding agents (Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.4, and others) on the task of autonomously implementing a complete AlphaZero-style self-play ML training pipeline for Connect Four within three hours on consumer hardware, then evaluating the trained agent against the Pascal Pons external solver. Claude Opus 4.7 won 7 of 8 trials with statistically significant superiority over competitors. Two features of this result matter for the AI-vs-human comparison literature:

- **Capability progression timescale.** The same task was unreliably completed by frontier agents in January 2026 and was near-saturated by April 2026 — a three-month transition from "rarely completes" to "near-saturation." This is faster than the GPT-3.5 → GPT-4 bar-exam transition (§4.1) and exemplifies the rapid, non-linear capability transitions characteristic of frontier coding agents.
- **Synthesis of paradigms.** The benchmark requires the agent (a frontier LLM, Paradigm I in the framing of Draft V) to autonomously implement a self-play RL system (Paradigm II). Cross-paradigm implementation by autonomous agents is qualitatively different from solving an isolated coding problem and resists easy comparison to a single "human baseline" — the appropriate comparison is to a senior ML engineer given the same task and budget, which the literature has not yet measured at scale.

The result is a 2026 capability data point, not yet replicated by independent groups, and is best read as one component of the rapidly evolving frontier rather than as a stable benchmark.

## Part 6: Scientific Discovery — Landmark Results

**6.1 AlphaFold2 and protein structure prediction**
Jumper, Evans, Pritzel et al. (2021, *Nature*, 596, 583–589) reported that AlphaFold2 achieved median GDT_TS scores above 90 across all CASP14 targets — performance described by the field as solving protein structure prediction, a problem that had resisted 50 years of concerted human effort. For comparison, the next-best CASP14 participant (a human-led group) scored approximately 75 GDT_TS (Kryshtafovych et al., 2021).

AlphaFold2 is the most unambiguous case of AI achieving qualitatively superhuman performance on a scientifically significant task — not merely matching the best humans, but achieving results that the best human methods could not approach within the foreseeable future.

**6.2 Mathematical reasoning**
Mathematical olympiad problems represent a challenge domain where AI performance has improved rapidly but not yet clearly surpassed expert human performance. AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 (DeepMind, 2024) together achieved performance on International Mathematical Olympiad problems equivalent to a silver medal — AlphaGeometry 2 solving geometry problems via neural-symbolic methods (DeepMind, 2024; for the predecessor AlphaGeometry system, see Trinh et al., 2024), and AlphaProof solving algebra and number theory via Lean-based formal proofs (DeepMind, 2024) — below gold-medal performance but above the majority of IMO 2024 contestants — the silver-medal threshold falls above the median participant score, with roughly 75% of the ~609 competing students scoring below silver-medal level (International Mathematical Olympiad, 2024). This is a domain where symbolic reasoning meets creative problem-solving; the rate of improvement suggests that expert-level performance is approaching within the current development trajectory.

**6.3 Algorithm design: AlphaEvolve**
AlphaEvolve (DeepMind, 2025) extends the scientific-discovery pattern to algorithmic domains: rather than solving predefined problems, it discovers new algorithms superior to existing human-designed ones. Using a Gemini-based evolutionary search — Gemini Flash for broad candidate generation, Gemini Pro for depth refinement — guided by automatic correctness evaluators, AlphaEvolve improved the Strassen 4×4 matrix multiplication algorithm for the first time since 1969, surpassing a mathematical result that had withstood 56 years of human effort. The human comparison point is unambiguous: not a standardised benchmark, but a well-known result verified by the research community over decades. AlphaEvolve also found new solutions to open combinatorial problems including the kissing number problem, demonstrating that automated algorithmic search can open rather than merely saturate established problem domains.

## Part 7: Synthesis — What the Literature Establishes

**7.1 The performance profile divergence**
AI systems do not have uniform performance profiles relative to humans. A characteristic pattern across domains:

| Task type | AI relative performance | Human advantage |
|---|---|---|
| High-volume pattern classification in well-defined spaces | At or above specialist | Contextual judgment, rare edge cases |
| Recall and application of codified knowledge | At or above expert | Novel, undocumented situations |
| Formal search over constrained state spaces | Categorically superior | None at the core task |
| Procedural code generation (new tasks) | Comparable to competent engineer | Deep codebase context, requirement elicitation |
| Novel creative synthesis | Task-dependent; often impressive (Doshi & Hauser, 2024) | Transformational creativity, subjective intentionality |
| Social and relational tasks | Below human (Zhou et al., 2024) | Embodied trust, contextual pragmatics |
| Learning and skill formation | Below human with AI assistance | Ability to form robust mental models |

**7.2 The benchmark saturation problem**
A consistent pattern: AI systems rapidly saturate benchmarks designed to test "human-level" performance, prompting the creation of harder benchmarks, which are then saturated in turn. MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021) — designed to cover professional and academic knowledge — was saturated within two years of publication (OpenAI 2023; Google 2023). ARC-AGI (Chollet, 2019) — designed to resist pattern-matching by requiring abstract reasoning on novel patterns — showed rapid AI progress through 2024, with OpenAI's o3 achieving approximately 87.5% on the semi-private test set in December 2024 (ARC Prize Foundation, 2024), up from near-zero scores for frontier models in 2022; performance under fully reproducible conditions and the benchmark's long-term viability as a capability ceiling remain open questions. The rate of benchmark saturation makes it difficult to maintain stable benchmarks for longitudinal human-AI comparison; the Sherwood et al. (2026) result discussed in §5.4 — a January-to-April 2026 transition from "rarely completes" to near-saturation on autonomous ML pipeline implementation — is the most compressed transition documented to date and illustrates that the saturation timescale for agentic capabilities is now measured in months rather than years.

**7.3 The ecological validity gap**
Controlled benchmark performance consistently overestimates real-world performance. The Ardila et al. (2019) result showed AI matching radiologists on curated benchmark images; subsequent deployment studies found larger false-positive rates in clinical settings (Schreuder et al., 2021). The SWE-bench performance of coding agents on standardised issues does not straightforwardly transfer to the open-ended, underspecified real-world software engineering tasks (Jimenez et al., 2024). Ecological validity is the most consistent methodological shortcoming in the AI-vs-human performance literature.

## Open Questions

1. **Is "human-level performance" a meaningful target?** Human performance varies widely and is itself domain-, individual-, and context-specific. What would it mean for AI to reach "human-level" in a domain where the performance distribution spans multiple orders of magnitude?

2. **What is the correct metric for HI design?** For hybrid intelligence, the relevant question is not "does AI match or exceed humans?" but "where do AI and human error distributions diverge, and how can they be combined to reduce total error?" Performance comparison studies rarely report the information needed to answer this question.

3. **How do we study performance at the frontier?** Comparisons against median or expert humans underestimate what the best humans can achieve. As AI systems approach world-class performance in domain after domain, what are the appropriate human reference points — and how do we study human-AI collaboration when the relevant humans are themselves increasingly rare?

4. **Does benchmark performance predict deployment value?** The gap between benchmark performance and real-world utility is not well-characterised. How much of the gap is methodological (ecological validity, benchmark contamination) and how much is fundamental to the difference between narrow benchmark tasks and open-ended professional practice?

5. **What is the right timescale for skill formation comparisons?** Shen & Tamkin (2026) measure skill formation over weeks. The GPS/hippocampus literature (Draft H) suggests that effects on cognitive structure may operate over years or decades. Productivity benchmarks measure performance at a single point in time. How do these different timescales interact?
