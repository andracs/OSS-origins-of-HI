# Draft D — The Open Source Organization: Institutional Templates for Human-Agent Collectives
**Version:** v0.11 (2026-05-11)
**Status:** active

---

## Framing

The previous sections of this handbook establish that hybrid intelligence systems depend on the interplay of human cognition and machine reasoning. This section asks a structural question: *what kind of organization can sustain that interplay at institutional scale?*

The argument is that open source software development — one of the most thoroughly studied forms of large-scale distributed coordination — provides not merely an analogy but a set of *tested institutional templates* for organizations that blend human and agent participants. Open source projects have solved, imperfectly but demonstrably, problems that hybrid human-agent organizations will face: how to allocate decision rights without centralized authority; how to structure contribution so that quality is maintained without gatekeeping; how to handle disagreement between participants with different information and different values; and how to create exit mechanisms that preserve the integrity of the whole.

The claim is not that open source governance is optimal. It is that open source governance has *accumulated* solutions to coordination problems that are structurally similar to those facing hybrid organizations — and that the playbook metaphor is apt precisely because these solutions are modular, combinable, and have documented failure modes.

## 1. The structural parallel: why open source governance maps onto hybrid intelligence organizations

The empirical foundation for this transferability claim is robust. Benkler's (2006) analysis of *commons-based peer production* in *The Wealth of Networks* establishes that large-scale, distributed, non-hierarchical production can outperform both market and managerial coordination on tasks where information is widely distributed, contributors are heterogeneous, and modular decomposition is feasible — conditions that match both mature open-source projects and the agentic-AI deployment contexts examined here (Benkler, 2006). Von Hippel's (2005) *Democratizing Innovation* provides the complementary user-innovation grounding: distributed user-innovators with sticky local information often outperform centralized R&D on need-identification and prototype generation, and the resulting innovations diffuse through "horizontal innovation networks" rather than firm-mediated channels (von Hippel, 2005). Raymond's (1999) *The Cathedral and the Bazaar* — already cited in Pattern 4's IETF analogue — closes the triangle by documenting the specific governance affordances (release early, release often; treat users as co-developers; many eyeballs make bugs shallow) that operationalize Benkler's and von Hippel's general claims at the level of a software project. For hybrid human-agent organizations, the inheritance is direct: the organizational forms that emerged from open source were not optimized abstractly; they were selected against the same coordination problems — heterogeneous capability, voluntary participation, distributed information, modular outputs — that hybrid intelligence systems now confront at organizational scale.

Open source projects share four structural features with hybrid human-agent organizations:

**1. Asymmetric capability distribution.** In open source projects, contributors differ dramatically in their ability to reason about different parts of the codebase. A contributor who is expert in the networking stack may be unable to evaluate a change to the cryptography module. In hybrid organizations, humans and agents differ in their ability to process certain kinds of information: agents may be faster and more consistent on structured, high-volume reasoning tasks; humans may be better at contextual judgment, ethical reasoning, and managing external relationships (Autor, 2015; Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017). Neither form of asymmetry is fixed — contributors learn, models improve — but both require governance that does not assume uniform competence.

**2. Distributed contribution without centralized employment.** Open source governance must coordinate participants who are not in a hierarchical reporting relationship, whose contributions are voluntary (or semi-voluntary), and whose motivations are heterogeneous. Hybrid organizations that incorporate AI agents face an analogous design problem: agents are not employees, their "motivations" are encoded in training and configuration rather than arising from interests, and they cannot be managed through the same accountability mechanisms as human staff.

**3. Audit trails as governance infrastructure.** Git commit histories, issue trackers, code review threads, and RFC processes create a comprehensive record of how decisions were made, who contributed what, and what was rejected and why. This record is not merely archival — it is the primary mechanism through which trust is established in open source communities. The sociomateriality lens of Orlikowski (2007) is apt here: these tools are not neutral instruments overlaid on a separately existing governance practice but are *constitutively entangled* with it. Orlikowski's central claim — that "there is no social that is not material, and no material that is not social" — implies that the version control system, the code review interface, and the issue tracker are not merely the *site* of governance but partly *constitute* what governance is in an open source community (Orlikowski, 2007). Decision authority, accountability, and visibility are inseparable from the artefacts that record them. Hybrid organizations require analogous infrastructure for agent actions: logs of what was decided, what information was accessed, what alternatives were considered. Following Orlikowski, the choice of *which* logging infrastructure is adopted is itself a governance choice — different audit instruments will materialize different forms of accountability.

**4. Fork as institutional exit.** When a community disagrees irreconcilably about direction, open source enables fork: a subset of participants takes the shared codebase and develops it independently. This exit mechanism — costly but available — shapes the equilibrium of governance: knowing that a faction can fork disciplines the exercise of authority by those who hold it (Robles & González-Barahona, 2012; Nyman & Lindman, 2013). Hybrid organizations need analogous mechanisms for managing irreducible disagreements between human and agent participants, or between human participants with different views of how agents should be used.

**Ostrom's benchmark for self-governing commons.**
The Benkler/von Hippel/Raymond triangle establishes *that* open-source-style distributed coordination can work; Ostrom's (1990) *Governing the Commons* establishes *under what structural conditions* it does. Against the "tragedy of the commons" prediction that shared resources are overexploited in the absence of private property or state management, Ostrom's analysis of 200+ long-surviving irrigation systems, fisheries, and pasturelands identified eight design principles that distinguish institutions that succeed from those that collapse: (1) clearly defined boundaries of entitlement; (2) rules adapted to local conditions; (3) collective-choice arrangements that allow those affected to modify the rules; (4) effective monitoring; (5) graduated sanctions; (6) accessible conflict-resolution mechanisms; (7) recognition of rights to organize by external authorities; and (8) nested governance for larger systems (Ostrom, 1990). The four structural features enumerated above map onto Ostrom's criteria: asymmetric capability distribution → principle 2 (rules must fit local conditions, not be imported uniformly); distributed contribution → principle 3 (those affected must be able to modify rules); audit trails → principle 4 (effective monitoring); fork → principle 6 (accessible conflict resolution and exit). A hybrid organization that incorporates AI agents strains the framework at exactly the points where agent participation differs from human participation: agents currently struggle to participate in collective-choice arrangements (principle 3) and to engage in conflict resolution as equals (principle 6) [though recent work on AI deliberation — Tessler et al. (2024) on machine participation in collective decisions, Bakker et al. (2022) on AI consensus-building — complicates this categorical framing], and their "boundary" status is ambiguous — they are simultaneously resource users, tools, and governance participants. Satisfying the Ostrom criteria therefore requires explicitly *designing around* these current agent participation constraints rather than treating agents as equivalent to human commons participants.

## 2. Four organizational design patterns (playbooks)

The following four patterns are not prescriptions. They are abstractions from observed practice — in open source governance, in early hybrid AI deployments, and in the theoretical literature on principal-agent relationships. Each pattern is described with: a structural definition, the typical context in which it fits, known failure modes, and an illustrative narrative.

The principal-agent framing of Jensen and Meckling (1976) provides the formal scaffolding for these patterns. In their account, the agency relationship is a contract under which a principal engages an agent to perform some service on her behalf and delegates decision-making authority; whenever the agent's utility function diverges from the principal's, the principal incurs *agency costs* — monitoring expenditures, bonding expenditures, and residual loss (Jensen & Meckling, 1976, pp. 308–309). The patterns below differ primarily in which of these cost categories they minimize and which they accept. Hybrid organizations face a particular wrinkle: an AI agent has no exogenous utility function to align with the principal's; its objectives are configured. This collapses bonding expenditures (the agent cannot credibly commit on its own behalf) and shifts the burden onto monitoring infrastructure and configuration governance — the digital analogue of what Jensen and Meckling termed the costs of structuring, bonding, and monitoring.

### Pattern 1: The Delegated Executor

**Structure:** The agent acts autonomously within a defined task scope; all outputs are reviewed by a human authority before taking effect. The human may approve, reject, or modify; the agent cannot initiate action beyond its defined scope.

**Typical context:** High-volume, structured tasks where agent speed and consistency are valuable but where errors are reversible and the cost of human review per item is low. Examples: automated code review suggestions (human merges or ignores), draft generation for human editing, data pipeline monitoring with human-triggered remediation.

**Open source analogue:** Automated bots in GitHub repositories — dependabot for dependency updates, CI pipelines for test runs — where bot-generated PRs require human merge approval. Wessel, Serebrenik, Wiese, Steinmacher, and Gerosa (2022) employed a regression-discontinuity design across 1,194 GitHub projects, complemented by twelve practitioner interviews, and found that adopting a code review bot increased monthly merged pull requests, decreased monthly non-merged pull requests, and decreased communication among developers — a quantitative confirmation that the Delegated Executor pattern can deliver throughput gains while degrading the discursive layer in which novices learn (Wessel et al., 2022, "Quality gatekeepers: investigating the effects of code review bots on pull request activities," *Empirical Software Engineering*, 27(108)). The paper's Section 6 notes that the same communication reduction implicates newcomer onboarding — bots can replace the feedback loop through which newcomers historically received mentoring, raising a separate concern about long-run community attrition. In agency-cost terms, monitoring is shifted from line-by-line human review to bot-mediated structured checks; residual loss reappears as community attrition rather than as defective code.

**Failure modes:** Review fatigue — if volume is high enough, human reviewers begin approving without reading, defeating the oversight mechanism (van der Sijs et al., 2006). Scope creep — agents optimizing within a narrow scope may generate outputs that have large effects outside it.

**Narrative vignette:** A municipal procurement office deploys an AI agent to draft tender evaluation summaries. Procurement officers review each summary before it is entered into the record. In the first three months, the review catches several cases where the agent has misread scoring criteria. By month six, the volume has increased and reviews are averaging 90 seconds each. An external audit finds that 23% of summaries in month six contain unreviewed errors that passed into the official record.

### Pattern 2: The Augmented Deliberator

**Structure:** Humans retain full decision authority; the agent's role is to provide structured challenge — presenting evidence that conflicts with the human's stated position, identifying gaps in reasoning, surfacing alternatives that were not considered. The agent has no vote and cannot block decisions, but its outputs are formally part of the deliberation record.

**Typical context:** Strategic decisions with high stakes and uncertain information environments, where the primary governance risk is groupthink or anchoring bias among human decision-makers.

**Open source analogue:** RFC (Request for Comments) processes in the IETF, where structured written critique is required before a proposal can advance. The RFC process does not give any individual veto power but creates a formal record of objections that must be addressed.

**Failure modes:** Automation bias in reverse — if the agent consistently argues against proposals that are eventually adopted, humans may learn to discount its objections categorically (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Alternatively, if the agent's challenges are perceived as always technically valid, humans may defer to it in domains where contextual judgment should dominate.

**Narrative vignette:** A hospital ethics board integrates an AI agent that reviews proposed treatment protocols against published clinical evidence and flags conflicts with established guidelines. Before each vote, board members receive the agent's analysis. The board retains full authority to override. In one case, the agent flags a proposed chemotherapy protocol as inconsistent with three recent trial results. The board's oncologist responds that the cited trials used different patient populations. The board votes to proceed; the agent's objection and the oncologist's response are both entered into the minutes.

### Pattern 3: The Autonomous Steward

**Structure:** The agent operates with significant autonomy within a bounded domain, making decisions and taking actions without per-instance human approval. The boundaries of the domain are set by humans; the agent is accountable to humans for outcomes within those boundaries but has discretion over method. Regular reporting and exception-triggered escalation are the primary oversight mechanisms.

**Typical context:** Stable, well-defined operational domains where the cost of per-instance oversight is prohibitive and where the consequences of individual errors are limited and recoverable. Examples: infrastructure monitoring and automated remediation, content moderation first-pass triage, financial reporting under defined accounting rules.

**Open source analogue:** "Maintainer" roles in large open source projects — individuals (or, increasingly, automated systems) who have been granted write access to a repository and can merge contributions within their domain without project-wide approval, subject to periodic review of their activity. Shah's (2006) inductive comparative study of two software communities with sharply different governance regimes — an open community (Apache-style) and a gated/sponsored community — provides the most cited empirical anchor for how this pattern persists. Shah finds that initial participation in both regimes is overwhelmingly driven by a *need-based* motive: contributors want a software-related problem solved. The majority leave once their immediate need is met; a small subset — Shah's "hobbyists" — remain, and their motives evolve from need-based to enjoyment-based and identity-based engagement (Shah, 2006, *Management Science*, 52(7), 1000–1014). It is this hobbyist subset that takes on unglamorous architectural maintenance work — exactly the steward function this pattern formalizes — and Shah documents that the long-term viability of the codebase depends on it. Crucially, governance regime affects whether hobbyists develop at all: gated communities that retain proprietary control over critical architectural decisions tend to produce fewer enduring stewards, because the contributor's evolving sense of co-ownership is structurally limited (Shah, 2006). The implication for AI autonomous stewards is direct: an agent assigned to a stewardship role does not have an evolving motivational arc; its "engagement" is configured. This collapses what Shah identifies as the principal long-term mechanism of code quality maintenance, and creates an organizational dependency on the configuration regime to substitute for what intrinsic motivation provided in the human case.

The steward role is particularly consequential in open source because maintainers are not merely executing a defined task; they are making ad-hoc judgment calls that shape the project's technical direction. Raymond (1999) emphasizes that the maintainer's authority is not formal but reputational: a maintainer loses write access if the community loses confidence in their judgment. This reputational accountability is unavailable to AI agents. A human steward who makes a poor decision faces career consequences within the community; an agent's configuration can be changed unilaterally by the organization that owns it. The implication is that Pattern 3 deployments for agents require stronger *structural* governance constraints than humans require *reputational* constraints. Where humans rely on professional identity and community standing, agents require monitoring infrastructure and override capacity.

An additional complication arises when steward tasks involve dispute resolution or technical judgment in contested domains. Mockus, Fielding, and Herbsleb's (2002) comparative study of Apache and Mozilla documents that the proportion of a maintainer's time devoted to *coordination* (responding to emails, resolving conflicts, justifying design decisions) becomes a dominant cost at the scale of mature open source communities — coordination load growing substantially as contributor communities expand beyond a core set of active committers (Mockus et al., 2002). For agents, this coordination overhead is poorly captured by traditional metrics. An agent that resolves a dispute purely through technical analysis without engaging the social dimension of the controversy may technically "solve" the problem while degrading community cohesion. This suggests that Pattern 3 is ill-suited to tasks that require legitimacy maintenance or conflict negotiation, and that the Pattern 2 (Augmented Deliberator) posture is often more appropriate when a decision affects contested organizational values.

**Failure modes:** Domain boundary ambiguity — in practice, operational domains are rarely clean, and agents optimizing within their defined domain will routinely encounter actions that affect adjacent domains. Exception escalation failure — if the agent's exception-detection logic is miscalibrated, it may operate autonomously in situations that should have triggered human review. Identity-motivation gap — unlike humans who develop ownership and investment in steward roles, agents lack the evolved identity-based motivation that sustains unglamorous work; organizations must compensate through explicit incentive configuration and periodic override exercises to prevent automation atrophy.

**Narrative vignette:** A logistics company grants an AI agent autonomous authority to reschedule deliveries when weather events cause route disruptions. The agent has access to driver schedules, vehicle availability, and customer priority ratings. It operates without human approval for rescheduling decisions under a defined cost ceiling. During a major storm event, the agent's autonomous rescheduling generates 1,200 revised delivery orders in four hours — a task that would have required a 12-person operations team for eight hours. A post-event review finds three cases where the agent prioritized cost optimization over customer contractual commitments it had not been configured to recognize. The company implements a new policy: once per quarter, a human operations manager re-executes one week of historical decisions to maintain the team's capability to override the agent if needed.

### Pattern 4: The Hybrid Board

**Structure:** Agents and humans are formal co-participants in a decision-making body, with defined rights and responsibilities for each participant type. Agents have speaking rights (they can present analysis and positions) but voting rights are a design parameter that varies by context and jurisdiction. The body's rules specify how agent outputs are weighted, how conflicts between human and agent positions are resolved, and what classes of decision require human-only deliberation.

**Typical context:** Governance bodies for complex sociotechnical systems — standards organizations, platform governance boards, research consortia — where the information complexity exceeds what human participants alone can process in the time available for deliberation.

**Open source analogue:** Technical steering committees in large open source foundations (Apache Foundation, CNCF, Eclipse Foundation) — bodies with defined membership criteria, structured voting procedures, and documented accountability to a broader contributor community. The peer-reviewed gap-diagnosis is direct: Ait, Jouneaux, Cánovas Izquierdo, and Cabot (2025), in their ASE 2025 paper proposing a domain-specific language for human-AI agent collaboration governance, observe that as OSS stakeholder bases expand to include "AI-powered agents collaborating together" alongside human contributors, "explicit policies are often lacking or unclear" — motivating their DSL proposal precisely because no formalized voting/decision-right schema currently distinguishes agent participants from human ones at the policy level (Ait et al., 2025, *ASE 2025*, doi:10.1109/ASE63991.2025.00337). The Hybrid Board pattern, in other words, has neither a flagship empirical instantiation in a major foundation nor a standardized policy vocabulary; the latter is now under active proposal but unadopted.

**Failure modes:** Legitimacy deficit — human stakeholders may refuse to accept decisions made by bodies in which AI agents participated, regardless of the formal rules. Adversarial gaming — if agents' positions are predictable from their configuration, human participants may strategically position proposals to obtain favorable agent outputs. The empirical record from decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs), the closest extant approximation of a Hybrid Board with codified voting rules, is cautionary: across multiple peer-reviewed surveys, voting participation typically fluctuates around 30–40% measured by voting power and frequently below 10% measured by token-holder headcount, with delegation networks producing concentrations functionally indistinguishable from centralized governance (Faqir-Rhazoui et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2024, arXiv:2407.10945). The DAO experience suggests that formal co-participation rights are insufficient: without active legitimacy maintenance and transparent delegation rules, a Hybrid Board collapses into rule-by-the-attentive-minority — a failure mode the structural design alone does not preclude.

**Narrative vignette:** A national standards body establishes a technical working group on cybersecurity protocols. Two AI agents — one maintained by a university research group, one by a participating industry consortium — are formal members, responsible for reviewing proposed standards against published vulnerability databases and generating structured position papers. Human members vote; agent members do not. In one deliberation, the industry agent's position paper is later found to have been generated under a configuration that weighted industry adoption criteria over security robustness. The episode prompts the working group to adopt a configuration disclosure requirement for all agent participants.

## 3. Anti-patterns and failure modes

*(Connection note: the prompt-injection vulnerability class developed in `e-llm-structural-vulnerabilities.md` is not just a technical threat but an organizational anti-pattern amplifier. In Pattern 3 (Autonomous Steward) and Pattern 4 (Hybrid Board) designs, an injected instruction in an untrusted artefact can bypass deliberative safeguards, because the agent’s “inputs” include attacker-controlled text that is architecturally indistinguishable from operator instructions. This makes “content provenance” and “instruction/data separation” preconditions for delegating authority, not optional security hardening.)*

Three failure modes recur across pattern types and are sufficiently general to warrant separate treatment:

**The accountability gap.** In organizations with human-only participants, failures can be traced to individuals who bear professional, legal, or reputational responsibility. When agents participate in decisions, the accountability chain is diffuse: the agent's developer, the organization that deployed it, the human who approved the configuration, and the human who acted on its output may all share partial responsibility — without any single party bearing clear primary accountability (Matthias, 2004). This diffusion is not unique to AI agents (it parallels accountability problems in complex bureaucracies), but the technical opacity of agent reasoning makes it more severe (Diakopoulos, 2016). Governance designs that include agents must address accountability explicitly: who is responsible when an agent's output causes harm? Jafari Meimandi et al.'s (2025) ARGO study — an empirical assessment of responsible-AI governance across multiple business units of a multinational organization — corroborates this anti-pattern in the field: their identified pattern of "inconsistent accountability in risk oversight" emerged precisely where central governance principles were translated into locally autonomous AI deployments without harmonized monitoring infrastructure (Jafari Meimandi et al., 2025, arXiv:2510.03368).

**Counter-evidence: open organizational forms can amplify, not dampen, AI agent coordination failures.** The ARGO study above offers an early organizational field case study against the implicit prior that open or decentralized organizational templates straightforwardly *improve* hybrid intelligence outcomes. Across more than fifty business units operating with substantial autonomy, Jafari Meimandi et al. (2025) document four recurrent failure patterns: (1) a *principle–practice gap* between abstract responsible-AI commitments and operational implementation — a pattern independently documented in peer-reviewed work on organizational AI accountability (Raji et al., 2020); (2) *inconsistent application* across regional and functional divisions, eroding the standardization that Ostrom's design principle 4 (effective monitoring) requires; (3) *authority fragmentation* between centralized guidance and localized interpretation; and (4) the *accountability gaps* described above (Jafari Meimandi et al., 2025). These patterns are structurally analogous to — but *worse* than — the coordination failures Shah (2006) documented in human-only open-source projects, because the configuration heterogeneity of locally tuned agents introduces a dimension of variance that human contributors did not. The implication for the patterns above is that open-source organizational templates do not, by themselves, dominate closed alternatives once AI agents are participants; their advantage is contingent on the precondition infrastructure of Section 4 actually being met.

**Legitimacy collapse.** Organizations derive authority not only from formal rules but from participants' acceptance of those rules as legitimate (Suchman, 1995). Suchman distinguishes pragmatic legitimacy (based on self-interested calculation), moral legitimacy (normative approval), and cognitive legitimacy (taken-for-granted acceptance) — and notes that each type is both harder to construct and easier to lose than the others (Suchman, 1995). If key stakeholders — employees, clients, regulators, affected communities — do not regard decisions made with agent participation as legitimate, the formal authority of the decisions degrades regardless of their technical correctness. Legitimacy collapse is difficult to detect in advance and difficult to reverse once it occurs.

**The automation ratchet.** Once an agent is embedded in an organizational process and performs reliably, the organizational capacity to perform that process without the agent atrophies. Humans who previously executed the process lose the skill and the contextual knowledge required to do so (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997; Lee et al., 2025). If the agent fails — technically or normatively — the organization may be unable to revert to unaugmented operation. This dynamic is structural in open source as well (few organizations could rebuild Linux from scratch if the kernel community dissolved), but it is more consequential when the automated function involves judgment rather than merely computation.

## 4. Institutional preconditions

The patterns above are not universally applicable. Four institutional preconditions appear necessary for hybrid organizational designs to be viable:

1. **Auditable agent action logs.** The organization must be able to reconstruct, after the fact, what each agent did, why (in terms of its configured objectives), and what information it acted on. Without this, the accountability gap is irreparable. Per the sociomateriality framing introduced in Section 1 (Orlikowski, 2007), the design of the logging artefact is itself a governance act: a log that records only outcomes will materialize a different kind of accountability than one that records intermediate reasoning and rejected alternatives. Organizations should not treat audit infrastructure as a downstream technical implementation detail of governance — it *is* the governance. Ostrom's (1990) design principles for self-governing commons establish that monitoring and graduated sanctions are preconditions for any polycentric governance regime; applied to hybrid organizations, this means audit infrastructure is not optional for patterns beyond the Delegated Executor (Ostrom, 1990, Chapter 3). 

In practice, this requires adopting observability standards that capture the full decision chain. Jaeger, the open-source distributed tracing system now adopting OpenTelemetry as its core instrumentation layer, has emerged as a candidate infrastructure for this function in AI agent pipelines (CNCF, 2024). The distributed tracing approach — recording causally linked spans across system components — was established at scale by Sigelman et al. (2010) in Google's Dapper infrastructure and has since been standardised through the OpenTelemetry specification maintained by the Cloud Native Computing Foundation. OpenTelemetry/Jaeger allows reconstruction of multi-step agent call chains — which model was invoked, with what context, at what latency, with what output — providing the audit trail that Pattern 3 (Autonomous Steward) and Pattern 4 (Hybrid Board) governance requires. Because both Jaeger and OpenTelemetry are CNCF open-source projects maintained under permissive licenses, their adoption as audit infrastructure is consistent with the OSAID legibility argument in Section 5: the governance infrastructure itself is inspectable and forkable, not a proprietary black box controlled by the agent provider. Production observability tools for LLM agents — including LangSmith (LangChain, 2024), Arize Phoenix (Arize AI, 2024), and AgentOps (2024) — already capture I/O events and, in some configurations, intermediate reasoning steps at the application layer. The ReAct framework (Yao et al., 2023) demonstrated that structured reasoning traces can be interleaved with agent actions to produce interpretable logs at inference time without a dedicated tracing infrastructure, confirming that reasoning-stage capture is feasible in principle. The remaining empirical question is whether distributed tracing systems can sustain this reasoning-stage capture without incurring prohibitive performance penalties at the latency and scale requirements of production Pattern 3 and 4 deployments in high-consequence domains (e.g., medical decision support, procurement), and whether application-layer traces are granular and tamper-resistant enough to satisfy the formal accountability requirements of those domains.

2. **Defined configuration governance.** Agent behavior is a function of its configuration (training, fine-tuning, system prompts, access rights). Organizations must have governance processes for who can change configuration, how changes are approved and documented, and what the default configuration is. This includes a specific requirement for Pattern 3 and Pattern 4 deployments: instruction/content separation in the agent's input pipeline. Because prompt injection (see `e-llm-structural-vulnerabilities.md` Section 5) exploits the agent's inability to distinguish instructions from data, Pattern 3 (Autonomous Steward) and Pattern 4 (Hybrid Board) organizations cannot delegate authority over data-consuming operations without first implementing architectural safeguards that separate structured operator instructions from untrusted external content (Liu et al., 2025, arXiv:2509.22040).

3. **Human override at every pattern level.** Even in the Autonomous Steward pattern, humans must retain the technical and organizational capacity to override agent decisions and revert to unaugmented operation. This capacity must be exercised periodically to prevent the automation ratchet. Trist and Bamforth's (1951) classic sociomaterial study of coal mining demonstrated that when a technical subsystem is reorganized (replacing room-and-pillar with longwall production), the social subsystem must be redesigned correspondingly — failure to do so produces lower throughput, higher absenteeism, and psychological damage (Trist & Bamforth, 1951). Applied to agent governance, this means the organization cannot simply replace human decision-makers with agents while preserving the social coordination norms and skill-base that sustained the prior system. In practice, this requires designating humans as "shadow stewards" — individuals who regularly audit agent decisions, override them on a sampling basis, and maintain sufficient procedural knowledge to execute decisions manually if the agent fails (Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven, 2018). The frequency of override exercises depends on stakes: in high-consequence domains (medical decision support, criminal justice, financial trading), human override should be exercised continuously alongside agent operation; in medium-consequence operations (procurement, resource scheduling), quarterly sampling is a reasonable floor.

4. **Explicit legitimacy maintenance.** Organizations that include agent participants must actively maintain the acceptance of that participation by key stakeholder groups. This is not a one-time design decision but an ongoing governance task — more analogous to stakeholder relations than to technical configuration. Ostrom (1990) identifies this as the "recognition of rights to organize" principle: for governance to be sustainable, the broader community must explicitly acknowledge both the legitimacy of the governing authority and the rules it enforces (Ostrom, 1990). In hybrid organizations, legitimacy must be actively re-negotiated as agent capabilities change: a Pattern 2 (Augmented Deliberator) design that is legitimate when an agent provides written analysis may become illegitimate if the agent's outputs are later perceived as steering decisions rather than informing them. Conversely, a Pattern 3 (Autonomous Steward) deployment that begins with stakeholder acceptance may face a legitimacy crisis if prominent failures expose the automation as less reliable than originally believed. The governance task is therefore not "design legitimacy once" but "sustain legitimacy as capabilities evolve and as external perceptions of risk shift."

**Narrative vignette:** A regional environmental agency adopts an AI agent to triage permit applications, routing simple cases to immediate approval and flagging complex ones for human review. The public perceives the system as legitimate — reviews are published, the criteria are transparent, and initial outcomes are good. Two years later, an investigative journalist documents 15 cases in which the agent approved permits for projects in flood-prone areas that would have been flagged by a human reviewer, despite the criteria explicitly listing flood risk. The incident generates public pressure; environmental advocates demand transparency into the agent's training and decision logic. The agency discovers that the training data included several high-profile projects that proceeded despite flood-risk flags, inadvertently teaching the agent to weight other criteria (development benefit, local economic impact) more heavily than the explicit rules specified. The formal governance is unchanged, but legitimacy has collapsed: stakeholders now distrust both the agent and the agency that deployed it, even after the agency corrects the training data.

## 5. Connection to the open source organization thesis

The organizational patterns described above share a structural property with successful open source governance: they are *legible*. Participants — human and agent alike — can understand what the rules are, who has what authority, and how decisions are made. This legibility is not a coincidence. It is a design requirement for governance of systems whose complexity exceeds any single participant's comprehension.

The open source community discovered this empirically: projects that concentrated authority in opaque hierarchies were consistently less resilient than projects that encoded authority in legible, documented processes (Schweik & English, 2012). The Linux kernel's subsystem maintainer structure, the Apache Foundation's voting rules, and the IETF's RFC process are all legibility solutions — ways of making governance comprehensible to participants who cannot observe all of it at once.

Hybrid organizations that include AI agents face a higher version of this challenge: not only must the governance be legible to human participants, but the agents' own behavior must be legible — their configuration must be documented, their reasoning must be auditable, and their role boundaries must be precisely defined. The institutional templates described above are proposals for what that legibility might look like in practice.

The Open Source Initiative's *Open Source AI Definition v1.0* (OSI, 2024) makes the legibility requirement normative for AI systems claiming the "open source" label. To qualify, a system must grant the four classical freedoms — to use, study, modify, and share — and, critically, must release the *preferred form for making modifications*: the data information (provenance, selection methodology, labeling procedures), the complete training and inference code, and the model parameters under OSI-approved terms (OSI, 2024). The translation to organizational structure is direct: an organization that incorporates an AI agent into its governance cannot satisfy the analogue of the OSAID standard unless it discloses, to the relevant stakeholder community, the configuration that determines the agent's behavior. The four patterns above are compatible with OSAID-grade legibility to differing degrees: the Delegated Executor and Augmented Deliberator can satisfy it relatively cheaply because the agent's outputs pass through human filters; the Autonomous Steward and Hybrid Board require substantially more configuration governance to remain legible, because the agent's actions take effect without per-instance human mediation. OSAID does not yet specify governance procedures — it specifies the artifacts that must be available — but it establishes the floor below which "open source AI organization" becomes a marketing claim rather than a structural property.
