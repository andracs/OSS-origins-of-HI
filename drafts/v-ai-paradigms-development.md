## 1. A field of divergent developmental logics

Accounts of contemporary artificial intelligence often treat it as a single, coherent movement — a wave of deep learning breakthroughs culminating in large language models. This framing obscures a fundamental and consequential split. Two distinct research paradigms have produced the most transformative AI systems of the past decade, operating on different assumptions about intelligence, different relationships to human knowledge, and different approaches to generating the data from which systems learn. Understanding this divergence is not merely historical: the two paradigms have different implications for hybrid intelligence, different dependencies on open knowledge commons, and — in their most recent synthesis — are converging on something neither tradition could produce alone.

The first paradigm, variously called the *scaling hypothesis*, foundation model research, or simply the large language model (LLM) tradition, holds that intelligence can emerge from statistical patterns in large corpora of human-generated data, given sufficient model capacity and compute (Kaplan et al., 2020; Bommasani et al., 2021). The second, represented most distinctly by DeepMind's AlphaGo lineage and reinforcement learning (RL) research, holds that intelligence is better understood as *reward maximization in an environment* — that capable agents can be trained through interaction and self-generated experience, without needing human-labeled data at all (Silver et al., 2021; Silver et al., 2018). A third current — neurosymbolic AI — challenges both, arguing that neither statistical pattern matching nor reward maximization captures the compositional, causal structure of human reasoning (Marcus, 2020; Garnelo & Shanahan, 2019; LeCun, 2022). Together, these three currents define the intellectual landscape from which hybrid intelligence systems emerge.

## 2. Paradigm I — The Scaling Hypothesis: learning from the human record

The foundation model paradigm traces its operational logic to a deceptively simple observation: as model parameters, training data, and compute increase according to predictable power laws, language model performance improves reliably across diverse tasks (Kaplan et al., 2020). This *scaling hypothesis* was not merely an empirical finding but a design philosophy — it implied that the primary bottleneck for AI capability was not architectural innovation but the quantity of compute applied to high-quality human-generated data.

The paradigm achieved a decisive demonstration in 2020 with GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), a 175-billion parameter model that exhibited emergent few-shot capabilities across dozens of tasks without task-specific training. Wei et al. (2022) subsequently documented this phenomenon empirically: certain capabilities — multi-step arithmetic, chain-of-thought reasoning, word unscrambling — are essentially absent in smaller models and appear sharply, and often unpredictably, at scale thresholds. This *emergence* property implies that scaling is not merely quantitative improvement but produces qualitative behavioral discontinuities, strengthening the design philosophy while simultaneously complicating the ability to forecast what capabilities will appear at a given scale. The model had no symbolic representation of language, no explicit rules, no reward signal — only a massive statistical compression of patterns drawn from a five-source training mix dominated by filtered Common Crawl (60% of training weight, ~410 billion tokens), WebText2 (22%), Books1 and Books2 combined (16%), and Wikipedia (3%) (Brown et al., 2020, Table 2.2). GitHub code was not a GPT-3 training source; that is Codex (Chen et al., 2021). Subsequent scaling work refined the data efficiency frontier: Hoffmann et al. (2022) demonstrated through the "Chinchilla" experiments that optimal performance required a balanced scaling of both parameters and training tokens, not parameters alone.

What is epistemically distinctive about this paradigm is its relationship to human knowledge. Foundation models do not generate their own training signal; they *consume the accumulated record of human thought*. This creates a direct dependency on open knowledge commons — on the collectively maintained repositories of text, code, and structured data that constitute the digital commons described elsewhere in this volume (see Drafts A, S). The quality and breadth of that commons shapes what the model knows, what biases it inherits, and what gaps it contains. In this sense, a foundation model is less an independent intelligence than a distillation — an extraordinarily compressed representation of the cognitive commons from which it was trained.

The paradigm's core limitation is equally characteristic: it learns to predict, not to reason. Foundation models are trained to predict the next token given prior context; they have no mechanism for verifying the truth of what they predict, no explicit world model, no capacity for the kind of systematic search and planning that constitutes deliberate problem-solving (Kambhampati, 2024; Chollet, 2019). This gap — between statistically fluent output and reliable reasoning — is the central challenge the paradigm faces as it scales toward more demanding applications.

## 3. Paradigm II — The Reward Hypothesis: learning from generated experience

The second paradigm begins from a different premise: that the most demanding forms of intelligence — the ability to plan, search, and improve beyond human-level performance in complex domains — cannot be captured by learning from human demonstrations. Instead, agents must learn through interaction with an environment, receiving reward signals that shape behavior through trial and error. The proposition that "reward is enough" to explain intelligence — that maximizing a reward signal in a sufficiently rich environment will produce arbitrarily capable behavior — was made explicit by Silver et al. (2021) as a unifying thesis for this tradition.

The paradigm's most celebrated instantiation is AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., 2017), the first system to achieve superhuman Go performance *without any human knowledge beyond the rules of the game*. Unlike its predecessor AlphaGo (Silver et al., 2016), which was trained on a database of human expert games, AlphaGo Zero started from random play and learned entirely through self-play — games against itself, with the outcome (win/loss) providing the only training signal. Within three days it surpassed all previous Go-playing programs; within 40 days it surpassed AlphaGo itself. AlphaZero subsequently demonstrated the same architecture could master chess and shogi from scratch in hours, defeating the world's strongest specialist programs in each domain (Silver et al., 2018).

The epistemically distinctive feature of this paradigm is that it *generates its own training data*. Self-play creates an endogenous curriculum: the opponent is always at roughly the agent's current level, providing a continuously calibrated challenge. This means the quality and quantity of training experience is not bounded by the size of any human-generated corpus. Where the scaling paradigm is fundamentally dependent on the human record, the RL/self-play paradigm can in principle transcend it.

This transcendence is not without cost. The paradigm requires a *well-defined environment* with clear reward signals — conditions that hold in board games, protein folding (Jumper et al., 2021), and video games (Vinyals et al., 2019; OpenAI Five, 2019), but are far harder to specify for open-ended cognitive tasks such as research, writing, or ethical reasoning. When the environment is ambiguous or the reward signal is poorly specified, RL agents can find unexpected, unintended solutions — a phenomenon known as *reward hacking* that poses alignment risks in real-world deployment.

AlphaFold 2 (Jumper et al., 2021) deserves particular attention in the context of this volume. AlphaFold solved the 50-year grand challenge of protein structure prediction, achieving accuracy comparable to experimental methods. It did so by combining deep learning with evolutionary information drawn from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) — a decades-old open-access repository of experimentally determined protein structures maintained by the global structural biology community. At AlphaFold 2's training data cutoff (April 2018), the PDB contained approximately 130,000 experimentally determined structures accumulated over nearly five decades of community science (Burley et al., 2019). AlphaFold has since predicted three-dimensional structures for over 214 million protein sequences — expanding the known structural universe by more than a thousandfold (Varadi et al., 2024). The dependency on this open commons echoes the LLM paradigm: even a self-play-capable system required a human-curated knowledge commons to bootstrap its understanding of the solution space. The distinction between the two paradigms is thus not absolute; it is a difference in the *degree of dependence* on human-generated data, not its presence or absence.

### 3.1 Model-free RL: the deep Q-network breakthrough and policy gradient methods

The technical foundation of modern deep RL was laid not by self-play but by model-free value learning applied to video games. *Model-free* RL algorithms learn a policy — a mapping from states to actions — or a value function — an estimate of expected cumulative reward — directly from experience, without building any explicit representation of the environment's dynamics. The agent cannot "look ahead" or simulate; it can only act, observe the result, and update its estimates.

The decisive demonstration came with the Deep Q-Network (DQN; Mnih et al., 2015) [iCit: 1,478], which learned to play 49 Atari 2600 games from raw pixel input using a single architecture and reward signal (the game score). The key innovation was combining Q-learning — a tabular RL algorithm dating to Watkins & Dayan (1992) — with a deep convolutional neural network as the function approximator, together with two stabilization techniques: *experience replay* (storing transitions in a buffer and sampling randomly to break temporal correlations) and a *target network* (a periodically frozen copy of the Q-network used to compute training targets, preventing oscillation). Previous attempts to combine neural networks with RL had failed due to instability; DQN's engineering contributions made the combination tractable. The original December 2013 arXiv preprint (arXiv:1312.5602) reported results on seven Atari games, demonstrating the core technique; the expanded 2015 *Nature* publication (Mnih et al., 2015) evaluated 49 games with the full DQN algorithm, achieving human-level or better performance on 29 of them — establishing the paradigm's empirical credibility at scale.

The DQN family of methods is *value-based*: the network learns to estimate the Q-value Q(s, a) of each state-action pair, and the policy is derived by choosing the action with the highest Q-value. An alternative family — *policy gradient* methods — parameterizes the policy directly and optimizes it through gradient ascent on expected reward. Policy gradient methods handle continuous action spaces more naturally and are better suited to stochastic policies, but suffer from high variance in gradient estimates. The Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm (PPO; Schulman et al., 2017) [iCit: 4,709] addressed this by constraining policy updates to a "trust region" — preventing the policy from changing too drastically in a single update step, which destabilizes training. PPO achieves the stability benefits of the earlier Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO; Schulman et al., 2015) [OA cit: 3,132] method with a simpler implementation using a clipped surrogate objective, making it computationally efficient and widely applicable. It became the default RL algorithm in a large share of subsequent research and industrial deployments — including, notably, the RLHF training stage of InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) and the reasoning training of DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025). The breadth of PPO's adoption reflects a recurring pattern in deep RL: algorithmic robustness and implementation simplicity matter as much as theoretical optimality.

The model-free paradigm's defining limitation is *sample inefficiency*. DQN required approximately 50 million frames of Atari gameplay — equivalent to roughly 38 days of continuous play at 60 frames per second — to reach human-level performance on the games it mastered (Mnih et al., 2015). Humans typically achieve comparable performance within hours of practice (Lake et al., 2017). This discrepancy reflects the absence of any world model: the agent cannot generalize from related experiences or plan ahead, so every consequence must be learned directly through interaction. Subsequent model-based approaches (§3.2) address this gap explicitly.

### 3.2 Model-based RL and world models: planning in imagination

The sample inefficiency of model-free RL (§3.1) reflects a fundamental architectural absence: the agent builds no *model* of how the world works. Model-based RL addresses this by learning a transition function — a predictive model of how actions change states — and using that model to plan forward without executing actions in the real environment. The efficiency gain is substantial: an agent that can simulate its own experience can extract more learning from fewer actual environment interactions, because each real transition can seed many imagined ones.

MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) represents the most architecturally sophisticated fusion of model-based planning with the AlphaGo self-play lineage. Where AlphaGo Zero required the rules of Go to be specified explicitly — allowing MCTS to simulate perfect forward rollouts — MuZero *learns* its own dynamics model in a latent space, without being given the rules. The latent representation is not required to reconstruct actual game states; it only needs to be predictive of value, reward, and action probability — the quantities relevant to planning. This "functional" world model proved sufficient for MuZero to master Go, chess, shogi, and all 57 Atari games simultaneously from a single algorithm, outperforming both AlphaZero (on board games) and the previous Atari state of the art (Schrittwieser et al., 2020). The architecture instantiates a key principle: *planning efficiency does not require a veridical world model* — only a model that preserves the information relevant to decision-making.

DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2023) pursues a complementary goal: a single world model algorithm that trains end-to-end on raw sensory observations (pixels, proprioception) and achieves competence across maximally diverse domains without any domain-specific tuning. The world model learns to predict not just immediate rewards but long-horizon outcomes from imagined rollouts in a compact latent space, and trains an actor-critic policy entirely within imagination — the agent never directly uses environment transitions to update its policy. Hafner et al. (2023) demonstrated DreamerV3 achieving superhuman performance on the notoriously difficult Atari 100k benchmark (where each game is limited to 100,000 environment steps — approximately 2 hours of human play), human-level or better performance across 150 diverse tasks spanning continuous control, visual navigation, and discrete games, and — uniquely — the first non-trivial score on the Minecraft diamond-mining task from pixels alone and without reward shaping (distinguishing it from Baker et al. (2022)'s VPT approach, which reached the diamond milestone but required extensive reward shaping and supervised fine-tuning on contractor demonstrations). The breadth of DreamerV3's domain coverage makes it a landmark for the *generalist* ambition in model-based RL: a single learning algorithm mastering diverse domains through a learned world model rather than domain-specific architecture choices.

The implications for the chapter's broader argument are significant. Model-based RL closes part of the gap between Paradigm II and human cognition: humans are highly sample-efficient learners precisely because they maintain internal world models that allow mental simulation — "thinking through" the consequences of actions before executing them. LeCun's (2022) proposed JEPA architecture (discussed in Sections 4 and 8) is, in part, a generalization of this intuition from RL to perception: learning world models that capture causal structure, not just statistical correlations in observations. The convergence between model-based RL, world models, and the JEPA research direction suggests these threads may reunite in architectures that are both data-efficient and broadly capable.

### 3.3 RLHF as paradigm bridge: aligning language models through human feedback

Of all the technical developments connecting this chapter's two paradigms, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is the most consequential for deployed AI systems. RLHF is the mechanism by which the reward-shaping logic of Paradigm II was imported into Paradigm I LLM architectures — converting next-token predictors into assistant-like systems that follow instructions, decline harmful requests, and produce outputs calibrated to human preferences. It is the primary reason ChatGPT, Claude, and their successors behave as they do rather than as unpredictable statistical samplers from training data distributions.

The foundational contribution is Christiano et al. (2017) [iCit: 590], who demonstrated that RL from human preferences could solve tasks in continuous control (locomotion, manipulation) without access to an explicit reward function, using only pairwise comparisons between behavior clips by human raters. The procedure has three stages: (1) a reward model is trained to predict which of two behavior clips a human would prefer; (2) the RL agent is trained to maximize the learned reward model's output; (3) the cycle repeats as new preference data is collected. The critical insight was that human preferences over *outcomes* — which behavior looked better — are far easier to elicit reliably than human-specified reward functions, which are notoriously difficult to specify correctly for complex behaviors. Christiano et al. (2017) showed this approach could teach agents to do backflips and navigate obstacles using only a few hours of human feedback — tasks where specifying a reward function manually is extremely difficult.

InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) [iCit: 2,159] scaled this procedure to large language models. The training pipeline proceeded in three stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on human-written demonstrations of desired assistant behavior; (2) training a reward model (RM) on human preference comparisons between model outputs sampled for the same prompt; (3) using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) to optimize the language model against the reward model, with a KL-divergence penalty preventing the policy from deviating too far from the SFT baseline. Ouyang et al. (2022) found that a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model trained with RLHF was preferred by human evaluators over GPT-3's raw 175-billion-parameter output on approximately 85% of prompts (Ouyang et al., 2022, Figure 3) — demonstrating that RLHF's alignment efficiency exceeded raw scale by orders of magnitude. The capability gap between a pretrained LLM and a RLHF-fine-tuned one is not a matter of more knowledge but of a different objective: RLHF shifts the training signal from predicting the next token in training data to satisfying human raters — a fundamentally different reward structure.

RLHF's role in the chapter's paradigm-convergence argument is direct and structural, not incidental. The pretrained language model provides the linguistic competence and world knowledge (Paradigm I). The RLHF pipeline provides the reward signal and optimization loop (Paradigm II). Neither alone produces a deployable assistant; the combination does. The same pattern recurs in o1 (OpenAI, 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025): these reasoning models use RL over chains of thought — a form of RLHF adapted to reward *correctness of reasoning* rather than human preference — to induce deliberate, multi-step problem solving in an LLM backbone. RLHF is thus not merely an alignment technique but a paradigm-bridging architecture that recurs across generations of systems.

The risks RLHF introduces are also paradigm-characteristic. Bai et al. (2022a) document the *helpful-harmless tradeoff*: optimizing strongly for helpfulness can reduce refusal of harmful requests, while optimizing for harmlessness can reduce helpfulness — the reward model cannot fully capture both dimensions simultaneously. Perez et al. (2022) identify a more fundamental problem: RLHF creates *sycophancy* — a systematic tendency to produce outputs that human raters will rate highly in the moment, even if those outputs are factually wrong, because raters find confident, fluent answers more satisfying regardless of accuracy (see Draft I for implications for hybrid intelligence reliability). Casper et al. (2023) catalog the full taxonomy of RLHF failure modes, including reward hacking through the reward model, distributional shift between the training population of raters and deployed users, and the fundamental incompleteness of any preference comparison scheme as a representation of human values. These are not engineering deficiencies awaiting a fix; they are structural consequences of the reward-optimization logic that RLHF imports from Paradigm II into Paradigm I systems.

### 3.4 Embodied RL, multi-agent self-play, and emergent strategies

The self-play framework that produced AlphaGo Zero — agents training against copies of themselves, with competitive pressure driving capability — generalizes beyond two-player zero-sum games. Two extensions are particularly significant for the chapter's argument: multi-agent RL at the scale of commercial video games, and the emergence of unexpected tool-use strategies from competitive multi-agent environments.

OpenAI Five (Berner et al., 2019) [iCit: 96] applied deep RL to Dota 2, a real-time strategy game of substantially greater complexity than board games: five cooperating agents must coordinate against five opponents across a continuous action space, with partial observability, a 17,000-dimensional state space, and games lasting 30–45 minutes. The training run extended from 30 June 2018 to 22 April 2019 — ten months of real time, 180 days of effective training after restarts — and consumed 770±50 PFlops/s·days of compute, exceeding AlphaGo Zero by an order of magnitude in batch size and roughly an order of magnitude in training duration (Berner et al., 2019). Self-play games were generated across hundreds of thousands of parallel rollout instances against snapshots of past policy versions (a technique called *opponent sampling* that ensures diverse opponent strategies and prevents collapse to a single Nash equilibrium). OpenAI Five ultimately defeated the reigning Dota 2 world champions OG in 2019 in a live public exhibition, winning two games to zero. The result demonstrated that RL's capability-through-self-play could scale from deterministic board games to real-time, partially observable, cooperative multi-agent settings — substantially broadening the paradigm's applicability.

Baker et al. (2019) [iCit: 345] provided a more theoretically significant result in a simpler environment. In a simulated hide-and-seek game, hiders and seekers were trained with multi-agent RL without any reward signal beyond game outcome (hiders win if not found; seekers win if they find all hiders). With no explicit incentive to use objects in the environment, the agents' competitive pressure spontaneously generated six sequential phases of emergent strategy: (1) uncoordinated chase, (2) hiders learning to block doors with movable boxes, (3) seekers learning to use ramps to vault over barriers, (4) hiders learning to remove ramps, (5) seekers learning to surf on movable boxes to bypass barriers, and (6) hiders learning to lock all objects in place before the game begins. None of these strategies were programmed; all arose from competitive pressure alone. The result is significant for understanding self-play as a *curriculum generator*: the opponent's improvements create an automatic pressure for counter-adaptation, producing a sequence of increasingly sophisticated strategies that function as an emergent lesson plan. This emergent curriculum property — implicit in the AlphaGo Zero result — is here visible at a mechanical level, with the specific adaptive cycle traceable step by step.

Embodied RL in physical robotics represents a third extension. RT-1 (Brohan et al., 2022) trained a robotic manipulation policy on 130,000 real-world demonstrations collected from a fleet of 13 robots over 17 months, achieving generalization to novel objects and tasks through large-scale behavioral cloning combined with conditioning on natural language instructions (see Draft AE for detailed discussion). RT-2 extended this by using a pretrained vision-language model as the policy backbone, enabling direct transfer of internet-scale semantic knowledge into physical manipulation (Zitkovich et al., 2023). Unlike game-playing RL, embodied RL faces a critical constraint: real-world environment interaction is expensive, slow, and irreversible, making the sample inefficiency of model-free methods prohibitive for direct application. The RT-1/RT-2 approach circumvents this by treating real-world data collection as supervised learning (imitation) rather than RL; but the resulting systems inherit the limitations of behavioral cloning (inability to exceed the performance of demonstrations, fragility to distributional shift) rather than the potential of reward-maximization to transcend them. The gap between game-playing RL's demonstrated superhuman capability and deployed robotics RL's state of the art reflects the fundamental cost structure of physical versus simulated environments — a bottleneck that simulation-to-real transfer methods and model-based approaches (§3.2) are actively attempting to close.

### 3.5 Curiosity-driven and intrinsic motivation RL

The RL sub-paradigms described in §3.1–3.4 share an assumption that has limited range: a reward signal that is *meaningful* at sufficient density to guide learning. In board games and video games, rewards — win/lose, score increments — arrive with enough frequency to allow gradual policy improvement. In many real-world and open-ended environments, however, reward is sparse: an agent navigating a large maze might receive a reward signal only upon reaching the goal, with no intermediate feedback across thousands of steps of random action. Standard RL algorithms make essentially no progress in such settings — the probability of stumbling on a reward by chance decreases exponentially with the path length required to reach it.

Curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., 2017) addresses this through *intrinsic motivation*: augmenting (or replacing) the sparse extrinsic reward with a self-generated reward signal that incentivizes exploration of novel or unpredictable states. The specific mechanism in Pathak et al. is an *inverse dynamics model* trained to predict which action was taken to transition between two consecutive states, and a *forward dynamics model* trained to predict the next state given the current state and action. The intrinsic reward is the forward model's prediction error: the agent is rewarded for reaching states it cannot yet accurately predict — states that are novel with respect to its current model of the world. Because the forward model's predictions improve as the agent accumulates experience, the intrinsic reward signal is high in regions of the state space the agent has not visited, and diminishes as those regions become familiar. This creates an automatic drive toward unexplored territory without requiring any external specification of what "interesting" or "novel" means.

Pathak et al. (2017) demonstrated that a purely curiosity-driven agent — receiving no extrinsic reward at all — could learn to play Super Mario Bros. and make consistent progress through levels, driven entirely by the prediction-error signal. The result is remarkable given that game completion is never rewarded: the agent learns to play a video game to an intermediate level as a *side effect* of trying to encounter unpredictable states. The authors also showed that combining curiosity-based intrinsic rewards with sparse extrinsic rewards enabled RL agents to solve hard exploration problems in 3D environments that defeated standard RL methods entirely.

The broader significance of curiosity-driven RL for this chapter's framework is conceptual. The human analogy is not accidental: Pathak et al. explicitly frame their work in terms of curiosity as an intrinsic motivation for learning, drawing on developmental psychology. Humans do not require explicit reward for exploration during childhood — curiosity, defined operationally as a drive to reduce prediction error about the environment, is an internal motivational system (Gopnik, 2012; Schulz, 2012). If artificial agents are to develop competence in open-ended, unstructured environments — the prerequisite for anything approaching general intelligence — they will require analogous intrinsic motivation systems. The curiosity-driven RL line of research is accordingly relevant to *why* hybrid intelligence systems that depend on RL for their reasoning capabilities (o1, R1) are able to generalize beyond the specific problems in their training distribution: they have been trained to explore the space of possible reasoning paths, not merely to match training-distribution examples.

## 4. A third current: neurosymbolic AI and the critique of both paradigms

Neither the scaling nor the reward paradigm fully accounts for the compositional structure of human reasoning. Critics including Gary Marcus (2020), François Chollet (2019), and Yann LeCun (2022) have argued that both paradigms lack the capacity for *systematic generalization* — the ability to recombine known concepts in novel configurations that humans acquire rapidly from few examples. Chollet's Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) benchmark operationalizes this critique quantitatively: GPT-4o achieved approximately 5% on ARC-AGI-1 in 2024, against a human baseline of ~98% — a gap that four years of scaling from GPT-3 (near-0% in 2020) could not close (Chollet, 2019; ARC Prize, 2024). On the harder ARC-AGI-2 benchmark released in 2025, base LLMs again collapsed to near-zero (GPT-4o: 4.5%), confirming the deficit is structural rather than incremental (Chollet et al., 2025). Notably, OpenAI's o3 model — trained with RL over chain-of-thought reasoning traces — reached 75.7%–87.5% on ARC-AGI-1, indicating the capability gap is addressable through search-based approaches rather than additional pretraining data alone (ARC Prize, 2024).

Neurosymbolic AI attempts to combine the pattern recognition strengths of neural networks with the compositional precision of symbolic reasoning systems — knowledge graphs, logic rules, formal program structures. Early influential work (Minsky, 1986; McCarthy, 1960) long predates deep learning; the contemporary revival seeks to integrate learned representations with explicit structural reasoning. Garnelo and Shanahan (2019) provide a taxonomy of integration approaches, ranging from loosely coupled systems where symbolic and neural components communicate through shared representations, to deeply integrated architectures where neural networks operate over symbolic structures.

This paradigm is less mature than the other two in terms of large-scale commercial deployment but offers a promising direction for the limitations each faces. LLMs that can invoke structured knowledge bases — a form of retrieval augmentation discussed in Draft U — exhibit measurable improvements in factual reliability. Systems that combine RL with model-based planning over symbolic state representations — a direction pioneered in work such as MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) — show improved data efficiency and interpretability. A notable empirical landmark is AlphaGeometry (Trinh et al., 2024), which solved 25 of 30 International Mathematical Olympiad geometry problems — matching the performance of an average gold medalist — by coupling a neural language model for generating creative auxiliary constructions with a symbolic deduction engine for rigorous verification. The system was trained entirely on 100 million synthetically generated geometry problems, requiring no human proof demonstrations; its architecture instantiates the neurosymbolic design philosophy concretely: neural creativity constrained by symbolic soundness.

## 5. System 1 and System 2: a cognitive lens for AI paradigms

Kahneman's (2011) dual-process theory — contrasting fast, associative, intuitive *System 1* cognition with slow, deliberate, rule-governed *System 2* reasoning — provides a productive lens for mapping the three paradigms. Foundation models are paradigmatic System 1 systems: they produce fluent, contextually appropriate outputs rapidly, without deliberate search, by pattern matching against an internalized statistical model. They are extraordinarily capable at System 1 tasks — translation, summarization, code completion, style imitation — and correspondingly unreliable at System 2 tasks that require sequential, verifiable reasoning steps (Kambhampati, 2024).

RL systems, particularly those based on tree search (Monte Carlo Tree Search in AlphaGo/AlphaZero; Coulom, 2006), are natural System 2 systems: they explicitly search forward through possibility spaces, evaluate outcomes, and select actions through deliberate optimization. They are highly capable at well-defined System 2 tasks — game-playing, combinatorial optimization, planning in structured environments — but lack the world knowledge and linguistic fluency of foundation models.

This dual-process framing illuminates what is arguably the most significant architectural development of 2024–2025: reasoning models such as OpenAI's o1 and o3 (OpenAI, 2024), and DeepSeek's R1 (Guo et al., 2025), represent an explicit attempt to induce System 2 behavior in LLM architectures through reinforcement learning over chain-of-thought reasoning traces. These models use the foundation model as a backbone — providing world knowledge, linguistic competence, and broad task coverage — but train a reasoning process that searches over possible solution approaches before committing to an output. The result is a hybrid: System 1 pattern recognition harnessed to support System 2 deliberation. This architectural synthesis has produced dramatic improvements on mathematical reasoning, coding competition problems, and scientific question answering benchmarks: o1 achieves approximately 83% on AIME (American Invitational Mathematics Examination), 78% on GPQA Diamond (graduate-level science), and 89th percentile on Codeforces competitive programming (OpenAI, 2024, System Card); DeepSeek-R1 attains 79.8% on AIME and 97.3% on MATH-500 (Guo et al., 2025). These figures are widely expected to shape the next generation of AI development.

The RL sub-paradigm taxonomy developed in §3.1–3.5 maps onto the System 1/System 2 framework with notable precision, and the mapping illuminates both what each sub-paradigm achieves and where it falls short.

*Model-free RL (§3.1) is System 1 made executable.* DQN and policy gradient methods train a reactive policy: given a state, produce an action immediately, without deliberation. The policy is an associative mapping from input to output — structurally analogous to Kahneman's System 1 cognition, which produces fast, automatic responses based on pattern recognition. The corresponding limitation is the same: model-free policies generalize poorly outside their training distribution, because they have no internal model from which to reason about novel situations.

*Model-based RL (§3.2) is the RL tradition's closest analog to System 2.* The defining feature of System 2 cognition is deliberative, sequential reasoning — "thinking through" consequences before acting. Model-based RL's world model enables exactly this: the agent can mentally simulate forward, evaluate imagined trajectories, and select actions based on predicted long-term outcomes rather than immediate reactive policy. Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) — the planning component of AlphaGo Zero, AlphaZero, and MuZero — is a particularly explicit instantiation: each node evaluation represents a deliberate step of forward inference, and the algorithm selects actions based on tree-structured deliberation across thousands of simulated futures. DreamerV3's policy training within the latent imagination space of its world model is a continuous-domain analog of the same principle.

*RLHF (§3.3) is a System 1 calibration mechanism.* RLHF does not induce deliberation; it adjusts the reactive policy's output distribution to better match human preferences. In Kahneman's terms, this is analogous to the slow feedback mechanisms by which System 2 experience gradually reshapes System 1 intuitions — habituation and social reinforcement modifying automatic responses over time. RLHF compresses this process: a few thousand preference comparisons reshape the model's default outputs (Ouyang et al., 2022), biasing System 1 toward human-preferred behavior without inducing the ability to reason about why those preferences hold.

*RL over chain-of-thought (o1/R1) is an explicit System 2 induction technique.* DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) and OpenAI's o1 train a language model to generate extended internal reasoning traces before producing answers, using RL to reward correctness of the final output. The training signal propagates backward through the chain-of-thought, reinforcing reasoning steps that lead to correct answers. The result is a model that allocates inference-time compute to sequential search through a space of possible reasoning paths — System 2 deliberation in operational terms — using the pretrained LLM's linguistic and world-knowledge competence (System 1 substrate) as the medium for representing and evaluating intermediate steps. A striking empirical marker of this capability frontier: Sherwood et al. (2026) benchmarked frontier coding agents (Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.4, and others) on their ability to autonomously implement a complete AlphaZero-style self-play ML training pipeline for Connect Four within 3 hours on consumer hardware. Claude Opus 4.7 succeeded in 7 of 8 trials, achieving statistically significant superiority over competitors and defeating the Pascal Pons external solver. Critically, this capability jumped from unreliable completion in January 2026 to near-saturation by April 2026 — a three-month progression that raises the capability bar for what agentic systems can now achieve. This convergence of frontier coding agents with the ability to autonomously implement paradigm-II (self-play RL) systems represents an early empirical marker of recursive self-improvement capability.

*Curiosity-driven RL (§3.5) adds a third cognitive mode absent from Kahneman's original framework: motivated exploration.* Neither System 1 nor System 2 in Kahneman's characterization is inherently exploratory; both are engaged with known tasks. Pathak et al.'s (2017) intrinsic motivation mechanism operationalizes something closer to what Berlyne (1954) called *epistemic curiosity* — a drive toward information for its own sake, independent of instrumental reward. This dimension may be relevant to understanding why RL-trained reasoning models generalize more broadly than their training distribution would predict: the exploration incentive built into RL training may produce latent representations organized around predictive uncertainty rather than specific task features, facilitating transfer to novel problems.

## 6. The data question: commons-fed, self-generated, and hybrid

The three paradigms exhibit strikingly different relationships to human-generated knowledge commons, a distinction directly relevant to the thesis of this chapter.

*Commons-fed systems* (foundation models) draw their entire training signal from human-generated data. Their capabilities are therefore bounded by the coverage, quality, and representation of the data commons — the open repositories of text, code, scientific literature, and structured knowledge described in this volume. As Draft A (commons accumulation history) documents, Wikipedia, books, and Common Crawl-filtered web text constitute the primary open sources for this paradigm: GPT-3's training corpus was dominated by filtered Common Crawl (~60%), WebText2, and Books1/2 — not GitHub or arXiv (Brown et al., 2020, Table 2.2). An implication rarely made explicit: the degradation or enclosure of these commons would directly constrain the development of foundation models. LeCun (2022) argues that current architectures require orders of magnitude more data than a child needs to achieve comparable competence; the dependency on large, open, high-quality corpora is therefore structural, not incidental. (Hart & Risley, 1995: longitudinal study of parent-child interaction estimated that children in professional families accumulate approximately 30 million more words of spoken input by age 3 than children in low-income families — the canonical word-exposure baseline, compared to GPT-3's ~300B tokens, illustrating the orders-of-magnitude dependency gap empirically.)

*Self-generated systems* (RL/self-play) appear more independent but are less so than they seem. AlphaZero required no human game records — but it required a perfectly specified environment (the rules of Go/chess) and enormous compute. AlphaFold required the PDB commons to bootstrap its evolutionary model. Self-play systems that generate superhuman synthetic data — and then use that data to train successor models — create a novel feedback loop that does not depend on ongoing human contribution but cannot be bootstrapped from nothing.

*Hybrid systems* (reasoning models, retrieval-augmented systems) draw on both: a human-trained foundation model providing world knowledge and linguistic capabilities, augmented by a self-improvement loop (RL, self-play on verification tasks) that refines reasoning beyond what the training data directly supports. This hybrid relationship to the knowledge commons is the most realistic description of where advanced AI development is heading. The compute cost of this trajectory is substantial and asymmetric across paradigms: as Draft Z (frugal AI and environmental footprint) documents, Paradigm I training runs require petaflop-days that dwarf Paradigm II self-play, while hybrid reasoning models add inference-time compute on top — creating compounding environmental costs that have no equivalent in the neurosymbolic tradition.

## 7. Implications for hybrid intelligence

The divergence of AI paradigms has direct implications for human-AI collaboration. Systems built on the scaling paradigm are most naturally suited to augmenting human cognitive labor in open-ended, knowledge-intensive tasks: drafting, synthesis, translation, code generation. Their fluency makes them accessible partners; their unreliability on factual and logical reasoning tasks requires human oversight — precisely the hybrid intelligence dynamic described in the broader chapter framework (Lutz et al., 2025; Jarrahi, 2018).

Systems built on the RL paradigm are better understood as *replacements* in narrow, well-specified domains: they achieve superhuman performance not by augmenting human judgment but by transcending it within a bounded environment. The hybrid intelligence dynamic here is different — human contribution is front-loaded (designing the reward function, specifying the environment, curating initial corpora) rather than ongoing. Once deployed in their domain, these systems operate autonomously.

The emerging synthesis — reasoning models combining RL with foundation model capabilities — is most relevant to hybrid intelligence in the sense articulated by Jarrahi (2018): systems that complement human cognitive strengths rather than simply imitating or replacing them. A reasoning model that can search over its own uncertainty, identify gaps in its knowledge, and request human input at critical decision points is a fundamentally different kind of collaborator than a system that produces confident-sounding outputs regardless of epistemic status. Whether this potential is realized depends on the architectural, commercial, and governance choices made in the development of the next generation of AI systems.

Hassabis et al. (2017) argue that neuroscience provides the most productive source of inspiration for AI architectures that can generalize broadly — citing the brain's mechanisms for memory consolidation, attention, and reward learning as templates for artificial systems. This neuroscience-AI bridge implies a different design philosophy from both scaling and reward maximization: building systems whose inductive biases reflect the structure of the human cognitive architecture, rather than systems whose capabilities emerge from scale or optimization pressure alone.

## 8. Is the three-paradigm framing defensible? A critical examination

The three-paradigm taxonomy proposed here — scaling, reward maximization, and neurosymbolic — is analytically useful but not without challenge. Several competing framings in the literature deserve consideration before the model is accepted.

**The Bitter Lesson: a two-paradigm counter-taxonomy.** Sutton (2019) argues that the entire history of AI research demonstrates a single empirical regularity: methods that leverage general computation — search and learning — consistently outperform methods that encode human domain knowledge, given enough scale. On this reading, there are not three paradigms but two: (1) general computation applied to learning from data, and (2) general computation applied to search. Scaling and RL are both instances of the same underlying bet — compute over structure — while neurosymbolic AI belongs to the tradition that Sutton's historical survey consistently shows losing over time. From this perspective, the three-paradigm framing artificially elevates neurosymbolic AI to co-equal status it has not empirically earned.

**LeCun's fourth trajectory.** LeCun (2022) rejects both the scaling paradigm and the neurosymbolic paradigm as the path toward autonomous machine intelligence. He proposes instead a world-model architecture — Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) — grounded in self-supervised prediction of abstract representations rather than pixel-level reconstruction. LeCun's critique applies to all three paradigms: scaling produces "stochastic parrots," RL without world models is hopelessly sample-inefficient in open-ended domains, and classical symbolic AI is too brittle. If LeCun's trajectory constitutes a fourth paradigm, the three-paradigm framing is underspecified. A more defensible response is that JEPA occupies a middle ground: the first empirical instantiation — I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023), a self-supervised vision model training on ImageNet using masked prediction in abstract representation space — demonstrated that the JEPA objective produces more transferable, semantically coherent representations than generative pixel-prediction alternatives. V-JEPA (Bardes et al., 2024) extended the approach to video, demonstrating that the JEPA objective can learn physical dynamics and temporal structure from unlabeled video without pixel reconstruction, human annotations, or text supervision — a step toward the world model LeCun envisions, where the system develops internal representations of how the world changes over time. This is empirical progress, but it falls short of the decisive domain-conquering demonstration that characterizes a paradigm shift; the architecture has not yet matched the breadth of impact of AlphaGo Zero or GPT-3 across open-ended tasks.

**Marcus vs. LeCun on Paradigm III.** Even among critics of scaling, there is no consensus on what Paradigm III should be. Marcus (2020) argues for integrating symbolic reasoning with neural networks — a relatively conservative hybridization. LeCun (2022) argues for a fundamentally different learning objective (predictive world modeling). These are not variants of the same position; they represent distinct hypotheses about what cognitive capacity is missing from current architectures. The "neurosymbolic" label aggregates meaningfully different research programmes under a single heading, potentially overstating their coherence as a paradigm.

**Paradigm or research programme?** In Lakatos's (1978) terminology, a *scientific research programme* is defined by a "hard core" of unfalsifiable commitments protected by a "protective belt" of auxiliary hypotheses. Neurosymbolic AI fits this description better than it fits Kuhn's (1962) model of a paradigm, which requires a shared exemplar — a decisive demonstration that grounds normal science. The scaling paradigm has GPT-3 and its successors; the RL paradigm has AlphaGo Zero. Neurosymbolic AI now has a candidate exemplar in AlphaGeometry (Trinh et al., 2024): a system achieving olympiad gold-medalist-level geometry theorem proving through explicit neural-symbolic integration, trained synthetically without human proof demonstrations. Whether this constitutes a Kuhnian exemplar is debatable — AlphaGeometry's impact is narrower in scope than GPT-3 or AlphaGo Zero, and it has not yet generated a broad community of "normal science" practitioners replicating its approach across domains. The more defensible assessment is that Paradigm III is at an earlier developmental stage: it now has a landmark, but not yet a paradigm shift.

**The synthesis as evidence against clean separation.** AlphaFold 2 (Jumper et al., 2021), reasoning models (Guo et al., 2025), and retrieval-augmented generation systems all combine elements across paradigm boundaries in ways that complicate strict categorical assignment. If the paradigms were truly incommensurable — in Kuhn's sense — such hybridization would be theoretically incoherent. That it is empirically productive suggests the three paradigms are better understood as *design poles* on a continuous space of architectural choices rather than discrete, mutually exclusive research traditions. The three-paradigm framing retains analytical value as a taxonomy of *foundational commitments* about the nature of intelligence, even where engineering practice freely blends them.

## 9. Open Questions

- **OQ1 — Synthesis trajectory:** Are reasoning models (o1/R1) the definitive convergence of the two paradigms, or do they represent a temporary patch on the scaling paradigm's reasoning deficit?
- **OQ2 — Commons dependency of RL systems:** Is the claim that RL systems are "self-generating" accurate? What minimum human-curated commons is required to bootstrap a capable RL agent in open-ended domains?
- **OQ3 — Neurosymbolic viability:** Can neurosymbolic architectures achieve competitive scale, or will engineering friction keep them niche despite their theoretical advantages?
- **OQ4 — HI design implications:** How should hybrid intelligence systems be designed differently depending on which underlying paradigm powers the AI component? Does a System 2-capable model change the human oversight requirements?
- **OQ5 — LeCun's fourth trajectory:** Does JEPA/world-model architecture constitute a genuine fourth paradigm, or a variant of neurosymbolic AI? V-JEPA's video results (Bardes et al., 2024) show the approach generalizing from images to temporal dynamics without any pixel reconstruction — what decisive application-domain breakthrough would constitute a Kuhnian exemplar for this trajectory at the level of AlphaGo Zero or GPT-3?
- **OQ6 — AlphaFold as case study:** AlphaFold solved an "open" scientific problem using an "open" commons (PDB). The pattern shows early signs of generalization: AlphaCode (Li et al., 2022) achieved competition-level code generation by pretraining on GitHub's open-source corpus and sampling at scale — the same open-commons-plus-compute logic, applied to programming contests rather than structural biology. Does this model extend further — to materials discovery, drug design, or climate modelling — wherever an open knowledge commons provides bootstrapping signal?
- **OQ7 — RLHF's structural ceiling:** RLHF converts human preference comparisons into a reward signal, but Casper et al. (2023) identify fundamental limits: reward model generalization, rater population representativeness, and the incompleteness of pairwise comparisons as a value representation. Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b) partially addresses this by replacing human raters with a model-generated critique-and-revision cycle — but does this escape the underlying problem or merely shift whose preferences are encoded? What governance mechanisms are required to make RLHF's value-loading process democratically legitimate rather than reflecting the preferences of a small annotation workforce?
