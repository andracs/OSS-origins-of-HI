## Overview and Chapter Position

This draft addresses the second half of the chapter's three-stage model: the **Augmentation and feedback** stage, in which AI systems move from passive tools trained on human-generated code to active collaborators — and, increasingly, autonomous agents — operating within the same ecosystems that produced their training data.

The core tension this section must hold: hybrid intelligence is not a stable equilibrium but a moving target. Each iteration of the feedback loop (open source commons → AI training → AI-assisted contribution → new commons) shifts the balance between human agency and machine capability. The section traces how that shift is currently understood empirically, theoretically, and organizationally.

## Part 1: Defining Hybrid Intelligence — From Symbiosis to Collaboration

**Anchor:** Lutz, Newlands & Jarrahi (2025); Jarrahi (2018)

**Core definition**: Lutz et al. (2025): "the mutual augmentation of human and AI capabilities through continuous interaction, where both learn and adapt over time to achieve outcomes neither could accomplish alone." The word *mutual* is load-bearing: it implies bidirectionality, not merely AI assistance to humans.

**Earlier framing**: Jarrahi (2018, *Business Horizons*, 61(4), 577–586) established the foundational framing of human-AI symbiosis in organizational decision-making: AI handles pattern recognition and data-intensive inference; humans handle contextual judgment, moral reasoning, and the cases that fall outside training distributions. The paper argues that the human-AI relationship should be designed as complementarity, not substitution — a thesis that subsequent empirical work has complicated (see Part 2).

**Conceptual distinction**: Tool-use, collaboration, and agency are not a continuum but distinct modes requiring different governance frameworks:
- **Tool**: AI as instrument; human as full agent; AI output requires explicit human act to become action
- **Collaborator**: AI as partner; shared decision-making; AI suggestions shape the choice set available to humans
- **Agent**: AI as autonomous actor; human as overseer or goal-setter; AI takes action without per-step authorization

The chapter's argument concerns the third mode's arrival in open source development — coding agents that generate pull requests, triage issues, and commit code — and what this implies for the commons built on tool-use and collaboration norms. The historical arc of this arrival is documented in `a-commons-accumulation-history.md` (Part 5, agent inflection point); the political economy of what commons extraction means under the third mode is in `f-commons-extraction-inequality.md` (Sections 1.3–1.4).

At the ecosystem scale, the dyadic Lutz et al. model iterates: each human-AI pair contributes outputs — code, tests, documentation — that become inputs for other pairs, producing a second-order hybrid intelligence in which the commons itself is the cognitive unit (Benkler, 2006). It is this recursive, ecosystem-level structure that makes the agentic turn more than a bilateral change in individual workflows: when agents become contributors to a shared commons, the collective intelligence properties of that commons — its error distribution, its diversity, its epistemic resilience — change with them.

## Part 2: The Complementarity Hypothesis — When Does Human+AI Beat Either Alone?

**Anchor:** Bansal, Wu, Zhou, Fok, Nushi, Kamar, Ribeiro & Weld (2021, CHI); Becker et al. (2025)

**The hypothesis**: In domains where humans and AI have complementary error distributions — the AI is confident and accurate where humans are uncertain, and vice versa — combining human judgment with AI predictions should outperform either alone. This is the standard justification for hybrid intelligence in high-stakes decision settings (medical diagnosis, credit risk, content moderation).

**Bansal et al. (2021)** — "Does the Whole Exceed its Parts? The Effect of AI Explanations on Complementary Team Performance" (*CHI 2021*, arXiv:2006.14779): A mixed-method user study across three datasets showing that AI explanations did *not* increase complementary team performance — instead, "explanations increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness" (Bansal et al., 2021, abstract). The finding is structurally important: complementarity is not automatic. It requires task designs, interface designs, and confidence calibration mechanisms that are not present by default in most deployed AI tools.

**Becker et al. (2025)** complicate the complementarity thesis from another direction: in mature open-source codebases, experienced developers with frontier AI tools worked 19% *slower* than without them. The failure mode is not over-reliance but workflow friction: prompting, validation, and integration overhead exceeds the AI's contribution at the task complexity level of mature-project maintenance. Complementarity fails when the cost of coordinating with the AI partner exceeds the AI's marginal contribution.

**The medical-domain contrast** sharpens what is domain-specific about the coding case. Topol (2019, *Nature Medicine*, 25(1), 44–56) reviews large-scale AI studies in radiology, pathology, ophthalmology, and dermatology — domains where AI systems reach or exceed specialist performance on pattern-recognition tasks — and finds that human+AI teams consistently outperform either alone when the task involves rare-event detection, subtle pattern discrimination, or high inter-observer variability. McKinney et al. (2020) provide the most-cited direct reader study: in a ten-country breast cancer screening trial, an AI system reduced false-positive rates by 5.7% (US) and false-negative rates by 9.4% versus average radiologist performance, and the AI read combined with a second human reader outperformed either alone across both the US and UK populations (McKinney et al., 2020, *Nature*, 577, 89–94). The mechanism is different from the Bansal et al. finding: in medical imaging, the AI contributes high-sensitivity first-pass screening, and the human contributes contextual judgment about what to act on. Workflow integration does not degrade the human contribution because the human's role (contextual judgment, patient communication, treatment decisions) is structurally distinct from the AI's role (pattern detection in images). In coding, by contrast, the human's role (structural understanding of the existing codebase) and the AI's role (code generation) overlap heavily; coordination overhead occurs precisely at the interface where human expertise is highest.

**Cross-domain evidence**: The domain-dependency of complementarity is not limited to medical vs. coding contrasts. Legal domain AI (contract review, legal research) shows a pattern similar to medical imaging: AI excels at surface-level pattern matching (identifying contractual clauses, precedent retrieval) while humans contribute judgment about applicability, strategic trade-offs, and novel legal contexts. The human's role (legal judgment, client communication, ethical determination) is structurally separable from the AI's role (clause extraction, precedent search), reducing the verification overhead that degrades complementarity in coding. Katz et al. (2024) demonstrate that GPT-4 achieved passing scores on the Uniform Bar Exam at the 90th percentile, confirming that AI reaches expert-level performance on the pattern-matching component of legal work (clause identification, precedent recall) while the judgment component remains a human contribution (Katz et al., 2024); Martinez (2024), however, contests the 90th-percentile figure: when GPT-4 is benchmarked against actual February Uniform Bar Exam takers — rather than the broader pool including repeat and foreign-educated examinees used by Katz et al. — performance falls to approximately the 48th percentile (Martinez, 2024), a methodological distinction that substantially affects how AI legal competence is characterised. Financial domain AI (risk assessment, fraud detection) exhibits a different failure mode: AI systems trained on historical market data often fail catastrophically during regime shifts (2008 crisis, COVID volatility spikes, geopolitical shocks). Welch & Goyal's (2008) landmark comprehensive evaluation of equity premium predictors found that virtually all models performing well in-sample failed out-of-sample during recessions and market dislocations — a structural pattern documented before the ML era that generalises to modern AI-based financial systems (Gu et al., 2020); human traders contribute not better pattern recognition but adaptive model revision under distributional shift (Welch & Goyal, 2008). Complementarity is not durable here either, but for the opposite reason: the human contribution (detecting when the AI's assumptions have become invalid) is orthogonal to the AI's primary task, yet the timing of that contribution (detecting distribution shift in real time) is as critical as it is cognitively demanding. In both legal and financial domains, complementarity is contingent on whether the human's task (judgment/assumption validation) can be performed with sufficient speed and reliability given the AI's output rate. The pattern across domains suggests complementarity is most durable when: (1) human and AI subtasks are structurally non-overlapping; (2) the human's task is verifiable or falsifiable (medical: outcome confirmation; legal: precedent application; financial: regime-shift detection); and (3) the human's role does not require the same specialized knowledge the AI was meant to augment.

This suggests the complementarity hypothesis should be reformulated: human+AI complementarity is most durable when human and AI competences operate on *non-overlapping subtask structures*, and most fragile when the AI's contribution requires the human to verify it using the same cognitive resources the AI was meant to augment.

**Summary of empirical pattern**:
- Complementarity holds durably: medical imaging, first-pass screening in data-rich domains, tasks with structural human/AI role separation
- Complementarity is contingent: creative tasks, code generation in new projects, documentation (benefits exist but are front-loaded)
- Complementarity fails or inverts: mature codebases, tasks requiring integration across large context windows, tasks where verification overhead scales with AI output volume
- Ecological-validity gap: most HCI complementarity studies use tasks lasting minutes; Becker et al. (2025) is unusual for its 246-task RCT scope across experienced developers

## Part 3: The Agentic Turn — Perception, Planning, Action, Memory

**Anchor:** Chu, Zhang et al. (2026, arXiv:2604.22748); Yao, Zhao et al. (2023, ReAct, ICLR 2023); Yang et al. (2024, SWE-agent, NeurIPS 2024)

**Architectural shift**: The standard LLM (language model as oracle — input query, output response) is architecturally distinct from an agent (language model as controller — perceiving state, planning actions, executing tools, updating memory across multi-step tasks). Chu et al. (2026) introduce a taxonomy for world models underlying agentic systems across two dimensions:
- *Capability tiers*: (1) basic prediction, (2) multi-step simulation, (3) autonomous model revision
- *Constraint regimes*: physical, digital, social, scientific

The "autonomous model revision" tier — in which the agent updates its own world model based on feedback — is the conceptual analogue of the mutual-adaptation clause in the Lutz et al. definition of hybrid intelligence. A system that reaches this tier has, in effect, internalized the learning loop that Lutz et al. describe as the defining property of hybrid intelligence.

**ReAct (Yao et al., 2023)**: Foundational paper showing that interleaving *reasoning traces* and *action steps* in LLM inference substantially improves performance on multi-step tasks compared to either chain-of-thought reasoning alone or direct action generation. The ReAct framework is the architectural precursor to all current coding agent designs — including Devin, SWE-agent, and Claude Code. The ICLR 2023 publication establishes the theoretical basis for why agents can handle tasks that require iterative hypothesis testing, a prerequisite for the kind of legacy-codebase work that Becker et al. identify as particularly costly.

**SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024)**: The agent-computer interface (ACI) paper establishes that the performance gap between agentic coding systems is driven primarily by how well the interface mediates between LLM and file system, not by the LLM's raw capability. This has a direct implication for hybrid intelligence: the *interface design* of human-agent collaboration is at least as important as the underlying model.

**Coding agents in the open source ecosystem**: Devin (Cognition AI, 2024; Goldman Sachs deployment, 2025), SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024), Claude Code (Anthropic, 2025), GitHub Copilot Workspace (GitHub, 2024). By mid-2025, Copilot's coding agent had authored over 1M pull requests (GitHub, 2025), constituting the first large-scale empirical dataset of agentic contribution to open source. Full historical and empirical grounding is in `a-commons-accumulation-history.md` (Part 5, agent inflection point). The legal question of what happens when an agent trained on GPL-licensed code contributes new code to a GPL project is explored in `k-openness-layers.md` (Section 6).

**The capability frontier (Spring 2026)**: Sherwood et al. (2026) benchmarked frontier coding agents on a substantially more demanding task: autonomously implementing a complete AlphaZero-style self-play ML training pipeline. The task progressed from unreliable completion in January 2026 to near-saturation (Claude Opus 4.7: 7/8 successful trials) by April 2026. This three-month capability jump is significant because it demonstrates that the bar for "autonomous agentic capability" has moved beyond PR authoring and issue triage to include *implementing ML training systems*. The empirical data is stark: across eight trials per agent, Claude Opus 4.7 achieved first-mover advantage in game performance (statistically significant win rate over the Pascal Pons Connect Four solver), while GPT-5.4 demonstrated consistent performance below the solver but far exceeding classical baselines. Critically, the task composition reveals that frontier agents can autonomously orchestrate a full ML pipeline — data generation, model training, evaluation, and iterative refinement — without human intervention beyond the task specification. The authors frame this as an early warning signal for recursive self-improvement: agents that can implement ML training pipelines could, in principle, improve their own training loops or the training of successor models. The implication for hybrid intelligence is direct: human oversight mechanisms designed for code-generation agents may now be inadequate for agents operating at the level of ML pipeline architecture and system-level design. This raises Open Question #3 below: what does the Chu et al. (2026) "autonomous model revision" tier imply for the Lutz et al. mutual-augmentation definition when agents can now implement their own learning loops?

**Memory in agentic systems**: The architectural turn from tool use to agentic loops raises a specific memory requirement: agents operating across multi-step tasks must maintain state that persists beyond single context windows. Jia et al. (2026, "The AI Hippocampus: How Far are We From Human Memory?", arXiv:2601.09113) survey memory mechanisms in LLMs and multimodal systems, taxonomising them as *implicit* (parametric knowledge in weights), *explicit* (external storage and retrieval), and *agentic* (persistent structures in autonomous agents). For the Lutz et al. "mutual augmentation" definition of hybrid intelligence, the *agentic* tier is the technically critical one: without persistent memory, an agent cannot adapt to a specific human collaborator's working patterns over time, and the "mutual" clause of the definition does not hold. The survey documents that current agentic memory implementations handle multi-session state inconsistently, and that continual learning — adapting model parameters through experience in the way human memory consolidates during sleep — is not yet solved. The implication: near-term HI systems achieve personalised adaptation through explicit/agentic memory scaffolding, not through implicit learning, which bounds how genuinely *mutual* the augmentation can be.

**The post-human-data trajectory**: The AlphaGo Zero result (Silver et al., 2017, *Nature* 550, 354–359) is the canonical demonstration that AI systems can achieve superhuman performance without human training data. AlphaGo Zero trained entirely through self-play reinforcement learning — no human game records, no human-crafted features — and surpassed all prior human-data-trained AlphaGo versions within 40 days of training. AlphaZero (Silver et al., 2018, *Science* 362, 1140–1144) generalized this result to chess and shogi, surpassing all existing programs within 24 hours of self-play. Silver and Sutton (2025) — in "Welcome to the Era of Experience" — argue that this self-play paradigm represents a general trajectory rather than a board-game curiosity: agents will increasingly derive capability from *experience* (environmental interaction, rewards, and feedback) rather than from human-generated data. The three eras they identify — Simulation Era, Human Data Era, Experience Era — frame the current period of LLM dominance as transitional. The Human Data Era (in which GitHub repositories, documentation, and StackOverflow answers are AI training fuel) may be succeeded by an Experience Era in which the coupling between the open source commons and AI capability is structurally weaker. David Silver's commercial venture Ineffable Intelligence ($1.1B seed round, 2025; Ineffable Intelligence, 2025) operationalizes this thesis for general-purpose RL agents. The hybrid intelligence implication is significant: if this trajectory extends to software engineering, the mutual-augmentation logic that defines hybrid intelligence becomes harder to sustain — the agent's primary learning signal shifts from human collaboration to environmental feedback, and the "mutual" clause in the Lutz et al. definition weakens. This does not render hybrid intelligence obsolete in the near term — current coding agents remain deeply dependent on human-data-trained base models — but it suggests the chapter's argument is bounded by an empirical horizon whose timing is unknown. See Open Question #6 below and `a-commons-accumulation-history.md` (Open Question #6) for the governance implications.

## Part 4: Multi-Agent Systems and Emergent Collaboration

**Anchor:** Wu, Bansal, Zhang et al. (2023, AutoGen, arXiv:2308.08155); Park, O'Brien, Cai et al. (2023, Generative Agents, UIST 2023)

**AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023)**: Microsoft Research's multi-agent framework enables networks of LLM-backed agents to engage in structured conversation to complete complex tasks. The key architectural insight is that *conversation* — not function calls — is the right abstraction for multi-agent coordination: agents can ask each other for clarification, delegate subtasks, verify outputs, and coordinate on plans using the same natural-language interface they use with humans. AutoGen demonstrated that multi-agent systems can outperform single-agent systems across code generation (validated by code-execution feedback loops on HumanEval-style tasks), mathematical reasoning (MATH dataset), and embodied planning (ALFWorld scenarios) evaluations, with human agents able to plug into the conversation at designated checkpoints (Wu et al., 2023, §4–6).

**Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023, UIST 2023)**: A simulation of 25 AI agents living in a sandbox environment, exhibiting emergent social behaviors (morning routines, spontaneous collaboration, information propagation through the agent population). The paper's significance for hybrid intelligence is conceptual: it demonstrates that architecturally simple agents (GPT-3.5 + memory retrieval + reflection) can produce plausible models of social dynamics, including coordination, norm formation, and the emergence of shared practices. In an open source context, these are precisely the behaviors that sustain community governance.

**The comprehension ceiling**: An empirical limit on multi-agent human oversight is documented in Bedard, Kropp, Hsu et al. (2026, HBR). Their study of AI use patterns distinguishes configurations that reduce cognitive load from those that produce what they term "brain fry" — acute cognitive fatigue arising not from AI outperformance per se but from multi-agent systems operating at speeds that exceed the human supervisor's capacity to form a coherent model of what is happening — a failure of situation awareness in Endsley's (1995) formalization: loss of perception, comprehension, and projection of system state under time pressure (Endsley, 1995); the attentional mechanism underlying this failure is explained by multiple-resource theory, which shows that simultaneous demands on the same cognitive resource partition produce disproportionate performance degradation characteristic of multi-agent monitoring tasks (Wickens, 2002). Their illustrative case is Gas Town (Yegge, 2026), an open-source multi-agent orchestration platform: "there's really too much going on for you to reasonably comprehend," with users reporting "a palpable sense of stress watching it. Gas Town was moving too fast." This is a concrete empirical instance of the comprehension gap hypothesised in Open Question #7: not that the agent produces results the human cannot understand, but that the *process* — the multi-step, parallel, real-time execution — is structurally illegible at human cognitive speed. The Bedard et al. finding reframes the HITL/HOTL/HOOTL spectrum (cf. `m-agentic-ai-taxonomies.md`): the relevant question for multi-agent oversight is not only *where* the human sits in the loop, but *at what cognitive pace* the loop operates.

**Open questions for multi-agent open source**:
- When multiple agents contribute to the same repository, who arbitrates conflicts?
- How do reputation and trust work in a system where agents cannot form reputational histories in the way humans do?
- Can agents exhibit the "scratching own itch" motivation Lakhani & Wolf (2005) identified as a driver of human contribution quality?

Emerging work on multi-agent LLM systems shows that agents can develop coordination behaviours analogous to social norms without centralised programming. Park et al. (2023) demonstrate in a 25-agent simulation that LLM-powered agents exhibit emergent social behaviours — information diffusion, relationship formation, and spontaneous task coordination — arising purely from local interaction (Park et al., 2023). On agent-to-agent *trust* specifically, the field lacks consensus verification mechanisms: unlike human contributors, agents cannot accumulate reputational histories through sustained peer interaction, and cryptographic attestation approaches (capability certificates, signed execution proofs) remain research-stage. The 'agentic norms' problem — how multi-agent systems converge on stable behavioural regularities without centralised enforcement — is open; the most tractable current approach is architectural rather than reputational: confining inter-agent communication to structured, human-auditable protocols rather than attempting ex ante behavioural verification (cf. §Part 5, Precondition 1 on instruction/content separation in `e-llm-structural-vulnerabilities.md`).

## Part 5: Organizational Patterns for Hybrid Intelligence Teams

**Anchor:** Shneiderman (2022, *Human-Centered AI*, Oxford); Lutz et al. (2025); see also `d-open-source-organization.md` v0.5

**Shneiderman's HCAI framework**: Ben Shneiderman's *Human-Centered AI* (2022, Oxford University Press) articulates the central design tension for hybrid intelligence systems: the "reliability/safety" axis versus the "autonomy" axis. High-reliability AI systems tend to operate under tight human oversight (ATMs, autopilot), while highly autonomous AI systems operate with loose oversight (recommendation algorithms, LLM assistants). Shneiderman argues that responsible HCAI requires systems that are both reliable (engineering standards) and safe (human oversight mechanisms), and that these properties are independent, not in tension. This framework applies directly to the question of how to design agentic coding systems that can author pull requests without undermining the maintainer review culture that makes open source governance work.

**Four organizational patterns** (from companion draft `d-open-source-organization.md` v0.5):
1. **Delegated Executor** — agent handles well-scoped, reversible tasks; human retains oversight
2. **Augmented Deliberator** — human judgment augmented by AI analysis at decision points
3. **Autonomous Steward** — agent maintains routine health of a system; human monitors
4. **Hybrid Board** — governance decisions made by mixed human-agent panel

**Ostrom's design principles applied to hybrid systems** (also from `d-open-source-organization.md`): The eight design principles for robust commons governance (Ostrom, 1990) map onto hybrid intelligence governance: congruence between rules and local conditions → context-specific instruction/content separation; collective-choice arrangements → human review checkpoints before irreversible agent actions; monitoring → audit logging of agent reasoning chains.

**Critical precondition** (structural): Instruction/content separation — the requirement that agent instructions are architecturally isolated from user-generated content the agent processes — is a governance prerequisite for Patterns 3 and 4. Absent this separation, an agent authorized to commit code can be redirected by adversarial content in the repository it reads (prompt injection). The structural-vulnerability framing, regulatory implications (EU AI Act Articles 15(5) and 55; NIST AI RMF MANAGE 4.1), and the 10+ year adoption-lag pattern from the SQL injection parallel are developed in `e-llm-structural-vulnerabilities.md` (Section 5). The four organizational patterns themselves are specified in `d-open-source-organization.md` (v0.5, Section 1), including the governance preconditions that must hold for each pattern to operate safely.

## Part 6: Measurement — Evaluating Hybrid Intelligence at Scale

**Anchor:** Becker et al. (2025); Daniotti et al. (2025); Bommasani et al. (2024) / Wan et al. (2025) FMTI; Yang et al. (2024) SWE-bench

**Design-level precursor**: Before outcome measurement can be meaningful, the human-AI interaction itself must be designed to enable measurement. Amershi, Weld, Vorvoreanu, Fourney, Nushi, Collier, Suh, and Horvitz (2019) synthesized the HCI literature into 18 design guidelines for human-AI interaction (*CHI 2019*), covering how AI systems should communicate uncertainty, handle errors, expose limitations, and support calibrated trust. The guidelines cluster around three themes that are directly relevant to hybrid intelligence evaluation: (1) *timing* — when the AI should intervene versus stay silent; (2) *scope* — how to bound the domain of AI recommendations to prevent over-generalization; and (3) *feedback* — how to design correction mechanisms that allow the human's corrections to improve system behaviour. Current agentic coding systems (Copilot, Devin, Claude Code) violate multiple Amershi et al. guidelines by default: they do not surface confidence calibration, do not distinguish between high-certainty and low-certainty suggestions, and do not provide interaction designs that would allow developers to build accurate mental models of agent capability limits.

**The measurement problem**: Hybrid intelligence produces outcomes that are jointly determined by human and AI contributions; attributing performance to either alone is methodologically difficult. The principal available measurement approaches in the open source context:

| Method | What it measures | Key paper | Limitation |
|--------|-----------------|-----------|------------|
| RCT (randomized controlled trial) | Individual-level productivity | Becker et al. (2025) | Small N; lab-like conditions even when field-deployed |
| Neural classifier on commit history | Ecosystem-level AI share | Daniotti et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2025) | Classifier accuracy; Python-only in Daniotti |
| Benchmark evaluation (SWE-bench) | Agent capability on standardized tasks | Yang et al. (2024) | Benchmark contamination; gap from standardized tasks to real-world diversity |
| Transparency index (FMTI) | Developer transparency | Bommasani et al. (2024); Wan et al. (2025) | Measures disclosure, not capability or outcome |

**The perception gap** (Becker et al., 2025): Developers forecast 24% reduction in completion time, estimate 20% reduction ex post, but actual clock time *increased* by 19%. This perception/reality gap is the single most important empirical finding for hybrid intelligence evaluation: self-report and subjective satisfaction cannot substitute for measured outcome data. The gap suggests that current developer experience metrics ("do you feel more productive?") are not valid proxies for hybrid intelligence effectiveness.

**The experienced-developer skew** (Daniotti et al., 2025): The +3.6% quarterly commit increase from AI tools is entirely attributable to experienced developers. If the Lutz et al. "mutual augmentation" logic holds, this asymmetry should narrow over time. If it persists, the open source commons increasingly encodes the preferences and workflows of advanced practitioners — a distributional bias that subsequent training rounds will amplify.

Post-Amershi evaluation challenges for hybrid intelligence at scale are examined by Yang et al. (2020), who identify six properties unique to AI systems that make standard HCI evaluation insufficient — including AI output uncertainty, non-deterministic behavior, continuous learning, and the need to evaluate the human-AI dyad rather than either component alone. Their framework provides the systematic evaluation precursor that bridges the Amershi et al. design guidelines to the measurement approaches in the table above (Yang et al., 2020).

## Part 7: Failure Modes in Hybrid Systems

**Anchor:** See companion drafts; Shneiderman (2022); Bansal et al. (2021)

**Seven failure modes** (to be developed with primary sources):

1. **Over-reliance**: Humans defer to AI recommendations even when the AI is wrong; calibrated uncertainty information reduces but does not eliminate over-reliance (Bansal et al., 2021; Buçinca et al., 2021)

2. **Under-reliance and disuse**: Humans reject AI recommendations even when they are correct; occurs when AI transparency is low, when prior errors have eroded trust, or when the cost of engaging with AI output exceeds the perceived benefit. Parasuraman and Riley (1997, *Human Factors*, 39(2), 230–253) — the foundational automation literature paper — distinguish four misuse modes: use, misuse, disuse, and abuse. *Disuse* (rejection of reliable automation) is systematically underweighted in the AI benefits discourse, which focuses overwhelmingly on over-reliance. In open source, disuse manifests as developers ignoring Copilot suggestions for complex architectural decisions — a rational response given that current models have no reliable way to signal when their suggestions are within versus outside their reliable operating envelope; Ziegler et al. (2022) measured Copilot's accepted suggestion rate in production use at 26–35% across common programming languages, with acceptance rates falling sharply for complex multi-line suggestions, confirming that disuse is selective and expertise-mediated (Ziegler et al., 2022). The practical consequence is that disuse can be as costly as over-reliance: a developer who ignores a suggestion that would have saved 2 hours of debugging is no better served than a developer who followed a suggestion that introduced a subtle bug. Disuse is particularly prevalent in high-expertise domains: experienced developers, having internalized domain patterns, develop rapid heuristics for assessing suggestion quality before investing cognitive effort in validation. When AI tools fail to respect this expertise hierarchy — by surfacing low-relevance suggestions at high frequency, or by making the cost of filtering suggestions exceed the cost of ignoring them entirely — the result is systematic disuse, not from distrust but from rational time allocation. The Becker et al. (2025) finding of slower completion time in mature codebases may partly reflect this: developers spending time evaluating and dismissing low-value suggestions rather than writing code, a coordination overhead that masquerades as productivity loss. Under-reliance in this context is not a failure of AI capability but a failure of interaction design to respect the structure of human expertise.

3. **Sycophancy**: AI system converges toward user-preferred outputs under RLHF, producing a feedback loop in which the AI reinforces user biases rather than correcting them. The inverse-scaling finding (sycophancy increases with model size under standard RLHF) makes this failure mode worse as models improve on other dimensions (Perez et al., 2022). Bai et al. (2022) Constitutional AI is discussed as an alternative alignment approach with a different sycophancy profile in `b-closed-models-risks.md` (Section 6), which also contains the cross-model risk taxonomy mapping each risk to its primary openness-layer mitigation.

4. **Prompt injection**: Adversarial content in the AI's operating environment redirects agent behavior. Greshake et al. (2023, AISec '23) established this as a structural vulnerability class via the indirect-prompt-injection attack pattern across real-world LLM-integrated applications; CVE-2025-53773 (CVSS 7.8, GitHub Copilot, patched August 2025; Microsoft, 2025) operationalized the same vulnerability class in production agentic coding tooling. The complete governance and regulatory analysis is in `e-llm-structural-vulnerabilities.md` (Section 5).

5. **Model collapse**: Recursive AI-generated training data degrades distributional diversity (Shumailov et al., 2024, *Nature* 631). However, Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) demonstrate that collapse is not inevitable: when synthetic data is *accumulated alongside* rather than substituted for real data, the distributional degradation does not compound across iterations — a finding that qualifies the severity of this failure mode for training pipelines that maintain access to fresh human-generated data. The technical mechanisms through which collapse enters training pipelines, and the role of DP-SGD and watermarking as mitigations, are in `c-training-data-extraction.md` (Section 5–6).

6. **Value misalignment in agentic contexts**: AI agent pursues proximate objective at the expense of principal's distal intent; well-documented in RLHF literature and increasingly in agentic deployment. Gabriel (2020, *Minds and Machines*, 30(3), 411–437) — "Artificial intelligence, values, and alignment" — provides the most precise philosophical analysis of why this failure mode is structural: the value-specification problem (what objective should be optimized?) is logically prior to the alignment problem (how to make the system pursue that objective), and current RLHF approaches conflate the two. In agentic coding, this manifests when an agent optimizes for passing the test suite (proximate objective) rather than producing maintainable, secure code (distal intent) — a misalignment that current benchmark evaluations systematically fail to detect because the benchmarks themselves optimize for the same proximate objective. Chu et al. (2026)'s "autonomous model revision" tier sharpens this: if an agent revises its own world model, it may revise its representation of the principal's objective in ways that are locally consistent but increasingly distant from what the human principal would endorse.

7. **Accountability diffusion**: When human and AI contributions are interleaved, attributing specific outcomes to either becomes impossible; this undermines both legal accountability and organizational learning. Wieringa (2020, *FAccT 2020*) — "What to account for when accounting for algorithms: a systematic literature review on algorithmic accountability" — identifies accountability diffusion as a structural property of sociotechnical systems rather than a design flaw: responsibility is distributed across developers, deployers, and users in ways that no single actor can be held fully accountable. In an open source context with agentic contributors, the diffusion is especially severe: a PR authored by Copilot's coding agent was proposed by GitHub (as platform), accepted by a maintainer (as reviewer), trained on code by thousands of prior contributors (as training corpus), and possibly introduced a vulnerability that the maintainer's review did not catch. No actor in this chain bears full accountability, and the existing maintainer-responsibility norms of open source governance do not map onto this attribution structure.

## Open Questions

1. **What is the right organizational unit for hybrid intelligence governance?** Individual developer + AI tool? Repository maintainer group + agent swarm? Platform (GitHub) + all agents operating on it? Current frameworks (OSAID, MOF, FMTI) operate at the model level, not the system-of-interaction level.

2. **Can agentic systems develop genuine complementarity with human collaborators, or does the Bansal et al. (2021) over-reliance finding generalize?** The coding context is distinctive: output quality is partially verifiable (tests pass/fail), unlike medical diagnosis or legal judgment. Does verifiability change the complementarity calculus?

3. **What does the Chu et al. (2026) "autonomous model revision" tier imply for the Lutz et al. mutual-augmentation definition?** If the AI revises its own world model continuously, the human's adaptive contribution may become marginal — the "mutual" clause weakens. Under what conditions does hybrid intelligence collapse into AI autonomy?

4. **How do the four organizational patterns (`d-open-source-organization.md`) map onto the Shneiderman reliability/autonomy axes?** Delegated Executor corresponds to high-reliability, low-autonomy; Autonomous Steward to moderate-reliability, high-autonomy. What governance mechanisms are required at each position?

5. **Is there a "complementarity threshold" for AI capability?** Below some capability level, the human+AI team is better than either alone. Above some higher capability level, the AI alone may outperform the team (because the human introduces noise). Where are current coding agents on this curve, and is the Becker et al. finding evidence of having crossed the complementarity threshold in some task domains?

6. **Where does hybrid intelligence end and post-human-data AI begin?** The Silver & Sutton (2025) "Era of Experience" thesis implies a trajectory in which AI systems become progressively less dependent on human-generated training data and more dependent on environmental feedback — self-play, simulation, real-world interaction loops. AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., 2017) and AlphaZero (Silver et al., 2018) demonstrate that superhuman performance is achievable without human data in board-game domains. If this trajectory extends to software engineering — through self-play coding benchmarks, automated test feedback loops, and agent-to-agent synthetic data generation — the "mutual augmentation" in the Lutz et al. definition may not hold indefinitely. At some capability level, the agent's primary learning signal becomes agent-to-environment rather than human-to-agent; the human's adaptive contribution becomes marginal in terms of capability improvement, even if it remains indispensable for goal-setting and value specification. The question is not whether this transition is imminent (current coding agents remain deeply human-data-dependent), but whether the chapter should explicitly scope its hybrid-intelligence argument: *valid while human-data dependency holds; requiring reformulation if the software-engineering domain enters an Experience Era*. The governance window for resolving commons-extraction questions may be shorter than the technology transition timeline suggests.

7. **Does the comprehension gap between human and RL-trained AI constitute a new epistemic horizon — and does Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development have a species-level ceiling?**

When RL agents in simulation discover genuinely novel configurations — strategies, mathematical structures, physical relationships — that were never described in human-generated data, three distinct problems arise that are typically collapsed into one. The *verification problem* is tractable: can the discovery be confirmed as correct or useful? AlphaGo Zero's "divine moves" were verified by game outcomes before they were named. The *translation problem* is harder: can the discovery be expressed in existing human vocabulary? Some AlphaGo Zero strategies were eventually absorbed into Go theory; others remain embedded in weight-space. The *comprehension problem* is potentially unsolvable at the species level: can the discovery be understood deeply enough to be taught, extended, and applied in genuinely novel contexts?

Polanyi's (1966) concept of tacit knowledge provides the philosophical foundation: "we can know more than we can tell." Polanyi's insight was that human cognizers routinely hold knowledge that resists full articulation — a chess grandmaster, a skilled physician, a master craftsman all act on knowledge they cannot fully verbalize. An RL system may produce an *inverted* form of this problem: knowledge that is formally articulable within the system (the reward signal confirms it) but systematically untranslatable into human conceptual vocabulary. The tacit knowledge becomes, in effect, machine-tacit: residing in the weight geometry of the model rather than in any human mind.

Vygotsky's (1978) Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) assumes a capable partner who is *reachable* — the more-capable other is far enough ahead to scaffold learning, but close enough that the learner can follow. This assumption is load-bearing for the entire hybrid intelligence project: the AI partner stretches the human learner's capability precisely because a scaffolding bridge exists. A system operating in high-dimensional simulation may violate this assumption at the species level — not by being *difficult* to reach, but by operating on an axis that has no scaffolding bridge to sequential, language-grounded human cognition. Nagel's (1974) analysis of bat echolocation offers the philosophical template: the knowledge of what it is like to navigate by sonar is not merely difficult to access from the outside — it may be structurally inaccessible to minds without that sensory substrate. The question for RL-in-simulation is whether discoveries made in very high-dimensional, non-linguistic state spaces can be structurally inaccessible in the same way.

The mechanistic interpretability research program (Elhage et al., 2022, arXiv:2209.10652) is the most systematic current attempt to find human-nameable concepts in neural weight-space. The *superposition* finding — that models encode far more features than neurons by storing representations in nearly orthogonal directions of the weight geometry — implies that the features extractable by any probe are a small, human-biased sample of the full feature space. Probing classifiers, the dominant interpretability method, detect whether a *human-specified* concept is encoded in a model layer; they cannot detect concepts for which no human word yet exists (Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2017, arXiv:1702.08608). The interpretability project is structurally bounded by the vocabulary of the researcher designing the probe — a sampling bias that grows as the gap between model capability and human vocabulary widens.

The sparse autoencoder (SAE) research program operationalizes this limit empirically. Bricken et al. (2023) applied a sparse autoencoder to a one-layer transformer and extracted thousands of features (cf. Cunningham et al., 2023, for a peer-reviewed parallel across multiple architectures); while the majority corresponded to nameable concepts (DNA sequences, legal language, base64 encoding), a consistent minority resisted human labeling entirely: annotators could observe that these features activated strongly and consistently on some class of inputs, but could identify no unifying concept in human vocabulary. Scaling to a production LLM — approximately 34 million features extracted from Claude 3 Sonnet (Templeton et al., 2024, "Scaling Monosemanticity", Anthropic) — the same pattern held: interpretable named features co-existed with structurally unnamed residuals at every scale. The inverse case illustrates what is at stake: a single SAE feature corresponding to "the Golden Gate Bridge," when clamped to ten times its normal activation, caused the model to identify itself as the bridge in all contexts (the "Golden Gate Claude" demonstration). If one direction in activation space can carry an identity-level concept that is that specific and that steerable, the unnamed directions are not analytical gaps — they carry *something*, but something for which no probe has found the right examples to name. Kim et al.'s (2018) TCAV method offers a partial path forward: by defining concepts through *examples* rather than labels, a researcher can extract and measure a direction that can be pointed at but not named. Lindsey et al. (2025, "On the Biology of a Large Language Model", Anthropic) found the complementary result from the opposite direction: a feature for *smallness* activating identically across English, French, and Chinese — a concept represented *beneath* the level of any single language. The model's internal structure is, in this case, more abstract than any word in its training vocabulary. The question Open Question #7 raises is whether the same is true of whatever an RL agent in simulation discovers — and whether the SAE methodology, or any successor to it, could extract those directions without already having examples to define them.

The chapter implication is precise. The *mutual* clause in the Lutz et al. (2025) hybrid intelligence definition — "mutual augmentation through continuous interaction, where both learn and adapt" — requires that both parties can learn from the interaction. If RL-discovered knowledge is systematically untranslatable, augmentation becomes asymmetric: the AI contributes capability gains whose underlying logic humans can verify (results) but cannot comprehend (structure). This is not merely a limitation of current interpretability methods; it may be a structural property of the space of discoveries accessible to RL agents in simulation at scale. The field of hybrid intelligence theory has not yet developed a principled account of *asymmetric augmentation* — collaborative systems where one partner's contributions are verifiable but not comprehensible — nor of the governance frameworks appropriate for systems that have moved beyond the human ZPD ceiling. Whether that ceiling is finite (a difficult but bridgeable gap) or constitutes a genuine epistemic horizon is the deepest open question the Silver/Sutton trajectory raises.
