## Research Question

Big tech companies worth hundreds of billions of dollars were built on open source commons — free software, open data, shared infrastructure — and now train their AI systems on data produced by millions of unpaid contributors to those same commons. Meanwhile, wealth inequality is accelerating: the gap between the contributors to the commons and the shareholders of the platforms built on top of it is growing. **Can hybrid intelligence be designed to narrow rather than widen this gap?**

## Framing

There is a paradox at the heart of the digital economy. The most valuable companies in the world — measured by market capitalization — were built almost entirely on a foundation of shared labor: open source software developed by volunteers, datasets curated by communities, knowledge organized by unpaid editors. Linux, the operating system that runs most of the world's servers, was built by contributors who received no equity in the companies that depend on it. Wikipedia, the world's largest encyclopedia, was written by millions of volunteers whose work now trains AI systems that generate revenue for closed platforms. GitHub, Stack Overflow, Common Crawl — the entire substrate of machine learning — is a commons that was produced collectively and captured privately.

This is not a new observation. Economists have long noted the gap between social value created and private value captured. What is new is the scale, the speed, and the mechanism: AI systems trained on commons-produced data can now generate value at a rate that no individual contributor — human or algorithmic — could previously achieve. The extraction has been industrialized.

At the same time, wealth inequality is accelerating. The shareholders of the five largest technology companies have accumulated wealth at a rate that dwarfs wage growth for the workers — including the open source contributors, the Wikipedia editors, the Stack Overflow answerers — whose collective labor made those valuations possible (Piketty et al., 2018). The commons gave; the platforms received.

The question this chapter asks is not whether this is fair — that is a normative question with a clear answer — but whether it is structurally reversible. Specifically: can hybrid intelligence systems — systems that combine human and machine reasoning in distributed, participatory architectures — be designed to close the gap between commons contribution and commons return?

## 1. The extraction model: how big tech built on the commons

### 1.1 Open source as infrastructure for private value

The economic relationship between open source software and the companies built on it is a textbook case of externality capture. Google's infrastructure runs on Linux, Kubernetes, and dozens of other open source projects whose development was funded by volunteer time. Amazon Web Services is built on the same substrate. The combined market capitalization of the major cloud providers — each of which offers open source software as a service for profit — dwarfs the total funding of the foundations that maintain the software they depend on.

This is not exploitation in the simple sense: open source contributors choose to contribute under licenses that permit commercial use. But the distributional outcome — contributors receive recognition and intrinsic reward; shareholders receive financial return — creates a structural inequality between the producers of the commons and the extractors of its value. Lerner and Tirole's (2002) foundational analysis of open source economics explains contributor motivation through a *career concerns* model: programmers contribute to signal quality to future employers or clients, generating a public good as a by-product of private reputation investment. The model is elegant but reveals the core distributional problem: the signaling benefit accrues to the individual contributor, while the value of the accumulated codebase accrues to whoever builds services on top of it (Lerner & Tirole, 2002, pp. 212–218).

The deeper theoretical grounding comes from Benkler's (2006) analysis of *commons-based peer production* in *The Wealth of Networks*. Benkler argued that distributed, non-hierarchical, non-market production (Wikipedia, Linux, SETI@home) could outperform both managerial and market coordination on tasks where information was widely distributed, contributors were heterogeneous, and work could be modularized into small contributions. Crucially, Benkler observed that this peer production was structurally susceptible to *value capture by complements*: firms that controlled adjacent infrastructure (cloud hosting, search, app stores, distribution channels) could profit from peer-produced commons without contributing back and without bearing production costs (Benkler, 2006, ch. 4–5). Benkler treated this asymmetry as a distributional concern but not yet a sustainability concern — at the time of writing, the commons production base appeared self-sustaining. The AI training extraction reframes Benkler's diagnosis: the asymmetric value capture is no longer merely distributional, because the captured commons is now used to build systems that compete with the commons itself for attention, traffic, and contributor labour. The 2024–2025 Stack Overflow and Wikipedia data below are the empirical signal that Benkler's "peer production resilience" assumption no longer holds once AI-mediated substitution is added to the system.

### 1.2 Machine learning as a second extraction

The training of large language models represents a second, deeper extraction from the commons. Where the first extraction took code and turned it into services, the second extraction takes knowledge — the accumulated writing, reasoning, and creative output of millions of people — and turns it into model weights that generate commercial revenue.

The contributors did not consent to this use in any meaningful sense. Wikipedia editors wrote for Wikipedia, not for OpenAI's training corpus. Stack Overflow contributors answered questions for the benefit of other developers, not to provide training data for systems that would subsequently reduce Stack Overflow's traffic by replacing its function. Common Crawl scraped the web with no revenue model — and the scraped data, Common Crawl alone constituting approximately 60% of GPT-3's pretraining tokens (Brown et al., 2020), became the substrate for AI systems that have since generated billions in revenue for their developers (Maslej et al., 2025).

The measurable consequences are now documented. Stack Overflow, whose Q&A corpus is among the most heavily weighted sources in code-oriented LLM training sets, saw traffic fall 13.9% in March 2023 and a further 17.7% in April 2023 following the release of GPT-4 — a platform whose training data included years of Stack Overflow contributions (Similarweb, 2023). Stack Overflow itself subsequently confirmed an overall traffic decline of approximately 5% versus 2022, with a 14% dip in April 2023 attributed directly to GPT-4's launch (Orosz, 2023) — corroborating Similarweb's order-of-magnitude estimate. The platform's question volume, a direct measure of contributor engagement, fell from approximately 108,500 monthly questions at ChatGPT's November 2022 launch to under 26,000 by December 2024 — a decline of over 75% in under two years (Stack Exchange Data Explorer, 2024). Question volume continued declining into 2025 (Holscher, 2025). Wikipedia faces an analogous trajectory: every major LLM is trained on Wikipedia content, which is frequently over-represented in training corpora because it appears both as a direct dataset and as embedded content throughout web crawls (Wikimedia Foundation, 2023; Soldaini et al., 2024). A peer-reviewed analysis in *AI & Society* concluded that LLMs constitute an "endangered species" threat to Wikipedia's sustainability by effectively detouring the website and reducing its capacity to recruit new editors — the primary mechanism by which the encyclopedia has maintained both its contributor base and its donation revenue (Vetter et al., 2025). A Wikimedia Foundation analysis published in April 2025 confirmed that bandwidth used for downloading multimedia content from Wikimedia Commons had grown by 50% since January 2024, attributable to AI crawler traffic — bots consuming over 65% of the most resource-intensive bandwidth despite accounting for only 35% of pageviews, with no corresponding revenue from the commercial beneficiaries (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025). The cost is paid by donors; the Foundation set a 2025/2026 goal to reduce scraper bandwidth demand by 30%.

This is the value gap at the heart of the AI economy: the commons produces the knowledge; the platforms extract the value; the contributors receive nothing — and the institutional infrastructure that sustains the commons is damaged in the process.

### 1.3 The acceleration mechanism

Hybrid intelligence systems accelerate this dynamic in two ways. First, AI systems trained on commons data can now generate value faster than the commons can regenerate: a model trained on ten years of Stack Overflow answers can answer more questions per second than the entire Stack Overflow contributor community — and capture the advertising and subscription revenue that would otherwise flow to the platform supporting those contributors. Second, AI-generated content increasingly enters the commons — and the training pipeline — displacing human-generated content and reducing the quality of future training data (Liang et al., 2024). The extraction loop consumes itself.

### 1.4 Measuring commons harm: a quantitative taxonomy

The extraction described above is empirically tractable but currently measured in fragments. A coherent measurement framework requires five distinct categories:

1. **Direct traffic and engagement loss.** Where the commons platform depended on user visits for revenue or contributor recruitment, AI substitution shows up as referral decline. Stack Overflow's −13.9% (March 2023) and −17.7% (April 2023) traffic drops are the cleanest published figures (Similarweb, 2023); analogous declines are reported for search-referred traffic to news and reference sites in the wake of AI Overview rollouts.

2. **Infrastructure cost externalities.** Where the commons platform must absorb scraping load, costs are visible in bandwidth consumption. Wikimedia's documented +50% bandwidth increase in the twelve months from January 2024, attributable to AI scraper traffic — bots consuming over 65% of the most resource-intensive bandwidth — is the canonical case (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025). The cost is paid by donors; the value accrues to commercial model developers.

3. **Maintainer opportunity cost.** When the commons supplies training data, a portion of maintainer labour is implicitly subsidising commercial model providers. This category is hardest to quantify because it requires counterfactual reasoning (would the maintainer contribute under different conditions?), but it is the category most relevant to long-term commons sustainability. Vetter et al.'s (2025) "endangered species" framing — that LLMs effectively detour Wikipedia and reduce its capacity to recruit new editors — is one operationalisation: the harm is measured in the rate at which the contributor pipeline thins.

4. **Reputation and credibility externalities.** When AI systems answer queries by citing or paraphrasing commons content without attribution, errors and hallucinations are attributed back to the commons platform (Maynez et al., 2020). A 2023 Wikimedia Foundation statement on Wikipedia's value in the AI era articulates the asymmetry: AI systems trained on Wikipedia inherit its reliability, while AI-generated errors that cite Wikipedia as a source risk damaging the encyclopedia's reputation among readers (Wikimedia Foundation, 2023). This category is largely uncharted empirically but is structurally critical: a commons that loses public trust loses both donations and contributor motivation.

5. **Data depletion and homogenisation of the training substrate.** A fifth, structurally distinct category emerges when AI-generated content enters the commons and re-enters subsequent training runs. Shumailov et al. (2024) demonstrate in *Nature* that recursive training on model-generated data produces *model collapse*: the tails of the original distribution disappear, low-frequency events are forgotten, and successive model generations converge toward a narrower, lower-variance approximation of the original human-produced distribution. The harm here is not borne by any single platform's revenue or bandwidth but by the *substrate of the commons itself*: the diversity of language, style, niche knowledge, and minority viewpoint that gave the commons its training value. Where Categories 1–4 measure extraction from individual commons platforms, Category 5 measures the slow degradation of the commons-as-input under recursive reuse. The mechanism is documented experimentally for Variational Autoencoders, Gaussian Mixture Models, and large language models alike (Shumailov et al., 2024). The implication for the four prior categories is that they are not independent: contributor harm (3) reinforces depletion (5) because human-produced content becomes scarcer and more valuable, while attribution failure (4) accelerates depletion (5) by making it harder to filter synthetic content out of training corpora.

Together these five categories provide a measurement vocabulary for "commons harm" that the chapter's design principles (Section 4) can operationalize.

#### 1.4a — Falsification attempts and measurement challenges

Each of the five categories faces a specific evidentiary challenge. Taken together, they suggest the taxonomy is directionally correct but empirically fragile at the level of causal attribution.

**Category 1 (traffic decline):** The correlation between GPT-4's release and Stack Overflow's March–April 2023 traffic drop is the most cited evidence for AI substitution harm, but the causal attribution is contested. Stack Overflow underwent high-profile governance controversies in early 2023 — including disputes over AI-generated answer moderation policies — that may independently explain contributor and visitor behaviour changes. The Similarweb traffic estimates are third-party projections, but Stack Overflow itself confirmed the order of magnitude: approximately 5% overall traffic decline versus 2022, with a 14% April 2023 dip attributed to GPT-4's launch (Orosz, 2023). More strikingly, question volume — a direct measure of contributor engagement from Stack Overflow's own Data Explorer — fell over 75% between November 2022 and December 2024 (Stack Exchange Data Explorer, 2024). A falsifying test: if the decline were primarily AI substitution, platforms with lower AI-topic overlap (e.g. niche technical forums) should show lower traffic decline. This comparative analysis has not been published. GitHub's 2023 Octoverse report documented 21% year-on-year developer growth and 301 million contributions to open source projects in that year alone — metrics moving in the opposite direction from Stack Overflow, despite GitHub hosting structurally similar developer knowledge (GitHub, 2023). The divergence widened further: the 2025 Octoverse report records 180 million developers on GitHub (up from 100 million in 2023), 1.12 billion contributions to public and open source projects, and 36 million new developers joining in a single year — each figure a platform record (GitHub, 2025). GitHub's symbiotic relationship with AI tools (Copilot integration, AI-generated pull requests) may explain its divergent trajectory; what it confirms is that the mechanism is platform-specific rather than a blanket commons-decline dynamic. The strongest peer-reviewed counter-evidence to the substitution-causes-decline hypothesis on a *non-code* commons platform is Reeves, Yin, and Simperl (2025) in *Collective Intelligence*: a panel-regression analysis of twelve Wikipedia language editions before and after ChatGPT's launch (January 2021 – January 2024) finds *no fall* in any of four engagement metrics (page views, unique visitors, edits, editors), with page views and visitor numbers actually *rising* post-ChatGPT (Reeves et al., 2025). The comparative quasi-causal design is the most informative element: by exploiting the natural experiment that ChatGPT was unavailable in six of the twelve language editions (Amharic, Farsi, Russian, Tigrinya, Uzbek, Vietnamese), the authors find that the post-ChatGPT growth in *page views and visitor numbers* was generally smaller in ChatGPT-available editions than in unavailable editions, while edit counts and editor numbers showed no significant difference between the two groups (Reeves et al., 2025, Conclusion) — quasi-causal evidence that ChatGPT *dampens reader-side growth* without producing absolute decline and without measurable effect on the contributor pipeline at the encyclopaedic level. This finding sharpens the harm taxonomy: at the encyclopaedic-commons level, Category 1 harm appears to be a counterfactual-growth shortfall rather than an absolute traffic loss, distinguishing Wikipedia's trajectory from Stack Overflow's. Stack Overflow's revenue actually grew from $89 million (2022) to $125 million (2024) (Prosus N.V., 2022, 2024) despite the traffic collapse, suggesting the *contributor* harm is decoupled from the *commercial* harm — an important nuance for the harm taxonomy.

**Category 2 (infrastructure cost):** The Wikimedia Foundation's +50% bandwidth figure (Wikimedia Foundation, 2025) is now documented in an official Wikimedia Diff blog post (April 2025) and confirmed by independent technology press, removing the earlier verification gap. The bandwidth cost to a foundation that raises ~$150M annually in donations is unlikely to be existential; the more important question is whether the cost increase corresponds to *marginal training-related scraping* or to legitimate increased interest traffic driven partly by AI-awareness effects. The two sources of demand are not separated in the published figure. Additionally, the bulk of large-scale Wikipedia training-data extraction occurred in the 2020–2023 wave of major pretraining runs; however, frontier models continued to incorporate Wikipedia snapshots into 2024 and beyond — Llama 3's documented training corpus extends through approximately April 2024 (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and later model families have comparable or later data cutoffs — making the 2024 bandwidth increase attributable to a mix of continued training scraping by newer entrants and inference-time retrieval, with the two sources of demand not cleanly separable in the published Wikimedia data. The Foundation's own framing — that "crawlers impact the operations of the Wikimedia projects" — treats this as an ongoing operational burden, not a training-data accountability issue, which is the framing this chapter requires.

**Category 3 (maintainer opportunity cost):** This is the weakest category empirically. The Vetter et al. (2025) "endangered species" framing operationalises harm as *reduction in the contributor pipeline* — specifically the claim that LLMs detour traffic away from Wikipedia and reduce its capacity to recruit new editors. This is a plausible mechanism but the study relies on expert interviews (qualitative) rather than quantitative traffic data. A strong falsifying observation: if AI tools increase the productivity of existing contributors (which Daniotti et al. (2026) document for open source code output: +3.6% quarterly code output across approximately 170,000 GitHub developers, though the gains are concentrated among experienced developers), the maintainer opportunity cost may be offset or reversed. Yeverechyahu et al. (2024) provide complementary commons-level causal evidence using a difference-in-differences design that exploits GitHub Copilot's October 2021 selective rollout — Copilot supported Python and Rust at launch, but not R or Haskell — and document a significant increase in overall contributions to repositories in the treated languages relative to controls, with the gain concentrated in iterative innovation (maintenance, feature-refinement) rather than capability innovation (new code paths, novel features) (Yeverechyahu et al., 2024). This is, to date, the cleanest causal evidence that AI tooling can increase contribution rate at the project rather than individual level. The Daniotti et al. observational finding and the Yeverechyahu et al. natural-experiment finding are both directly challenged, however, by a randomised controlled trial: Becker et al. (2025) assigned 246 tasks to 16 experienced open-source developers under conditions randomly allowing or disallowing AI tool access, and found that AI *increased* completion time by 19% — experienced developers were slower with AI than without (Becker et al., 2025). Developers systematically overestimated the benefit before and after: they predicted a 24% speedup and, post-task, estimated a 20% speedup, against the actual 19% slowdown. This is the most rigorous causal evidence currently available on AI productivity effects for experienced open-source contributors specifically; it does not support the "offset" hypothesis at the individual level. A fourth study now adds direct evidence at the *coding Q&A platform* level — the empirical locus of Categories 1 and 3 most narrowly defined: Li and Kim (2024), in *Marketing Letters*, applied a difference-in-differences design to user post data from a large coding Q&A community before and after ChatGPT's introduction and found that, even as overall answer volume increased, the number of *high-quality* answers fell, and the decline was driven primarily by highly engaged contributors — what the authors describe as the AI tool mainly demotivating core users (Li & Kim, 2024). The Li and Kim mechanism — perceived question sophistication rising despite no actual content change, and reduced motivation among loyal contributors — operationalises the maintainer opportunity cost in the precise terms Category 3 requires: a measurable contributor-pipeline harm in a peer-reviewed DiD design, contemporaneous with the Stack Overflow trajectory documented in Category 1. The reconciliation suggested by the four studies together is that AI tooling raises the *commons-level* contribution rate in code repositories (Yeverechyahu, Daniotti) by lowering entry costs and enabling iterative work, while *demotivating* experienced contributors on Q&A platforms (Li & Kim) and *slowing* experienced contributors at the task level (Becker). The net effect on the contributor pipeline therefore varies by platform substrate (code repositories vs. Q&A communities) and by contributor seniority — a quantitative resolution that current data points toward, but does not yet close.

**Category 4 (reputation/credibility):** The Wikimedia Foundation's statement that AI systems trained on Wikipedia inherit its reliability while their errors create reputational risk for the encyclopedia is advocacy language from a stakeholder, not an empirical finding. The closest peer-reviewed entry point identified is Waddell (2025, *Digital Journalism*), which experimentally examines how AI source-attribution and source-priming cues shape news credibility judgements — directly relevant to whether errors propagate back to a cited commons platform's reputation, but framed for the news-domain rather than for encyclopaedic commons. Waddell (2025) does not yet establish the specific causal chain that the Wikimedia framing asserts (errors → cited-source reputation harm), and a generic "Haber et al. 2024" on attribution-of-AI-errors-to-sources, repeatedly searched in prior cycles, has not been locatable in any indexed catalogue and is treated here as not extant. The reputational harm mechanism is therefore plausible as a *long-run risk* with one peer-reviewed adjacent anchor (Waddell, 2025), but it is not yet empirically documented as an *occurring harm* against commons platforms specifically.

**Category 5 (data depletion/homogenisation):** This is the most theoretically grounded but practically hardest to attribute category. Shumailov et al.'s (2024) *Nature* paper provides controlled experimental evidence that recursive training on model-generated content produces irreversible distributional collapse. The internal validity of the demonstration is high; the external validity to large frontier models trained with mixed human/synthetic corpora and curated data filters is the open question. Major model developers now use synthetic data deliberately and have published mitigation strategies (rejection sampling, provenance tagging, human-in-the-loop curation), so the in-the-wild harm rate depends on how aggressively those mitigations work. The strongest empirical and theoretical challenge to Shumailov et al.'s collapse prediction now comes from Gerstgrasser et al. (2024), a Stanford–Harvard–MIT collaboration that pretrains language-model sequences on text corpora under two regimes: *replace* (each generation's synthetic data overwrites the prior real data) and *accumulate* (each generation's synthetic data is appended to all prior real and synthetic data). The replace regime reproduces collapse; the accumulate regime does *not* — test error stabilises at a finite upper bound independent of the number of model-fitting iterations, a result the authors prove analytically for linear models and confirm empirically for language models, diffusion models on molecule conformation, and variational autoencoders for image generation (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024). **[Source-quality note: Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) remains an arXiv preprint (arXiv:2404.01413) with iCit 10 as of 2026-05-16 — below STEERING.md's preprint threshold of iCit > 20, and no peer-reviewed venue (ICML, NeurIPS, TMLR) has been located. This source carries the principal counter-evidence to Shumailov et al. (2024) in this section. Its evidentiary status should be flagged in any submission draft as a below-threshold preprint pending peer-reviewed publication; the authors and editors should monitor for a conference or journal version before submission.]** The implication for §1.4 Category 5 is sharpening rather than overturning: Shumailov's collapse is a property of *data replacement*, not of *synthetic-data presence*, and frontier training pipelines that retain historical human-produced corpora alongside newer synthetic data should — by Gerstgrasser et al.'s analysis — exhibit a finite degradation ceiling rather than unbounded collapse. This makes Category 5 harm conditional on training-pipeline data-curation policy, which itself is opaque to outside observers and unevenly disclosed across model developers. A falsifying observation against the residual harm prediction would be evidence that frontier models trained 2024–2026 do not exhibit any distributional narrowing on benchmarks designed to probe long-tail knowledge; conversely, a confirming observation would be measurable degradation on rare-language, rare-domain, or rare-style generation tasks across model generations. Neither has been published in a form that maps cleanly onto Shumailov et al.'s or Gerstgrasser et al.'s theoretical predictions, leaving Category 5 *theoretically bounded, empirically pending* at production scale.

**Structural challenge to the taxonomy as a whole:** The five categories each measure harm at a specific level — platform-level (1, 2), contributor-level (3, 4), or substrate-level (5) — but the chapter's broader argument that the extraction *model* is structurally harmful to HI ecosystems requires evidence that the harm is (a) *caused* by AI training specifically rather than by platform competition generally, (b) *net negative* after accounting for AI-mediated benefits to the commons (increased discoverability, new contributor pathways), and (c) *attributable* to identifiable actors rather than to diffuse market forces. None of the five measurement approaches in their current form satisfies all three requirements simultaneously. The taxonomy is a necessary first step toward a rigorous harm measurement framework; it does not yet constitute one. Each principle in Section 4 is best understood as a counter-mechanism against one or more of these harm categories: attribution by default targets (4) and (5, by enabling synthetic-content filtering); revenue sharing targets (1) and (2); commons governance targets (3); fork rights and capability access target the long-run viability of (3) and (5, by preserving access to non-collapsed model lineages).

## 2. The inequality dimension

The value gap is not merely a matter of fairness to individual contributors. It is a driver of structural wealth inequality at scale.

The shareholders of AI companies are not uniformly distributed. They are concentrated: a small number of venture capital firms, founders, and early employees hold the majority of equity in the companies that capture the value extracted from the commons (Maslej et al., 2025). The contributors to the commons are distributed across the globe, across income levels, across employment statuses. Many are in the Global South; many are students or hobbyists; many contributed in periods of their lives when they had time and motivation but not financial resources (Wachs et al., 2022).

The standard economic framing — that open source contributions are compensated by reputation, skills development, and the public good of the software itself — was adequate when the companies built on the commons were also modest in scale. It is strained when the commons contributes to companies valued at hundreds of billions of dollars while its contributors receive no financial return.

This is Piketty's r > g applied to the knowledge economy: the return on capital (equity in AI companies) exceeds the return on labor (contribution to the commons that enables those companies) by a margin that increases with each AI cycle (Piketty, 2014). Srnicek's (2016) analysis of *platform capitalism* documents the mechanism: technology platforms extract value by controlling the infrastructure through which contributors must operate, capturing rents that exceed the cost of provision through network effects and switching costs. The platforms — Google, Meta, Microsoft — grow because they sit atop the commons, not alongside it. Zuboff (2019) situates this within surveillance capitalism's logic: the platforms do not merely capture exchange value; they accumulate what she calls "behavioral surplus" — data about contributor behavior that is fed back into the system to refine extraction mechanisms. Applied to AI training, the dynamic intensifies: the commons generates training data; the platforms generate value; the platforms then feed that value back into systems that further displace commons contributors.

Mazzucato's (2013) analysis of *the entrepreneurial state* adds a critical pre-history to this argument. The foundational technologies underpinning the modern AI stack — the internet protocol suite, GPS, the touchscreen, key search-ranking algorithms, and substantial portions of the foundational ML research itself — were developed under public investment (DARPA, NSF, NIH, public university research); Ahmed and Wahed (2020) document the contemporary counterpart: the concentration of compute and data resources required for frontier deep learning research has reversed earlier democratization trends, with a small number of large private actors now controlling the infrastructure that determines which research directions are computationally feasible. The private sector did not invent these; it built on them. The standard tech-sector narrative reverses the causality, attributing innovation to private R&D and the resulting capital concentration to risk-bearing entrepreneurship. Mazzucato's empirical correction shows that the public sector bore the substantive risk and the substantive innovation, while the private sector captured the returns (Mazzucato, 2013). The AI training extraction is the latest iteration of the same dynamic: commons-funded substrate — whether public R&D dollars or volunteer-contributed data and code — underwrites private value capture, with no institutional mechanism for return on the public/commons investment. The redistribution argument in Section 3 is therefore not a novel demand for what platforms owe the commons; it is the application to AI training of a redistribution problem that public-investment scholars have documented across multiple technology cycles.

The consequence for inequality is structural. In prior eras of technological transition, new forms of capital creation created new routes to wealth accumulation. Industrial capital created pathways to fortune; financial capital created venture-backed startups. AI is creating value at scales those transitions did not approach — but the routes to capturing that value remain concentrated. The commons contributors — if they are not employed by a major AI company — are systematically excluded from equity participation in the value they generate.

## 3. Can hybrid intelligence close the gap?

The argument that hybrid intelligence can reduce rather than widen the commons-to-tech-giants gap rests on three structural claims:

### 3.1 Distributed participation as a redistributive mechanism

Hybrid intelligence systems that incorporate human participants in their operation — not merely as data sources but as decision-makers, validators, and beneficiaries — create a potential mechanism for redistributing value. Cooperative AI systems in which contributors hold stakes, federated systems in which data remains under community control, and commons-governed AI systems in which the training data pool is owned by its contributors are all structural alternatives to the extraction model.

These are not hypothetical. Wikipedia's Wikimedia Foundation has been engaged in ongoing discussions with AI companies about data-use terms and attribution through 2024–2025, though no formal revenue-sharing partnership had been announced and independently documented as of this writing — the frequently cited "Wikimedia Foundation and Apple partnership to bring Wikipedia to Apple Intelligence" press release cannot be located in the Foundation's 2024 news archive and should be treated as unverified pending the author's direct confirmation with Wikimedia communications. Hugging Face operates as a hybrid community platform discussed in the literature as a design case for revenue sharing with model contributors (Huang & Siddarth, 2023), though no primary Hugging Face policy documentation of an enforced contributor revenue-share scheme has been independently verified at scale. Worker-owned data cooperatives in several countries have begun to organize contributors to demand compensation for training data use (Delacroix & Lawrence, 2019). The most developed institutional model is platform cooperativism — Sandoval's (2020) analysis of platform cooperatives demonstrates that alternative governance structures (where workers or data contributors own and govern the platform collectively) can redistribute both decision-making power and economic returns to contributors, shifting the owner-contributor relationship from extraction to reciprocal value-sharing (Sandoval, 2020, pp. 108–135). Delacroix and Lawrence's (2019) bottom-up data trust framework, developed at the Oxford Internet Institute, provides a complementary legal and governance template: by establishing a fiduciary trust structure that places data contributors as beneficiaries and requires trustees to manage data use under enforceable duties of loyalty and care, the framework operationalises contributor control without requiring individual negotiation of terms with each downstream user (Delacroix & Lawrence, 2019). The scalability question — whether cooperative or trust-based governance can operate at the billions-of-datapoints scale of frontier LLM training — remains open; cooperative platforms operating in EU jurisdictions are actively organising workers' data under such governance, but no peer-reviewed implementation case study at frontier-LLM scale has yet been published. The institutional and technical precursors exist; the demonstration at training-corpus scale does not yet.

### 3.2 Hybrid governance as accountability infrastructure

The open source organizational patterns described elsewhere in this handbook — legible governance, auditable decision processes, fork as exit — provide a template for hybrid intelligence systems that are accountable to the commons they draw on. A hybrid intelligence system governed by the community whose knowledge it was trained on is structurally different from one governed by its shareholders. The BigScience BLOOM project — in which over 1,000 researchers across 250+ institutions collectively governed the development of a 176-billion-parameter multilingual model under explicit data-use and model-release policies — provides a frontier-scale empirical case: collective governance decisions about training data sourcing, model access, and deployment restrictions were made through working-group processes rather than by a single corporate entity (Le Scao et al., 2022). The governance structure determines who captures the value.

### 3.3 Capability democratization vs. value redistribution

There is an important distinction between two claims: that hybrid intelligence democratizes access to AI capabilities (weaker claim) and that it redistributes the value created by AI (stronger claim). The first is more plausible and more often made: open source AI models give individuals and small organizations access to capabilities previously available only to large companies. But access to capability is not the same as ownership of value. The question is whether hybrid intelligence systems can be designed to ensure that those who contribute to the commons — in labor, data, or governance — receive a share of the financial value those contributions generate.

## 4. Design principles for commons-redistributive hybrid intelligence

*(Forward link: the three distinct layers of openness relevant to commons reciprocity—open weights vs. open source vs. open training data—are separated explicitly in `k-openness-layers.md`. The design principles below should be read as constraints at each layer: weight release affects capability access; source openness affects fork/exit and auditability; training-data disclosure affects extraction accountability and revenue-sharing feasibility.)*

The five design principles below operationalize, for the AI training context, what Ostrom (1990) identified as the eight design principles of long-surviving commons institutions: (1) clearly defined boundaries; (2) congruence between rules and local conditions; (3) collective-choice arrangements; (4) effective monitoring; (5) graduated sanctions; (6) accessible conflict-resolution mechanisms; (7) recognition of rights to organize; and (8) nested governance for larger systems (Ostrom, 1990, *Governing the Commons*). Where Ostrom's commons were physical (irrigation, fisheries, pastures) and the threat was overuse, the AI training commons is informational and the threat is extraction-without-replenishment — but the institutional template is structurally identical. Each of the five principles below maps onto one or more Ostrom criteria: *attribution by default* operationalizes (1) clearly defined boundaries; *commons governance of training data* operationalizes (3) collective-choice arrangements; *revenue sharing* operationalizes (4) effective monitoring and (5) graduated sanctions; *fork rights for communities* operationalizes (6) accessible conflict resolution; *capability access as a commons right* operationalizes (7) recognition of the right to organize. The mapping is not coincidental: Ostrom's criteria emerged inductively from cases where commons institutions persisted across centuries, and the AI training context now poses the same institutional design problem at a new scale and over a new substrate.

If hybrid intelligence is to narrow rather than widen the gap, the following design principles are necessary conditions:

1. **Attribution by default.** Training data sources are documented and contributor identity preserved (where consented). This is a precondition for any form of compensation or credit. The EU AI Act's training data summary requirement (Article 53; binding August 2, 2025) operationalizes the floor for this principle, though the standard remains voluntary disclosure for commercial developers outside the EU. Hacker, Engel, and Mauer's (2023) regulatory analysis at FAccT (the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency) demonstrated that the EU AI Act's framing — written before ChatGPT's release — would require substantial extension to govern foundation-model training-data extraction; the Article 53 transparency obligation is the principal mechanism that survived the Act's late-stage amendment process to address general-purpose AI training data, and the authors flag that the same provision creates the legal substrate on which downstream attribution and revenue-sharing claims could subsequently be built (Hacker et al., 2023).

2. **Commons governance of training data.** The decision about what data enters a training corpus is made by the community that produced it, not unilaterally by the model developer. This mirrors the governance structures of successful open source projects. Hugging Face's FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024) and LAION Re-LAION-5B (Schuhmann et al., 2022) demonstrate this model at the dataset level; data cooperatives in the EU (Solid Cooperative, Cooperative Platforms) are applying it at the contributor level for training data contributed by workers (Sandoval, 2020).

3. **Revenue sharing for data contributors.** Commercial models trained on commons data share a defined percentage of revenue with the organizations and individuals whose data was used. The mechanism may be a data trust (following Delacroix & Lawrence's 2019 bottom-up trust framework), a cooperative, or a statutory levy. The Wikimedia Foundation's ongoing discussions with AI companies about data-use terms and attribution — active through 2024–2025 — represent an initial institutional movement toward this principle, though no revenue-sharing framework had been publicly confirmed as of this writing (Wikimedia Foundation, 2023; the previously cited Wikimedia Foundation (2024b) "Apple partnership" press release cannot be verified in the Foundation's news archive and has been removed pending author confirmation); operator models like Spawning.ai's Do Not Train Registry provide the technical infrastructure (Longpre et al., 2024).

4. **Fork rights for communities.** Communities whose data is used in a model retain the right to fork — to withdraw their data and develop alternative models under different governance. This disciplines the exercise of platform power in the same way that fork rights discipline open source project governance. The OSI Open Source AI Definition's 'Data Information' clause (OSI, 2024) — which requires documentation sufficient for a skilled person to recreate a substantially equivalent system while explicitly permitting non-public training datasets, a provision widely criticised for not requiring actual training data release — operationalizes a partial version of this principle at the technical level.

5. **Capability access as a commons right.** AI systems that are trained on commons data should provide capability access to the commons that funded them — not merely through open weights, but through computing subsidies, API access, or community deployments. OpenAI's researcher access program and Anthropic's nonprofit API discount are partial models (OpenAI, 2024; Anthropic, 2024) — programs that reach primarily credentialled academic researchers rather than the volunteer editors, open-source maintainers, and commons contributors who generated the training data, and that do not reverse the structural concentration of AI compute and talent in a small number of large private actors (Ahmed & Wahed, 2020); full implementation would guarantee that communities contributing training data have subsidized access equivalent to for-profit users.

## 5. Objections and responses

**Objection: Open source licenses already permit commercial use — contributors agreed to this.**
Response: License terms governing code reuse were written before large-scale ML training was technically possible. The legal permission is not equivalent to normative consent, and the distributional consequences were not legible to contributors at the time of contribution.

**Objection: Requiring revenue sharing will kill open source.**
Response: The concern conflates two different obligations. Revenue sharing from AI training is not the same as restricting open source licenses. A company may use Linux under GPL and still agree to compensate Wikipedia for AI training use — these are separable legal and normative relationships.

**Objection: Hybrid intelligence is too diverse a category — some HI systems widen the gap, some narrow it.**
Response: Correct. The argument is not that hybrid intelligence necessarily redistributes value, but that it can be designed to do so, and that the design principles above are what distinguish commons-redistributive from commons-extractive implementations.
