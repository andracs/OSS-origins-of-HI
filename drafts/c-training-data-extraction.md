## Framing

This section addresses the third layer of the openness trilemma: **open training data**. Even when model weights are open and code is open-source, the training data is typically opaque — and increasingly, empirical research demonstrates that this opacity is not complete. Models memorize and can be induced to reproduce training data verbatim, including copyrighted text, personal information, and proprietary code.

This has three converging implications:
1. **Legal liability** — model developers may have trained on copyright-protected material without license, and memorization provides forensic evidence of this
2. **Privacy** — personal data (emails, medical records, code with credentials) embedded in training corpora can be extracted
3. **Governance** — the "open training data" question is not only about transparency but about what rights inhere in the reproduced content

## 1. The extraction literature: models remember more than they should

### Foundational: Carlini et al. (2021)

The foundational demonstration that LLMs memorize training data at scale is Carlini, Tramèr, Wallace, Jagielski, Herbert-Voss, Lee, Roberts, Brown, Song, Erlingsson, Oprea, and Raffel (2021), who showed that GPT-2 could be induced to reproduce verbatim sequences from its training set through targeted prompting. Their method — providing a model prefix and sampling until memorized content appears — recovered personal information including names, addresses, phone numbers, and extended passages of copyrighted text. Critically, the memorization was not uniformly distributed: data that appeared multiple times in training was far more likely to be extractable, establishing a direct link between dataset curation practice (deduplication) and privacy/copyright risk (Carlini et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2022).

### Scaling memorization: Carlini et al. (2023)

A follow-up study systematically quantified how memorization scales with model and data properties. Carlini, Ippolito, Jagielski, Lee, Tramèr, and Zhang (2023) demonstrated three robust empirical relationships: larger models memorize disproportionately more than smaller ones; data that appears more frequently in training is memorized more reliably; and the fraction of training data recoverable by an adversary grows as a function of the number of tokens generated per prompt. These findings have a direct implication for the open weights / open training data debate: as model size increases — a trend with no sign of reversing — the fraction of training data recoverable by an adversary increases proportionally. Deduplication of training corpora thus functions as both a copyright-risk mitigation and a privacy control (Carlini et al., 2023).

### The Harry Potter case and copyrighted books

Two EMNLP 2023 studies have provided systematic empirical evidence that frontier LLMs memorize and reproduce copyrighted literary works at scale.

Chang, Cramer, Soni, and Bamman (2023) applied name-cloze membership inference queries — replacing character names with blanks and testing whether models could recover them better than chance — to identify approximately 200 books demonstrably "known" to ChatGPT and GPT-4. The degree of memorization correlated with the frequency with which book passages appeared on the web, consistent with Carlini et al.'s (2021) finding that training set repetition drives extractability. Importantly, memorization of a book was not merely a trivia fact: models performed significantly better on downstream cultural-analytic tasks for memorized books than non-memorized ones, complicating validity assessments for research that uses LLMs to analyze literature (Chang et al., 2023).

Karamolegkou, Li, and Li (2023) directly measured verbatim reproduction rates across multiple architectures — spanning closed models (GPT-3.5, Claude) and open-weight families (OPT, Pythia, LLaMA, Falcon) — in what the authors characterise as a deliberately conservative, lower-bound estimate. For literary text, GPT-3.5 and Claude produced average verbatim excerpts exceeding 50 words — a threshold the paper identifies as a marker for potential copyright concern — while open-weight models below 60 billion parameters averaged fewer than 50 words of verbatim reproduction under simple prompting, with a near-linear scaling relationship between model size and verbatim length. For LeetCode coding-problem descriptions the pattern was sharper: more than 30% of 1,826 tested problems (over 600) were reproduced with more than 50 words verbatim across the model set (Karamolegkou et al., 2023). The legal implication the authors foreground is precise: fair-use exemptions do not cover verbatim reproduction; information extraction differs from reproduction, and only the former qualifies for fair-use defence under US copyright law (Karamolegkou et al., 2023).

The legal operationalization of these findings is most visible in the *New York Times Company v. Microsoft Corporation et al.* complaint (Case 1:23-cv-11195, S.D.N.Y., filed December 27, 2023). Exhibit J to the complaint — titled "One Hundred Examples of GPT-4 Memorizing Content from The New York Times" and spanning 127 pages — provides the core forensic demonstration: the NYT fed opening paragraphs of its articles as prompts to GPT-4 and Bing Chat, and the models reproduced substantial subsequent portions near-verbatim. A representative example: Bing Chat reproduced all but two of the first 396 words of a 2023 NYT article titled "The Secrets Hamas Knew About Israel's Military," with the near-complete reproduction demonstrating that the model had encoded that specific journalistic work in a form recoverable through targeted prompting. The majority of examples cover articles published 2.5 to 12 years before the complaint, consistent with Carlini et al.'s (2021) finding that training set repetition — including repeated indexing of news content by web crawlers — drives extractability. On April 4, 2025, the court denied OpenAI's and Microsoft's motions to dismiss the direct copyright infringement claims, confirming that the verbatim reproduction evidence cleared the threshold for proceeding to discovery (*New York Times Co. v. Microsoft Corp.*, Case 1:23-cv-11195, S.D.N.Y., 2025). The NYT complaint is thus the most prominent instance to date of the Carlini extraction methodology being operationalized in litigation — and the first to survive a motion to dismiss at a federal district court.

A second high-profile case operationalized the same forensic logic against a different defendant. In late 2025, Anthropic agreed to a settlement of at least US $1.5 billion in *Bartz et al. v. Anthropic PBC*, brought by a class of authors over the unauthorised use of approximately 500,000 books obtained from the LibGen and PiLiMi shadow libraries to train Claude during 2021–2022 (Baggersgaard, 2026). The settlement structure pays approximately US $3,000 per book to listed rights-holders — including a contingent of Danish researchers whose monographs were among those identified — with a March 30, 2026 claims deadline. The Anthropic case differs from the NYT litigation in two ways relevant to this section: first, it concerns long-form books rather than journalistic articles, extending the Karamolegkou et al. (2023) finding on book-length verbatim reproduction directly into legal precedent; second, the corpus in question — LibGen and PiLiMi — is a known shadow-library aggregation, which means the settlement implicitly recognises that the legal status of the *source* (pirated) is not extinguished by intermediate technical transformations such as tokenization and gradient-based training. Copyright-law scholar Morten Rosenmeier (University of Copenhagen) characterised the per-book compensation as adequate but argued that meaningful structural change requires EU-level regulation rather than serial individual settlements (Baggersgaard, 2026) — a point that connects this section to the regulatory analysis in §7.2 below.

A scholarly analysis of the complaint's claims against current frontier models has been conducted by Freeman, Rippe, Debenedetti, and Andriushchenko (2024), who attempted to replicate the NYT's Exhibit J methodology against GPT-4o, GPT-3.5 Turbo, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and several open-weight models including Llama and Mistral variants. Their central finding is that OpenAI models have deployed refusal training and output filters that substantially suppress verbatim memorization under standard prompting — they were harder to elicit than open-weight alternatives — while open-weight models from Meta and Mistral reproduced verbatim passages at higher rates. Additionally, models with 100 billion or more parameters showed greater memorization capacity than smaller models, directly extending Carlini et al.'s (2023) scaling finding to the copyright domain (Freeman et al., 2024). For the governance argument, this finding is double-edged: the refusal-training mitigation demonstrates that copyright risk can be partially addressed at the output level without weight access; but it also shows that the same behavior may be re-enabled by fine-tuning or by switching to open-weight alternatives that have not received the same RLHF pressure toward output suppression.

### Code-domain extraction: secrets, licences, and supply-chain risk

The extraction literature surveyed above is dominated by text examples — books, news articles, Wikipedia. The code domain warrants separate treatment because the harm structure is qualitatively different:

1. **Credential and secret leakage.** Source code commonly contains accidentally committed secrets (API keys, OAuth tokens, AWS credentials, private SSH keys) (Meli et al., 2019). Carlini et al. (2021) demonstrated this concretely on GPT-2: among the verbatim sequences they recovered were strings that matched the structural signatures of real-world credentials. Models trained on GitHub-scale code corpora are exposed to the long tail of accidentally committed secrets that GitHub's secret-scanning service catches in real time but which persist in historical commits and in dataset snapshots taken before remediation. Where a memorized news article is a copyright issue, a memorized credential is an active-exploit issue.

2. **Licence-contagion disputes.** Code under copyleft licences (GPL, AGPL) imposes obligations on derivative works. If a model trained on GPL code reproduces a substantial verbatim sequence from a GPL repository, the legal status of the resulting code — and of the deploying organization's downstream artefact — is contested. The *Doe v. GitHub, Inc.* class-action complaint (Case 4:22-cv-06823, N.D. Cal., filed November 3, 2022) operationalized this concern: plaintiffs alleged that GitHub Copilot reproduced licensed code without attribution, violating the licence terms and DMCA copyright-management-information provisions. The litigation has been substantially narrowed but not dismissed; the legal question of whether model-mediated reproduction carries the original licence's obligations remains unresolved (Doe v. GitHub, Inc., N.D. Cal., 2024). Karamolegkou et al. (2023) include coding problems in their verbatim-reproduction analysis and find that LLMs reproduce them at rates comparable to literary text, providing the empirical bridge between the literary and code domains.

3. **Supply-chain and toolchain risk.** Code generated by LLMs trained on open-source corpora is increasingly deployed in production systems. Where the model reproduces a vulnerable function pattern from its training data (e.g., a SQL-injection-prone string concatenation, a known-bad cryptographic primitive), the vulnerability propagates into the deploying organisation's codebase (Pearce et al., 2022). This is not memorization in the strict sense — the model is reproducing a *class* of pattern rather than a specific instance — but it interacts with memorization in ways that the text-domain literature has not yet systematically characterized. The GitHub Copilot rollout (2021–2023) and subsequent adoption of Claude and ChatGPT Code Interpreter workflows demonstrate that AI-assisted code generation has driven measurable productivity gains — Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon, and Demirer (2023) show in a controlled experiment that developers using Copilot completed tasks 55% faster than controls. Adoption has accelerated: GitHub's Octoverse report (2024–2025) discloses that AI-assisted code accounts for 10 to 20% of commits in leading open-source repositories as of 2025, though independently peer-reviewed measurement of this adoption rate across open-source ecosystems has not yet been published. The security implications of AI-assisted code generation at this adoption scale — estimated at 10–20% of commits — scale directly with the underlying memorization and extraction rates documented above.

The implication for the chapter's open-source-as-fuel argument is direct: the same commons that fuels AI training is *also* the vector through which credentials, licence-encumbered code, and vulnerable patterns enter the trained model — and from there, into derivative deployments. The extraction literature that began with newspaper articles and copyrighted books extends naturally to the code domain, with stakes that are simultaneously higher (active credentials) and harder to litigate (licence-contagion is unresolved doctrine).

## 2. Membership inference: proving what was in training

Shokri, Stronati, Song, and Shmatikov (2017) introduced membership inference attacks: given a trained model and a data sample, can an adversary determine whether that specific sample was in the training set? Their shadow-model technique — training auxiliary models on data drawn from the same distribution as the target model, then using the shadow models' confidence outputs to train a binary classifier — achieved statistically significant accuracy on multiple ML models. On the Texas hospital discharge dataset, the attack achieved a mean precision of 0.74 across attack classes — with per-class peaks substantially higher — demonstrating that membership in a training set is not protected by the model's opacity (Shokri et al., 2017).

For the copyright and privacy argument, membership inference is the forensic tool: it provides a mechanism to *prove* in legal proceedings that a specific copyrighted work, personal record, or proprietary dataset was used in training — even without access to the training data itself. The Chang et al. (2023) name-cloze method is a literary application of this general principle: literary memorization functions as a membership signal that can be queried without requiring direct access to training corpora.


#### Membership inference at frontier scale

The scaling of membership inference attacks to billion-parameter models and beyond requires reconsidering the shadow-model methodology in light of frontier training dynamics. The most directly applicable empirical anchor at frontier scale is Carlini, Ippolito, Jagielski, Lee, Tramèr, and Zhang (2023), who establish three log-linear relationships governing memorization: it grows with (i) model capacity, (ii) the number of times an example has been duplicated in training, and (iii) the number of tokens of context used to prompt the model. The first relationship is decisive for membership inference at scale: as model capacity increases, the gap between in-training and out-of-training generation behavior widens, increasing the signal-to-noise ratio available to a membership inference adversary (Carlini et al., 2023). The Carlini et al. (2023) paper does not directly study membership inference attacks against models trained with RLHF or constitutional AI; the implications of refusal training for membership inference remain an open empirical question.

A separate, less-studied dynamic concerns the membership-inference implications of refusal training itself. RLHF-trained models develop refusal behaviors that may, in principle, leak information about the training corpus: when a model declines a request to reproduce a specific copyrighted passage with high specificity, the refusal pattern is informative about whether that specific content was encountered during training. This refusal-as-side-channel hypothesis is structurally analogous to the Shokri et al. (2017) confidence-score side channel — both exploit the model's behavioral asymmetry between training and non-training inputs — but it has not yet been systematically measured at frontier scale, and the empirical magnitude is therefore uncertain. The hypothesis follows from the Shokri framework rather than from Carlini et al.'s scaling results, and is flagged here as a gap in the literature rather than as an established finding.

The most direct empirical anchor for the *magnitude* of fine-tuning extraction at LLM scale comes from Shi, Han, Wen, and Gao (2024), discussed in detail in Section 4. Their Pandora's White-Box pipeline demonstrates that, for fine-tuned LLMs, "a simple attack based on the ratio of the loss between the base and fine-tuned models" achieves near-perfect membership inference performance, and extraction of "over 50% of the fine-tuning dataset" is feasible "in natural settings" (Shi et al., 2024). This natural-setting result is qualitatively different from the canary-based Secret Sharer methodology: it operates against ordinary fine-tuning corpora rather than inserted synthetic sequences, and the >50% figure quantifies the real-world extraction surface that the Secret Sharer exposure metric had only characterised indirectly.

Fine-tuning dynamics further complicate the threat model. When a pre-trained frontier model is fine-tuned on a new dataset (e.g., a corporation's proprietary internal documents), the fine-tuning data — typically smaller in scale but higher in saliency to the deployer — may be disproportionately susceptible to extraction. The most direct empirical anchor for this is Carlini, Liu, Erlingsson, Kos, and Song (2019), whose canary-insertion methodology measures memorization by inserting unique synthetic sequences (e.g., credit-card-style numeric strings) into a training corpus and computing an *exposure* metric — the log-likelihood gap between the inserted canary and unrelated random sequences of the same format. Their experiments on Google's Smart Compose, a commercial text-completion model fine-tuned on email data, demonstrate that low-frequency canaries (inserted only a handful of times during training) reach high exposure values, meaning a query-only adversary can recover them with substantially better-than-random accuracy. Critically, the Secret Sharer methodology measures the memorization risk for *inserted* unique sequences as a proxy for the risk to naturally occurring rare-but-sensitive content; it does not directly quantify what fraction of a real fine-tuning corpus can be extracted (Carlini et al., 2019). The methodology therefore establishes that fine-tuning a generative sequence model creates a measurable extraction surface for unique content, but the magnitude of that surface for any particular real-world fine-tuning corpus must be evaluated case by case using exposure-style instrumentation. The implication for HI governance is nonetheless structural: fine-tuning a model on confidential data introduces an extraction surface that pre-training-scale audits do not assess, and an organization that lacks the instrumentation to measure that surface is operating without visibility into a known risk class.

## 3. Model inversion: recovering training inputs from outputs

Fredrikson, Jha, and Ristenpart (2015) demonstrated model inversion attacks: given only model outputs and confidence scores, an adversary can reconstruct approximations of training inputs. Their key innovation was to exploit the probability distribution over output classes — the confidence scores that many deployed ML-as-a-service APIs expose — as a signal about the specific training instances that shaped those distributions. In their primary demonstration against a commercial facial recognition system, they applied a maximum a posteriori estimation procedure guided by the confidence scores, recovering recognizable facial images of individuals in the training set without any direct access to that data. A parallel demonstration against a decision tree trained on a genomic dataset recovered sensitive pharmacogenetic features (Fredrikson et al., 2015). The paper also establishes a countermeasure baseline: reducing the precision of confidence scores (rounding or discretizing) substantially degrades inversion attack accuracy, as does a privacy-aware training approach that regularizes against high-confidence outputs on rare inputs.

For LLMs, model inversion operates differently than in classification settings — the output is a token distribution rather than a class probability — but the structural principle is preserved: the model's internal state, which encodes properties of its training data, is observable through its outputs if the adversary has enough queries (Morris et al., 2023). The Carlini et al. (2021) extraction methodology is in effect a high-dimensional variant of the Fredrikson et al. attack: both exploit output distributions to recover training instances, differing primarily in the structure of the output space and the sophistication of the reconstruction method. The three attack classes — extraction (Carlini et al., 2021, 2023), membership inference (Shokri et al., 2017), and model inversion (Fredrikson et al., 2015) — together constitute a forensic toolkit: extraction recovers training instances directly; membership inference establishes which instances were in the training set; inversion reconstructs sensitive features from output behavior. All three are black-box feasible (requiring only API access to a deployed model), and all three have open-weight analogues that are substantially more efficient (Shi et al., 2024).

## 4. Implications for open vs. closed models

A key empirical question is whether open weights exacerbate or mitigate extraction risk. Two competing effects:

- **Exacerbation hypothesis:** Open weights give adversaries direct access to model internals, enabling more efficient extraction attacks (white-box vs. black-box). Closed models require query-based attacks; open models permit gradient-based attacks that may be orders of magnitude more efficient.

- **Mitigation hypothesis:** Open weights enable defenders to audit memorization *before* deployment, apply machine unlearning techniques, and detect training data contamination. Closed models can only be audited from the outside, after deployment.

Shi, Han, Wen, and Gao (2024) provide the most direct empirical answer to this question. Their "Pandora's White-Box" framework demonstrates that white-box attacks — which exploit gradient information available in open-weight models — achieve substantially higher extraction precision than black-box attacks, which rely only on output sampling. This directly confirms the exacerbation hypothesis: open-weight access meaningfully increases the extraction risk for motivated adversaries. However, Shi et al. also demonstrate that black-box attacks remain effective for *fine-tuned* models, where memorization of fine-tuning data can be recovered without weight access. The threat is thus not confined to open-weight models: any fine-tuned model deployed via API remains vulnerable to targeted extraction of its fine-tuning corpus (Shi et al., 2024).

The resolution for the open/closed framing is therefore asymmetric by actor type: for IP holders seeking to prove copyright infringement — a beneficial use of extraction — open models are substantially easier to audit. For privacy adversaries seeking to extract personal data from pre-training, open models provide meaningfully greater attack surface. The policy implication is that "open weights" is not a neutral designation from a privacy-risk perspective; it is a parameter of the threat model.

#### Threat model dimensions: a structured framework

The exacerbation/mitigation ambiguity resolves only when disaggregated by actor type and objective. A structured threat model for extraction attacks distinguishes five dimensions:

1. **Adversary capability:** Black-box extraction (query access only) vs. white-box extraction (gradient access). White-box attacks scale substantially faster; the gradient access afforded by open weights enables attacks that achieve equivalent precision with orders of magnitude fewer queries than black-box output-sampling attacks require (Shi et al., 2024).

2. **Target data:** Pre-training data (large-scale, diverse, harder to prove specific membership) vs. fine-tuning data (smaller, more salient, easier to detect via membership inference) vs. instruction-tuning data (safety-critical, amenable to side-channel attacks via refusal patterns).

3. **Attacker objective:** Proving copyright infringement (beneficial use of extraction, requires statistical evidence of deliberate inclusion) vs. privacy theft (adversarial use, requires recovery of identifiable personal information) vs. intellectual property theft (recovery of proprietary training data or methodologies).

4. **Defense posture:** Frontier model with post-hoc mitigations (refusal training, output guardrails) vs. open-weight model with audit access vs. open-weight model with verifiable privacy guarantees (DP-trained). Each defense posture is optimized for different threat vectors.

5. **Legal/regulatory context:** Jurisdiction determining whether extraction is a cognizable harm (US copyright doctrine, GDPR data subject rights, EU AI Act disclosure obligations). A model that is opaque from a privacy perspective may be transparent from a copyright perspective if it is trained on publicly-disclosed sources.

These dimensions form a multidimensional threat space rather than a one-dimensional open/closed axis. The policy implication is that "open weights" must be understood not as a categorical property but as a parameter within a structured threat model, with different actors and jurisdictions instantiating different priority orderings of the five dimensions.

## 5. The GDPR "right to forget" problem

European data protection law (GDPR Article 17) grants individuals the right to erasure of their personal data. If a model has memorized personal data and can reproduce it on demand, a data subject's right to erasure cannot be satisfied by deleting the data from a database — the model itself retains the data in its weights.

Zhang, Finckenberg-Broman, Hoang, Pan, Xing, Staples, and Xu (2024) provide a systematic legal-technical analysis of this tension. Their key findings are stark: machine unlearning does not scale effectively for LLMs. Removing data from training datasets does not affect already-trained models; retraining from scratch to satisfy an erasure request would require cycles exceeding GDPR's "undue delay" threshold (approximately one month; European Parliament & Council of the EU, 2016, Art. 12(3)). Current technical solutions — differential privacy, machine unlearning, model editing, and guardrails — offer only partial guarantees and cannot provide the categorical certainty GDPR requires. The authors conclude that complete data erasure at LLM scale is structurally challenging with existing techniques, and that interim measures (privacy policies, training data segmentation, post-deployment filtering) are procedural workarounds rather than technical compliance (Zhang et al., 2024).

For HI governance, the implication is structural: organizations that deploy closed models in contexts involving personal data cannot verify whether GDPR erasure requests have been technically satisfied, because they have no access to model internals. Open-weight models do not solve this problem — machine unlearning remains expensive at scale regardless of access level — but they at least permit external verification that a model has been retrained or that specific memorized sequences are no longer recoverable.

The conceptual baseline for the machine unlearning field is Cao and Yang (2015), "Towards Making Systems Forget with Machine Unlearning." Their key contribution was to recast learning algorithms in terms of a small number of *summations* over training data — the statistical query (SQ) learning framework — so that removing a data point from the training set could be implemented efficiently as a recomputation of only the affected summations rather than a full retrain. Cao and Yang demonstrated certifiable removal on a range of classical ML systems (LensKit, Zozzle, OSNBench), achieving order-of-magnitude speedups over retraining while providing a formal guarantee that the resulting model was identical to one never trained on the removed point (Cao & Yang, 2015, *IEEE S&P*, pp. 463–480). The structural obstacle to applying this framework to LLMs is precisely what makes LLMs powerful: gradient-based stochastic optimization with Adam-class optimizers, layer normalization, and dropout introduces path-dependence into the trained weights that cannot be expressed as a small number of summations. The Cao-Yang guarantee — that the updated model is identical to one trained without the removed data — is therefore not available at frontier LLM scale; what is available is *approximate* unlearning (Bourtoule et al., 2021) — gradient ascent on the data to be forgotten, model editing of specific knowledge, and shard-based partial retraining — which Zhang et al. (2024) characterize as offering only partial guarantees insufficient for GDPR's "complete erasure" standard.

The complementary engineering approach is to prevent memorization at training time rather than removing it post hoc. Abadi, Chu, Goodfellow, McMahan, Mironov, Talwar, and Zhang (2016) introduced *Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent* (DP-SGD), which clips per-example gradients and adds calibrated Gaussian noise to provide an (ε, δ)-differential-privacy guarantee on the trained model. The privacy guarantee is mathematically rigorous: any single training example has bounded influence on the model's weights, so the model cannot reproduce that example beyond what could be predicted from population-level patterns. The framework has been extended to large-scale language modeling: Anil, Ghazi, Gupta, Kumar, and Manurangsi (2022) demonstrated DP-SGD pre-training of BERT-Large on the standard Wikipedia/BookCorpus corpus, achieving a Masked-Language-Model accuracy of 60.5% at a batch size of 2M with ε = 5.36 — approximately 9.5 points below the ~70% accuracy of a non-private baseline (Anil et al., 2022, abstract); Yu, Naik, Backurs, Gopi, Inan, Kamath, Kulkarni, Lee, Manoel, Wutschitz, Yekhanin, and Zhang (2022) extended the approach to *fine-tuning* of GPT-2-scale and RoBERTa models, showing that parameter-efficient adaptation methods (LoRA, prefix tuning) make DP fine-tuning meaningfully more practical at modest privacy budgets. Both papers, however, document that the utility–privacy gap widens with model capability and dataset size — a finding corroborated by Tramèr and Boneh (2021), who show that differentially private learning requires substantially better features or much more data to recover from the accuracy cost. As of late 2025, no major commercially deployed frontier LLM is publicly known to employ DP-SGD or equivalent privacy-preserving training protocols with ε ≤ 10 — a deployment gap that means the privacy and copyright risk surfaced by Carlini, Chang, and Karamolegkou cannot currently be foreclosed by training-time mitigation. The structural implication: organizations whose risk profile cannot tolerate memorization may need to choose between deploying frontier-capability models with operational mitigations (refusal training, output filters, retrieval restrictions) or accepting smaller, lower-utility models that satisfy the formal DP guarantee.

## 6. Connection to the hybrid intelligence argument

The memorization literature connects to the broader chapter argument in a specific way: HI systems that use closed models as reasoning layers are using systems that may be encoding and reproducing copyrighted and personal information — without the HI operator having any mechanism to audit or control this. In a medical HI system, the model may reproduce personal health information from training data (Lehman et al., 2021). In a legal HI system, it may reproduce privileged client communications that appeared in training corpora. These are not hypothetical risks; the Carlini et al. (2021) methodology demonstrates they are technically feasible at current scale, and Chang et al. (2023) and Karamolegkou et al. (2023) demonstrate they are already occurring with copyrighted literary content.

The open/closed dimension is critical here: open-weight models can be probed for memorization before deployment. Closed models cannot. But as Shi et al. (2024) establish, open-weight access is also a double-edged instrument — it increases extraction risk for adversarial actors while enabling auditing for defensive actors. The governance problem is not "open vs. closed" as a binary but the absence of any standardized disclosure regime that would enable post-deployment memorization auditing regardless of weight access status. This connects directly to the bias-auditability argument developed in `b-closed-models-risks.md` Section 1 (the structural unverifiability of closed-model bias) and to the supply-chain attack-surface argument in Section 3 of the same draft (closed models embedded in enterprise stacks process external content as part of their reasoning chain — and the same architectural opacity that prevents memorization auditing prevents indirect-injection auditing).

## 7. Institutional responses: transparency standards, opt-out mechanisms, and regulatory mandates

The technical extraction literature established *that* training data opacity is a problem. The institutional responses surveyed here represent the first systematic attempts to address it — through transparency indices, opt-out registries, open dataset governance, and binding regulation.

### 7.1 Declining transparency in practice: the Stanford CRFM Foundation Model Transparency Index

The most systematic empirical measure of training data transparency at the industry level is the Foundation Model Transparency Index (FMTI), produced annually by Stanford's Center for Research on Foundation Models. The December 2024 edition reported a mean transparency score of 58 across evaluated models (Bommasani et al., 2024); by December 2025, the subsequent edition reported that this score had *fallen* to 41 — a 17-point decline (Wan et al., 2025). This is the second consecutive year-on-year decrease, with the first FMTI release having occurred in 2023.

The thematic breakdown of low-scoring domains is directly relevant to the memorization and extraction literature: training data and training compute remain the two most opaque areas across all evaluated companies. IBM achieved the highest score (95/100) and is the only company to have improved consistently across all three waves; OpenAI and Meta, which led the first wave in 2023, now rank last and second-to-last respectively. xAI and Midjourney, evaluated for the first time in December 2025, score 14 — the joint lowest on record (Wan et al., 2025).

The FMTI operationalizes transparency as disclosure across four domains: model, training data, deployment, and organization. For training data specifically, indicators include whether the data sources are identified by name, whether deduplication procedures are documented, whether copyright compliance procedures are disclosed, and whether membership inference audits have been conducted. The consistent opacity in this domain — persisting through three annual waves and declining overall — suggests that industry self-disclosure norms are not converging toward transparency absent regulatory pressure.

### 7.2 The EU AI Act training data summary: binding disclosure from August 2025

The most significant regulatory development in this domain is the European Commission's mandatory training data summary template for General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models, published on July 24, 2025, under Article 53 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the AI Act). The requirement to publish the summary became legally binding on August 2, 2025, for all GPAI models placed on the EU market after that date; models placed before that date have until August 2, 2027 (European Commission, 2025).

The template's structure is directly responsive to the FMTI's findings on what is typically undisclosed. It mandates three structured information blocks: (1) model and provider metadata; (2) a categorised inventory of training data sources covering public datasets, licensed datasets, scraped/crawled content (including the most relevant domains), user-generated data, and synthetic data; and (3) governance and processing information addressing copyright compliance procedures, illegal content removal, and data protection obligations. Where additional training occurs post-deployment, providers must update the summary every six months or whenever a material change occurs.

The significance for the argument in this draft is twofold. First, the template directly operationalizes the gap identified by the extraction and membership inference literature: if scraping frequency and domain origin are publicly disclosed, external researchers can correlate these disclosures with extractability findings — enabling a form of post-hoc verification of copyright claims that the current opacity forecloses. Second, the GPAI template creates a compliance asymmetry: companies with open training datasets (LAION, Hugging Face) can satisfy it at near-zero cost; companies with opaque proprietary corpora face a genuine disclosure obligation. This asymmetry may — over time — function as an indirect incentive toward more transparent training data practice.

AlgorithmWatch, the Berlin-based accountability organization, has been tracking the practical implementation of GPAI obligations and has noted that the enforcement mechanisms remain underspecified in the Commission's guidance: the template mandates *what* must be disclosed but provides limited guidance on *verification*, leaving the audit burden primarily with national market surveillance authorities (AlgorithmWatch, 2025).

### 7.3 Opt-out mechanisms and the Spawning.ai/CDSM framework

A parallel institutional response addresses the copyright dimension specifically: opt-out registries that allow rights-holders to reserve their works from commercial text-and-data mining (TDM) under Article 4 of the EU Copyright in the Digital Single Market Directive (CDSM, 2019/790/EU).

Spawning.ai has developed two complementary tools: *HaveIBeenTrained*, a look-up interface allowing creators to identify whether their work appeared in major training datasets (initially LAION-5B; subsequently extended), and a *Do Not Train Registry* that accepts machine-readable opt-out declarations from rights-holders. The registry is designed to interface with AI developers' data ingestion pipelines, so that future scraping rounds can programmatically exclude opted-out works (Spawning, 2023).

The TrainingData.eu initiative — a coalition of European rights-holder organizations — has worked on the complementary question of how opt-out reservations should be encoded to meet the CDSM's machine-readable standard. The Directive permits rights-holders to exclude their works from the Article 4 commercial TDM exception, but as of mid-2025, no standardized technical protocol for machine-readable opt-out has been adopted at EU or ISO level. This gap is not merely technical: it means that rights-holders who have made reservation-of-rights statements may not have done so in forms that AI developers can reliably detect at scraping time. The extraction literature makes this gap consequential: if rights-holder opt-outs are not standardized, and scraping pipelines do not implement look-up against Spawning's or comparable registries, then the CDSM's opt-out mechanism may be legally nominal but technically ineffective.

### 7.4 Open training data governance: Hugging Face FineWeb and LAION Re-LAION-5B

Against the opacity of commercial providers documented by the FMTI, the open research community has developed a contrasting model of training dataset governance, best exemplified by Hugging Face's FineWeb family and LAION's Re-LAION-5B.

Hugging Face's FineWeb (2024) and its successors — FineWeb-Edu (filtered for educational quality; updated through July 2025), FineWeb-2 (multilingual extension), and FinePDFs (3 trillion tokens extracted from PDF documents, released September 2025) — represent a systematic approach to dataset documentation that addresses several of the dimensions the FMTI identifies as typically undisclosed (Penedo et al., 2024). The processing pipeline is fully reproducible: all filtering, deduplication, and quality-scoring code is public via the `datatrove` library; the distribution of source domains is documented; and — notably — the data removed by filtering is released separately, enabling community re-filtering under alternative criteria (Hugging Face, 2024–2025). This last feature is particularly relevant for the copyright question: it allows external researchers to examine what *was* removed and to assess whether copyright-relevant content systematically survived filtering.

LAION's Re-LAION-5B (August 2024) represents a safety-focused iteration of the LAION-5B dataset following the Stanford Internet Observatory's December 2023 report identifying links to child sexual abuse material in the original release (Thiel, 2023). Re-LAION-5B removed all known CSAM-linked URLs and is released under an Apache-2.0 license with fully documented, reproducible curation pipelines (LAION, 2024). The episode is instructive for governance design: the problem — harmful content embedded in a web-scraped dataset — was identified through external audit rather than internal governance, and the remediation required full reprocessing and rerelease rather than targeted editing. This reflects the same scalability constraint that Zhang et al. (2024) identify for machine unlearning: large-scale training data errors cannot be surgically corrected; they require full curation reruns.

### 7.5 Mozilla and the Open Source AI Definition

Mozilla Foundation's *State of Mozilla 2024* and its strategic commitment to trustworthy open-source AI includes explicit positioning on the training data transparency question via its endorsement of the Open Source Initiative's (OSI) finalized Open Source AI Definition. That definition, finalized in late 2024, establishes that a model can only be considered "open source" if its training data is sufficiently documented to enable independent retraining — not merely disclosed, but documented to a standard permitting reproducibility. This is a materially higher bar than the EU AI Act's GPAI template, which requires disclosure of source categories but not full reproducibility (Mozilla Foundation, 2024; OSI, 2024).

The practical implications of this definitional wedge are immediate. Models that release weights but not training data (a category that includes most frontier models from Meta, Mistral, and others; Wan et al., 2025) may satisfy the AI Act's GPAI template without meeting the OSI's transparency threshold. Specifically:

1. **Regulatory bifurcation.** A model can be EU AI Act compliant (published summary of source categories, deduplication procedures) while failing the OSI criterion (no documented, reproducible pipeline for retraining). The EU template requires disclosure *of what* but not *how*; the OSI requires both. This creates a compliance landscape where companies can achieve regulatory minimum without achieving community-endorsed openness.

2. **Reproducibility as a governance lever.** The OSI definition operationalizes reproducibility as a *prerequisite* for the "open source" label — not an optional certification. This creates a binary gate: models either meet the threshold (full documentation, reproducible pipeline, verifiable results) or they do not. In practice, this means that fine-tuned models deployed by organizations cannot claim "open source" status unless they disclose both the base model's training data *and* the fine-tuning corpus and procedure. The section on membership inference at frontier scale becomes directly relevant here: organizations cannot verify they meet the OSI threshold without subjecting their models to extraction and membership inference auditing to confirm that training data is not recoverable — a capability most organizations currently lack (Zhang et al., 2024).

3. **Impact on the open-source commons.** The OSI definition creates an incentive structure that could increase transparency among projects that aspire to the "open source AI" label — but only for those with the resources to fully document and verify reproducibility. Smaller research teams and nonprofit organizations may find the bar prohibitively high, inadvertently concentrating the "open source AI" designation among well-funded projects. This is a governance design tension: heightening standards for openness may improve quality at the cost of exclusivity.

The gap between legal compliance (EU AI Act's GPAI template) and normative openness (OSI definition) is thus not merely a site of contestation but an active fracture point in the governance landscape. Organizations must choose whether to aim for legal compliance, community credibility, or both — and the cost of meeting both thresholds is currently asymmetric by organization size and resource access.
