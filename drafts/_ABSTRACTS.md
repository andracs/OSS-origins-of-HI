## Draft A: The Open Source Timeline: From Individuals to Connected Communities to Agent Swarms

This chapter traces the evolution of open source software from its informal origins in academic computing through the formation of global collaborative communities, and into the present era in which AI coding agents are themselves becoming contributors to the repositories that once trained them. Structured around three stages — accumulation, mining and refinement, and augmentation with feedback — the chapter argues that the open source commons constitutes a form of collective intelligence whose aggregate value was never anticipated by its contributors. Drawing on the Bartók analogy for folk-melody collection, motivational research on contributor behavior (Lakhani & Wolf, 2005), and Dawkins's memetic framework for understanding evolutionary dynamics in collaborative repositories, the chapter establishes the historical and conceptual foundation for understanding how open source code became the primary training substrate for modern AI systems and why this transition introduces qualitatively new recursive dynamics.

## Draft B: Closed Models as Societal Risk Infrastructure

This chapter analyzes the systemic risks introduced when foundational AI models are deployed in closed, non-auditable configurations at societal scale. Four risk vectors are examined: bias without external auditability, hidden alignment to commercial incentives, security vulnerabilities in concentrated supply chains, and epistemic gatekeeping in information retrieval. Drawing on empirical research including cross-model bias comparison studies, the documented sycophancy failure of GPT-4o in 2025, indirect prompt injection attacks, and measurable output monoculture under RLHF alignment pressure, the chapter argues that closed deployment is not a neutral architectural choice but a structural concentration of epistemic and infrastructural power. The risks compound specifically in hybrid intelligence contexts, where trust calibration failures, bias inheritance from human-AI interaction loops, and cognitive framework homogenization become self-reinforcing dynamics that are invisible to external auditors.

## Draft C: Training Data Memorization and Extraction: What Models Remember and Who Owns It

This chapter examines the conditions under which language models memorize and reproduce their training data, and the legal, privacy, and governance consequences that follow. Drawing on the foundational extraction literature of Carlini and colleagues, membership inference attacks, and model inversion techniques, the chapter establishes a forensic toolkit for demonstrating what was in training corpora — a toolkit now operationalized in litigation including the New York Times complaint against OpenAI. The chapter gives particular attention to the code domain, where memorization risks extend beyond copyright to active credential leakage, licence-contagion disputes, and supply-chain vulnerability propagation. The open versus closed weight debate is examined through empirical evidence showing that white-box access to open-weight models enables more efficient extraction while also enabling pre-deployment auditability and machine unlearning interventions unavailable in closed-model deployments.

## Draft D: The Open Source Organization: Institutional Templates for Human-Agent Collectives

This chapter argues that open source software governance provides tested institutional templates for organizations that blend human and agent participants. Drawing on Benkler's analysis of commons-based peer production, von Hippel's user innovation framework, Raymond's bazaar model, and Ostrom's design principles for self-governing commons, the chapter identifies four structural features shared by mature open source projects and hybrid human-agent organizations: asymmetric capability distribution, distributed contribution without central employment, audit trails as governance infrastructure, and fork as institutional exit. Four organizational design patterns are described — the Delegated Executor, the Augmented Deliberator, the Autonomous Steward, and a fourth pattern — each with documented open source analogues, principal-agent cost analysis, and failure modes illustrated through narrative vignettes.

## Draft E: Structural Vulnerabilities in Language Models: Lessons from Buffer Overflow and SQL Injection

This chapter examines prompt injection as a structural vulnerability in language models by analogy with two paradigmatic cases from computing history: buffer overflow in C and SQL injection in relational databases. In each case, a deliberate design decision that enabled genuine capability simultaneously eliminated a security-relevant distinction, producing a vulnerability class that surface-level defenses proved insufficient to address at scale. The chapter argues that transformer architectures face a structurally analogous condition: the uniform token attention mechanism that enables in-context learning eliminates the distinction between operator instructions and external content, making prompt injection a property of the architecture rather than a patchable defect. Drawing on the RLHF defense literature, indirect injection taxonomy, and regulatory timelines from the memory-safety transition, the chapter projects likely adoption curves for any eventual structural fix.

## Draft F: The Commons Paradox: How Hybrid Intelligence Can Redistribute What Big Tech Extracts

This chapter examines the structural paradox at the heart of the AI economy: the most valuable AI systems were trained on knowledge produced by millions of unpaid contributors to open source repositories, encyclopedias, and question-answer platforms, yet the financial returns have accrued almost entirely to the companies that performed the extraction. Drawing on Benkler's theory of commons-based peer production and Ostrom's governance principles, the chapter documents the measurable harm: Stack Overflow traffic collapsed more than 75% between 2022 and 2024, Wikimedia Foundation absorbed a 50% bandwidth increase from AI crawlers with no corresponding revenue, and recursive AI-generated content entering training corpora risks model collapse via distribution narrowing. A five-category taxonomy of commons harm is developed, alongside design principles for hybrid intelligence architectures that could redirect value toward contributors rather than away from them.

## Draft G: Contemporary Themes in Hybrid Intelligence and Agentic Collaboration

This chapter examines the augmentation-and-feedback stage of hybrid intelligence development, in which AI systems transition from passive tools into active collaborators and autonomous agents operating within the open source ecosystems that trained them. Drawing on Lutz et al.'s mutual-augmentation definition of hybrid intelligence, Bansal et al.'s evidence that AI explanations can reduce complementary team performance, and Becker et al.'s finding that experienced developers work 19% slower with AI tools on mature codebases, the chapter identifies the conditions under which human-AI complementarity holds, fails, or inverts. The agentic turn — from language model as oracle to language model as ReAct-style controller with perception, planning, action, and persistent memory — is analyzed through empirical benchmarks, agent-computer interface research, and the trajectory from human-data-trained to experience-trained systems represented by AlphaGo Zero and its successors.

## Draft H: Human Augmentation and Co-Evolution in Hybrid Intelligence: From Individual Adaptation to Generational Change

This chapter examines how AI augmentation tools change the humans who use them across three timescales: individual cognitive adaptation, population-level stratification, and generational co-evolution. Drawing on Clark and Chalmers' extended mind thesis, Sparrow et al.'s documented offloading effects on memory, Maguire et al.'s neuroplasticity evidence from London taxi drivers, and Odling-Smee and Laland's niche construction theory, the chapter argues that AI tools are not neutral instruments but selective-environment modifiers that relax cognitive selection pressures in ways analogous to corrective lenses relaxing selection on visual acuity. Population-level effects are examined through the lens of the third digital divide — differential augmentation by prior skill level — and the compounding mechanism by which augmented users disproportionately shape future training data, potentially widening rather than narrowing capability gaps across populations.

## Draft I: LLM Overconfidence and Epistemic Limits: What Models Don't Know They Don't Know

This chapter examines a structural limitation of current large language models as hybrid intelligence partners: their systematic miscalibration between expressed confidence and actual accuracy, and their inability to distinguish what they know from what they do not know. Drawing on Kadavath et al.'s self-calibration studies, the KalshiBench prediction-market evaluation, Rawte et al.'s hallucination survey, and SelfCheckGPT's sampling-based detection approach, the chapter establishes that confabulation is not a patchable bug but a consequence of training models to generate probable continuations. Code-specific findings — ghost API generation rates of 8–40%, static analysis detection ceilings below 50%, and multi-step agentic hallucination propagation — are analyzed alongside the Dunning-Kruger dynamic whereby low-competence model states produce disproportionate overconfidence. The chapter concludes that effective hybrid intelligence requires human partners with sufficient domain knowledge to recognize AI error — making complementarity most durable where human expertise is concentrated where AI confidence is misplaced.

## Draft J: Beyond Human Language: LLMs, Emergent Communication, and the Limits of the Linguistic Substrate

This chapter examines whether natural language is an adequate medium for hybrid intelligence at the frontier. Drawing on the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, empirical evidence of LLM linguistic relativity in causal reasoning, emergent communication research from multi-agent coordination experiments, and the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, the chapter argues that language models operate partially in representational spaces that exceed what natural language can express. Sparse autoencoder interpretability findings reveal cross-lingual features beneath the language level; multi-agent systems develop efficient but non-interpretable communication protocols when unconstrained; chain-of-thought tokens function as synthetic computation rather than communication. The governance implication is that human-AI collaboration is constrained not only by the human partner's comprehension of AI outputs but by the medium itself: natural language may be insufficient as the interface for frontier hybrid intelligence systems.

## Draft K: Open Weights ≠ Open Source ≠ Open Training Data: Three Layers of Openness

This chapter provides a conceptual taxonomy for the term "open-source AI," which collapses three technically related but normatively distinct layers into a single label: open weights (distributional openness), open source training and inference pipelines (process openness), and open training data with provenance disclosure (accountability openness). Drawing on the Model Openness Framework, the OSI Open Source AI Definition, the EU AI Act's GPAI disclosure requirements, and the forensic implications of training-data extraction research, the chapter argues that each layer carries different governance stakes: open weights enable capability democratization; open source enables auditability and fork-as-exit; open training data enables accountability for extraction from the commons. The structural analogy to classical software supply-chain transparency — binary versus source versus dependency manifest — clarifies why layer conflation produces openwashing rather than genuine openness.

## Draft L: Machine Creativity: A Primer

This chapter surveys the conceptual landscape of machine creativity for hybrid intelligence researchers, using Boden's three-fold taxonomy — combinational, exploratory, and transformational creativity — as the analytical spine. Drawing on landmark demonstrations including AlphaGo's Move 37, Creative Adversarial Networks, and Novy-Marx and Velikov's experiment in AI-generated academic papers, the chapter traces three generations of machine creativity from rule-based systems through evolutionary and generative architectures to foundation model creativity. The authorship question is examined through Hertzmann's tool framing, which relocates creative agency to the human-AI system rather than the AI component alone. For hybrid intelligence design, the chapter argues that preserving the human's curatorial and evaluative role is more important than improving AI generative quality, and that the same architecture enabling industrial-scale combinational creativity also enables industrial-scale academic fraud.

## Draft M: Taxonomies for Agentic AI and Human-AI Collaboration

This chapter maps the recognised academic taxonomies that give structure to the terms "agentic AI" and "human-AI collaboration," providing a conceptual foundation for the handbook's other discussions of hybrid intelligence architecture and governance. The chapter surveys the Russell and Norvig classical agent taxonomy, the cognitive-architecture framework of perception, memory, planning, and action that now dominates LLM-agent survey literature, Parasuraman et al.'s ten-level autonomy scale, Shneiderman's Reliability-Goals matrix distinguishing supertools from teammates and autonomous agents, and the human-in/on/out-of-the-loop framework used in regulatory and military contexts. The chapter clarifies that most current production AI systems operate at autonomy levels four through six, that governance disputes concentrate at levels seven through nine, and that the taxonomic choices embedded in regulatory frameworks carry normative implications for accountability design.

## Draft N: The AI/ML Ecosystem: Who Makes What

This chapter maps the political economy of the AI and machine learning production system, tracing six supply-chain layers from silicon and compute infrastructure through foundation model pretraining, fine-tuning and alignment, to application deployment. Power concentration is shown to follow capital-intensity gradients: chip manufacture and cloud compute are oligopolistic; foundation model development is moderately concentrated among five frontier labs; application deployment is fragmented. The chapter identifies frontier labs, European and Chinese competitors, the HuggingFace open source axis, cloud infrastructure providers, academic research institutions, civil society, and end users as distinct actor types with different production roles, openness postures, and dependencies. The analysis connects the commons accumulation argument, the extraction inequality argument, and the openness typology to answer who actually holds power in the systems that constitute hybrid intelligence.

## Draft O: Hybrid Intelligence Ecosystems: A Synthesis of Empirical Studies

This chapter synthesizes empirical studies of hybrid intelligence ecosystems across domains, moving from laboratory experiments to observed phenomena in real organizational and societal contexts. Drawing on Dellermann et al.'s seminal definition of hybrid intelligence as superior complementary achievement, Lutz et al.'s three-layer micro-meso-macro framework, and Raisch and Krakowski's automate-augment-abandon typology, the chapter surveys domain-specific evidence from medicine (Topol's high-performance medicine model, AlphaFold), software development (Becker et al.'s productivity study, Wessel et al.'s bot-fatigue findings), and scientific research. The synthesis identifies conditions under which the augmentation strategy produces genuine hybrid superiority, conditions under which it degrades to automation bias or bot fatigue, and the governance and institutional factors that determine which outcome prevails.

## Draft P: Governance and Compliance for Hybrid Intelligence

This chapter maps the regulatory frameworks relevant to hybrid intelligence and identifies the governance vacuum at their intersection: existing regulation addresses either human decision-making with clear accountability, or autonomous AI with technical audit requirements, but hybrid intelligence — where accountability is distributed between human and AI components — fits neither category. The EU AI Act's risk categorization and GPAI provisions, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, sector-specific regulation in medicine, finance, law, and employment, and ISO/IEC standardization are surveyed. Key governance challenges are analyzed: the responsibility gap when hybrid systems fail, the inadequacy of formal human oversight that is HOOTL in practice, and the absence of operational definitions for meaningful human control. The chapter argues that HI governance requires new accountability instruments calibrated to the distributed nature of hybrid agency.

## Draft Q: Machines Using Humans: When Machines Set the Agenda

This chapter examines the inverse of the standard hybrid intelligence narrative: instead of humans using AI as a tool for human purposes, it analyzes systems in which machines structure, direct, and optimize human behavior to achieve machine-defined goals. Drawing on Gray and Suri's ghost-work analysis of humans-as-a-service in AI pipelines, Rosenblat and Stark's research on algorithmic management in gig platforms, Zuboff's surveillance capitalism framework, and empirical studies on LLM sycophancy and generative monoculture, the chapter documents that algorithmic direction of human behavior is not speculative but describes current operating conditions for millions of workers, information consumers, and RLHF annotators. The chapter connects commons extraction to behavioral extraction: not only data but human attention, labor, and cognitive patterns are resources harvested by optimizing systems, raising fundamental questions about who holds agency in hybrid intelligence.

## Draft R: What Hybrid Intelligence Teaches Us About Human Intelligence

This chapter uses hybrid intelligence as a mirror for understanding human cognition, approaching the human-AI encounter not only as a design problem but as a source of empirical data about the nature of intelligence, knowledge, and creativity. Drawing on Clark and Chalmers' extended mind thesis, Hutchins' distributed cognition in navigation systems, the Platonic Representation Hypothesis's evidence for convergent internal representations across architectures, and Polanyi's tacit knowledge framework, the chapter argues that hybrid intelligence reveals intelligence as always having been distributed, ecological, and culturally mediated. What AI cannot do — ground symbols perceptually, achieve calibrated metacognition, exercise normative judgment — reveals not its difference from human intelligence but the specific properties of human intelligence that presuppose embodied experience: properties that define the irreducibly human contribution to any hybrid system.

## Draft S: arXiv, Wikipedia, GitHub, and Hugging Face: Four Open Platforms for Knowledge

This chapter traces the histories of the four open knowledge platforms whose accumulated content became the primary training substrate for modern AI: arXiv (scientific preprints since 1991), Wikipedia (collaborative encyclopedic knowledge since 2001), GitHub (code and social infrastructure since 2008), and Hugging Face (models and datasets since 2019). Each platform is analyzed as an institutional realization of the commons accumulation phase: founded by volunteers or public institutions, governed through distributed norms, and subsequently harvested by commercial AI labs whose training corpora are built primarily on these platforms' outputs. The chapter examines the governance tensions each platform faces — peer-review bypass on arXiv, demographic skew and AI-generated content re-entry on Wikipedia, single private-owner risk on GitHub — and their shared position as infrastructural commons whose sustainability is threatened by the extraction paradox.

## Draft T: Agentic Tool Calling: From Function Calls to MCP and Skill Ecosystems

This chapter maps the technical and institutional development of agentic tool calling — the interface innovations that transformed language models from text generators into agents capable of perceiving, planning, and acting in external systems. From the ReAct paradigm's interleaved reasoning and action loops through OpenAI's June 2023 function calling standard and the ChatGPT plugin system, to Anthropic's Model Context Protocol (MCP) as an open USB-like interface standard, the chapter traces how agentic capability is produced through protocol design rather than model architecture. The chapter analyzes MCP's strategic logic — reducing M×N bilateral integrations to M+N via a standard protocol — alongside emerging skill ecosystems and multi-agent orchestration frameworks. The governance implication is that agentic capability is increasingly an infrastructure question: who designs and controls the protocols that mediate AI-world interaction shapes what agents can do and whose interests they serve.

## Draft U: The Single Source of Truth Problem in Hybrid Intelligence Systems

This chapter argues that hallucination in hybrid intelligence systems is fundamentally an epistemic infrastructure problem rather than a linguistic defect: it emerges where AI systems lack anchoring to formal, authoritative, and navigable sources of truth. Drawing on Rawte et al.'s hallucination taxonomy, Lewis et al.'s Retrieval-Augmented Generation as the dominant technical response, and Novy-Marx and Velikov's experiment showing that AI can contaminate academic citation graphs at industrial scale, the chapter develops a democratic epistemology framework for grounding hybrid intelligence. Wikipedia and Wikidata's community-governed verification workflows, GitHub's epistemology-by-diff commit provenance, FAIR data principles for machine-actionable government data, and the Bellingcat OSINT model of adversarial crowd-sourced verification are analyzed as distributed truth infrastructure. The chapter concludes that investing in open, well-governed knowledge infrastructure is a precondition for reliable hybrid intelligence, not merely an academic good.

## Draft V: Two Engines of Progress: Scaling, Self-Play, and the Emerging Synthesis in Contemporary AI

This chapter analyzes the two divergent research paradigms that have produced the most transformative AI systems of the past decade: the scaling hypothesis, which holds that intelligence emerges from statistical patterns in large human-generated corpora given sufficient compute, and the reward hypothesis of reinforcement learning, which holds that capable agents can be trained through environmental interaction and self-generated experience without human-labeled data. Drawing on the GPT lineage, AlphaGo Zero, DQN, PPO, MuZero, DreamerV3, and RLHF as paradigm bridge, the chapter traces how the two paradigms' different dependencies on open knowledge commons — the scaling paradigm is constitutively dependent on the human record; the RL paradigm can in principle transcend it through self-play — are converging in hybrid architectures like InstructGPT and DeepSeek-R1. The implications for hybrid intelligence are examined alongside emerging neurosymbolic approaches challenging both paradigms.

## Draft W: The Unharvested Commons: Oral Traditions, Indigenous Epistemologies, and the Case for Non-Digital Knowledge in AI Training

This chapter examines the profound selection bias in AI training corpora: of approximately 7,000 languages spoken today, fewer than 100 are meaningfully represented in leading language models, and the training data overwhelmingly reflects Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) societies, excluding oral traditions, indigenous knowledge systems, and pre-digital epistemologies. Drawing on Bartók's ethnographic collection methodology as historical precedent, Bender et al.'s stochastic parrots critique, Boroditsky's linguistic relativity evidence, and the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance, the chapter argues that the knowledge absent from AI training data is not merely a gap but an active distortion — making certain epistemologies structurally unthinkable for trained systems. Deliberate non-digital collection, community-controlled transcription pipelines, and CARE-compliant governance frameworks are proposed as mechanisms for extending the feedback loop that currently operates almost entirely within WEIRD contexts.

## Draft X: Authenticating Human Knowledge: Provenance, Detection, and the Epistemic Arms Race

This chapter examines the breakdown of a foundational assumption of academic epistemology — that published literature represents accumulated human intellectual effort — as AI systems capable of generating academically fluent papers at industrial scale render that assumption empirically contestable. Drawing on Novy-Marx and Velikov's demonstration that 288 academic papers with imagined literature can be generated in 12 hours for under $300, Polanyi's tacit knowledge as an epistemological marker of authentic scholarship, watermarking limitations documented under EU AI Act compliance surveys, and the Sybil attack analogy for mutual-citation synthetic clusters, the chapter surveys technical detection methods and their arms race dynamics. Institutional responses — open peer review, mandatory DOI citation verification, preregistration, and C2PA provenance metadata standards — are analyzed as structural defenses that raise the cost of synthetic fabrication without depending on any single detection technology.

## Draft Y: Singapore as a National AI Laboratory: Strategy, Governance, and Societal Integration

This chapter examines Singapore as arguably the world's most systematic national experiment in deliberate AI integration at societal scale, analyzing its National AI Strategy 2.0, AI Verify open governance testing framework, and AI Singapore national research programme as a case study in state-directed hybrid intelligence ecosystem building. Sector deployments in healthcare (SELENA+ retinal screening at national scale, hospital readmission prediction, agentic preventive care pilots), public administration (Singpass-anchored personalized services), and workforce transformation are examined as empirical HI cases where the state plays ecosystem-builder rather than regulator. The chapter situates Singapore's model within the governance frameworks surveyed elsewhere in the handbook, identifies the governance philosophy of soft-law-first iterative regulation as its distinctive contribution, and interrogates its exportability to democratic contexts without Singapore's level of centralized state capacity.

## Draft Z: Frugal AI: Satisfactory Performance at Minimum Environmental Cost

This chapter maps the environmental cost of AI and the efficiency techniques that challenge the dominant assumption equating capability with scale. Drawing on Strubell et al.'s foundational energy-cost analysis, Hoffmann et al.'s Chinchilla scaling laws demonstrating that smaller models trained on more data outperform larger undertrained ones, quantization techniques enabling frontier-scale models to run on consumer hardware, mixture-of-experts conditional compute architectures, and DeepSeek's 10–50× training efficiency breakthrough, the chapter argues for a frugal AI perspective that demands proportionality between task requirements and resource consumption. The inference cost iceberg — often exceeding training cost over a model's lifetime — is analyzed alongside the Jevons paradox: efficiency gains that increase aggregate consumption by lowering per-query costs. The chapter proposes the task-model fit principle as a practical governance heuristic for responsible hybrid intelligence deployment.

## Draft AL: Agent Memory and Continuity: Architectures of Persistence in Hybrid Intelligence Systems

This chapter argues that agent memory is not a single capability but four architecturally distinct subsystems with different storage, retrieval, and governance requirements. NHTSA's Tesla Autopilot investigations (PE21-020, EA22-002) document automation complacency at population scale — 2 million vehicles whose drivers had progressively reduced monitoring as a predictable consequence of system design. The humanoid robotics case documents the 2025–2026 commercial frontier: Figure AI's Figure 02 at BMW Spartanburg (1,250+ runtime hours, 30,000+ X3 vehicles), Agility Robotics' Digit at GXO Logistics (100,000+ totes milestone), Boston Dynamics' electric Atlas successor (early Hyundai-plant shipments 2026), and 1X Technologies' NEO — a cohort demonstrating that the warehouse-logistics task domain under complementary human-robot division is where commercial humanoid deployment is currently viable. Drawing on Endsley's (1995) situation-awareness framework (60–70% SA degradation within 10 minutes of disengagement) and the mode-confusion literature (Air Inter Flight 148; Tesla FSD update cycles producing mode confusion at scale), the chapter establishes that hybrid intelligence governance for autonomous physical systems must attend to cognitive architecture constraints that no amount of capability improvement eliminates.

## Draft BA: Applied AI and Student Learning: Personalization, Scaffolding, and the Transformation of Educational Practice

This chapter examines applied AI in education — the domain with the most directly measurable human-benefit case for hybrid intelligence — anchored in Bloom's (1984) foundational finding that one-on-one human tutoring produces outcomes approximately two standard deviations above conventional classroom instruction (placing the average tutored student at the 98th percentile of the conventionally taught distribution), and the four-decade history of attempts to close this gap at scale. The chapter argues three things: that current AI adoption in education is not a paradigm break but an acceleration of the adaptive-instruction trajectory begun by Carbonell's SCHOLAR system in 1970 and developed through intelligent tutoring systems of the 1980s–2000s (Carnegie Learning's Cognitive Tutor, AutoTutor) — which already achieved effect sizes of one to one-and-a-half standard deviations above conventional instruction in Koedinger and Corbett's (2006) meta-analysis; that the distinctive contribution of LLM-based tutors is not superior domain knowledge but conversational flexibility — the capacity to meet a learner where they are, in natural language, without requiring pre-specified curriculum paths, constraints that bounded every prior ITS; and that the primary risks of AI in education concern access distribution, metacognitive offloading (the cognitive-atrophy dynamic analyzed in Draft H), and the structural challenge of assessing learning in an environment where AI can perform most assessed tasks better than the students being assessed.

Empirical cases include Khanmigo (Khan Academy) as the most widely deployed LLM-based Socratic tutor; Mollick and Mollick's (2023) finding that LLM deliberate-practice simulations accelerate skill acquisition in business education (DOI 10.2139/ssrn.4475995); and the institutional response to AI-enabled academic fraud, where UNESCO's 2023 guidance (Miao and Holmes, Miao & Holmes, unesdoc ark) positions assessment redesign rather than detection as the durable response. The chapter's connection to Draft AC (AI vs. human performance benchmarks) frames LLM tutors within the broader question of task-specific AI outperformance and its pedagogical implications; the connection to Draft H (augmentation and co-evolution) addresses the longer-term population-level consequence of offloading cognitive challenge to AI during the formative years in which cognitive capacity is shaped — the question of whether AI tutors produce learning or merely the performance of learning.

## Draft BB: Learning from Demonstration: When Robots Watch, Then Do

This chapter examines learning from demonstration (LfD) — the paradigm in which robots acquire dexterous manipulation skills by observing human performance rather than from explicit programming — as a site where tacit knowledge transitions into computational policy and the human-robot relationship is fundamentally pedagogical. Starting from Polanyi's tacit knowledge framework and the formal impossibility of specifying high-dexterity industrial skills as explicit programs, the chapter analyzes why deformable-object manipulation — garment assembly, cable handling, biological tissue — has resisted automation despite decades of industrial robotics investment: the knowledge required lives in human motor memory, not in engineering documentation. The LfD paradigm's central insight is that knowledge elicitation can be bypassed by modelling what experts *do* rather than articulating what they *know*. The 2023 inflection point represented by ACT/ALOHA (Zhao, Kumar, Levine & Finn, arXiv:2304.13705) is analyzed in detail: 50 teleoperation demonstrations over roughly one hour yield over 80% success rates on dexterous bimanual tasks at USD 20,000 in hardware, making demonstration-driven training economically practical at the cadence of software development. Policy learning architectures are surveyed — behaviour cloning and its compounding error problem, DAgger's interactive correction loop, and Diffusion Policy's (Chi et al., arXiv:2303.04137) probabilistic treatment of multi-modal demonstrations — alongside foundation model approaches (RT-1, RT-2, Open X-Embodiment) that import general world knowledge from pre-training into the manipulation domain. The garment manufacturing case is developed as the canonical hard instance: a USD 1.5 trillion global industry where 40–60% of assembly time involves handling steps that remain human-performed because deformable fabric state is computationally intractable by classical model-based control. The chapter's hybrid intelligence argument is that the skilled worker in this framework is not replaced but structurally repositioned: her expertise becomes training data, her role shifts from production execution to demonstration provision and edge-case monitoring, and the meta-skills required for high-quality demonstration are learnable and economically significant. Distribution shift — policy failure outside its training distribution — ensures that human expertise remains necessary not merely historically but operationally: the human expert remains the live arbiter of when a learned policy is and is not within its competence, which requires exactly the tacit knowledge that motivated the LfD approach in the first place.

## Draft BC: The Infinite Loop Thought Experiment: Will an LLM Ever Finish?

This chapter develops a thought experiment — a language model placed in an indefinite generate-evaluate-revise loop — as a window onto foundational questions in agentic hybrid intelligence: convergence, self-evaluation reliability, and the governance of autonomous improvement. The loop has four steps: generate an output, evaluate it against a criterion, decide whether it is good enough, and repeat or halt. The question is simple and structurally important: will it ever stop? The chapter's answer is unambiguous — a large language model in a pure self-evaluation loop has no guaranteed termination. Three theoretical arguments establish this. Turing's halting problem (1936) establishes that no general external observer can guarantee termination by inspection of the loop's current state. Brouwer's fixed-point theorem, while guaranteeing at least one fixed point for continuous functions on compact spaces, does not apply straightforwardly to LLM output spaces — and existence does not imply reachability in any case. The self-evaluation literature provides the most practically damaging evidence: Kadavath et al. (2022) demonstrate systematic miscalibration in LLM confidence estimates; research on sycophancy (Perez et al., 2022; Sharma et al., 2023) shows that models bias toward validating their own outputs when evaluation criteria are vague; and RLHF-trained models exhibit revision compulsion — continuing to revise even high-quality outputs because the revision action itself is rewarded. Goodhart's Law applies throughout: the loop optimises the satisfaction proxy, not the underlying task quality.

Empirical evidence from Shinn et al.'s Reflexion framework (2023) confirms the theoretical prediction: loop convergence is reliable when and only when the evaluation criterion is external and objective (code passing automated tests); self-evaluation on open-ended tasks produces smaller gains and less reliable convergence. Three empirical failure patterns are identified: divergence (cascading confabulation in long loops, where each revision amplifies the errors of the previous one); cycles (deterministic loops alternating between equivalent outputs without improving either); and pseudo-convergence (the loop continues revising laterally, each step different but not better, because the model cannot recognise the plateau). Four design implications follow: external termination conditions are logical requirements, not engineering conveniences; the evaluation function is the dominant determinant of loop quality (the cross-model adversarial design of the ARIS framework — separate generator and evaluator models — addresses this directly); satisficing (halt at a threshold) is structurally superior to optimising (halt at best possible); and iteration count is a governance parameter, not a technical setting, that determines autonomous-operation scope under the Parasuraman et al. (2000) autonomy taxonomy.

## Draft BI: AI Slop, Cognitive Transformation, and the Dual Threat of Misuse and Misalignment

Pervasive AI use does not leave human cognition untouched. This chapter examines three structurally linked phenomena. First, *AI slop* — the proliferation of low-effort, AI-generated content across search results, social platforms, and preprint servers — functions as epistemic pollution that degrades the information commons users rely on to think. The mechanism is formal: Shumailov et al. (2024) demonstrate that models trained on AI-generated outputs undergo *model collapse*, a statistical degradation that amplifies the most probable outputs while suppressing tail distributions — the rare, deviant, and heterodox examples that constitute much of human knowledge's richness. Beyond data-level collapse, Doshi and Hauser (2024) show that AI-assisted writing increases average individual quality while simultaneously compressing the collective diversity of expressed ideas: a homogenizing pressure on discourse that parallels genetic vulnerability in biology. Second, extensive agentic AI use restructures human cognitive capacities asymmetrically. Gains are real — Noy and Zhang (2023) document 37% time reduction and 18% quality improvement in professional writing tasks, with the largest gains among lower-ability users; Dell'Acqua et al. (2023) confirm 40% quality outperformance on tasks within the AI's competence frontier. Losses are structurally embedded: Sparrow, Liu, and Wegner's (2011) Google-effect research shows that relying on external retrieval weakens the memory traces that consolidate learning; Risko and Gilbert (2016) confirm that cognitive offloading reduces retention and depth; Carr's (2011) synthesis documents a shift from deep to scanning reading under persistent AI-mediated interaction. The *jagged technological frontier* (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023) creates a skill trap: AI competence in one domain teaches over-reliance that transfers inappropriately to adjacent domains where AI fails silently. Third, the threat spectrum of advanced agentic AI runs in two directions. *Misuse* is a near-term and already observable threat: Goldstein et al. (2023) document that large language models lower the cost of synthetic propaganda operations by over an order of magnitude; Chesney and Citron (2019) identify the *liar's dividend* — the existence of synthetic media provides plausible deniability for genuine incriminating material, degrading the evidential value of all media. *Misalignment* is a structural long-horizon concern: Gabriel (2020) taxonomises value-loading failure, reward hacking, and specification gaming; Weidinger et al. (2022) provide a six-category harm taxonomy covering information hazards, misinformation, hate speech, privacy violation, cyberoffense, and value alignment failure. Gabriel et al. (2024) identify *sycophancy* — AI systems optimising for immediate user approval rather than accuracy — as a distinct misalignment mode that, applied to long-duration personal agents, produces cumulative epistemic distortion of the user's information ecology. The chapter closes with design implications: attribution-preserving information architecture, cognitively productive AI scaffolding rather than substitution, minimal-footprint agentic governance, and regulatory infrastructure for AI-enabled influence operations. Cross-references: Draft AS (technostress, Bainbridge ironies — not repeated here); Draft BE (hallucination cases and proliferation); Draft E (structural injection vulnerabilities); Draft AD (harm encoded in training data); Draft I (LLM overconfidence as precondition for automation bias).

## Draft BJ: Thinking Fast and Slow with Agentic AI: Dual-Process Theory and Hybrid Intelligence

Kahneman's dual-process theory — the distinction between fast, automatic System 1 cognition and slow, deliberate System 2 cognition — acquires a new structural dimension in the context of agentic AI. This chapter argues that current AI systems are, architecturally, System 1 machines: transformer-based large language models produce outputs through rapid, parallel pattern-matching over training distributions, generating fluent, confident responses without genuine causal inference or counterfactual simulation. Binz and Schulz (2023) confirm empirically that GPT-3 reproduces the classic Kahneman-Tversky biases — conjunction fallacy, base-rate neglect, anchoring — consistent with a System 1 processing profile. The most capable agentic systems combine both modes: Anthony, Tian, and Barber's (2017) "Thinking Fast and Slow with Deep Learning and Tree Search" (NeurIPS) explicitly instantiates the Kahneman architecture — fast neural pattern recognition (System 1) coupled with slow Monte Carlo Tree Search (System 2 deliberation) — as the design principle behind AlphaGo/AlphaZero. Bengio (2019) identified the construction of "System 2 deep learning" as the central research frontier, characterising capabilities still missing from current architectures: causal reasoning, variable binding, compositional generalisation. In hybrid human-AI systems the System 1/System 2 boundary distributes across the coupling: AI handles System 1 load while humans are positioned as System 2 supervisors. The chapter argues this redistribution is asymmetric and self-undermining. Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) document automation complacency as System 1 takeover: supervisors' own System 2 vigilance is suppressed by the fluency and confidence of automated outputs. Dietvorst et al. (2015) document the mirror pathology — algorithm aversion as System 1 backlash from a single observed error. Rastogi et al. (2020) demonstrate directly that AI-assisted decision-making shifts human processing toward System 1 heuristics, reducing the depth of independent System 2 engagement. The chapter closes with four design principles: preserve System 2 engagement through provisional rather than authoritative AI output presentation; design for appropriate reliance calibrated to actual AI reliability (Lee & See, 2004) rather than maximal trust; resist treating AI chain-of-thought traces as equivalent to human System 2 reasoning; and externalise collective System 2 into institutional structures — peer review, adversarial red-teaming, audit requirements — where individual deliberative capacity is unreliable. Cross-references: Draft BI (cognitive atrophy, jagged frontier skill trap); Draft AS (automation complacency, Bainbridge); Draft AA (psychology of AI outperformance); Draft H (co-evolution and atrophy).

## Draft BE: When the Machine Gets It Wrong: Notable AI Failures, Agent Breakdowns, and the Proliferation of Hallucinations

Hybrid intelligence systems fail. They fail in ways structurally different from how software has historically failed — not through crashes or explicit errors but through confident, fluent, and systematically wrong outputs. This chapter documents notable empirical cases of AI system failures, agentic AI breakdowns, and the emerging phenomenon of hallucination proliferation, arguing that understanding failure modes is a prerequisite for trustworthy hybrid intelligence design. Three failure axes are distinguished: hallucination — the generation of factually incorrect but syntactically plausible output, ranging from wrong dates and misattributed quotes to non-existent legal precedents, fabricated drug dosages, and ghost API references; bias and distributional failure — systematic errors reflecting training data inequalities that produce disparate impact at population scale, typically invisible until deployment reveals them; and agentic failure — failures specific to autonomous agents with tool access and multi-step planning, including goal misspecification, unintended side effects, coordination breakdowns between agents, and cascading errors across tool-call chains. A fourth, emerging category — hallucination proliferation — describes the feedback loop by which AI-generated falsehoods enter future training corpora, compounding errors across model generations and constituting a direct threat to the knowledge commons analyzed in Cluster 1.

Empirical cases documented include Amazon's recruiting algorithm (2014–2018; systematic downgrading of CVs containing "women's", a model that had trained on ten years of male-dominated hiring decisions, scrapped before deployment); the COMPAS recidivism prediction tool (ProPublica, 2016; 45% false positive rate for Black defendants versus 24% for white defendants, producing a sustained academic debate on the incompatibility of formal fairness criteria); the Mata v. Avianca legal hallucination case (fabricated case citations submitted to a US federal court by an AI-assisted attorney, resulting in sanctions); and agentic failure modes from the 2023–2025 deployment record including prompt injection chains, cascading tool errors, and goal misspecification incidents. The chapter's position within the handbook is explicitly connective: each failure type maps to structural analyses elsewhere — Draft E (prompt injection as architectural property of transformers), Draft I (epistemic limits and calibration failure as root cause of hallucination), Draft AD (societal harm encoded in training data as root cause of bias failures), and Draft AR (the harness as the governance layer responsible for agentic failure containment).
