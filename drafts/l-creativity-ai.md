# Draft L — Machine Creativity: A Primer

## 1. Why creativity matters for hybrid intelligence

Creativity is the faculty most frequently invoked when arguing that AI will never fully replace human workers (Frey & Osborne, 2017), and most frequently challenged when AI systems produce outputs indistinguishable from human art, music, poetry, or scholarship. For hybrid intelligence research, creativity occupies a pivotal position: if AI systems can genuinely create — not merely recombine, imitate, or interpolate — the nature of human-AI collaboration changes fundamentally. If they cannot, then creative judgment remains the distinctively human contribution to any hybrid system, and the design of such systems should preserve and amplify that contribution rather than simulate it.

This primer surveys the conceptual landscape of machine creativity: what creativity is, what kinds of creativity AI systems can and cannot exhibit, and what the most significant empirical demonstrations reveal about the current state of the question.

## 2. What is creativity? Boden's three types

The most influential framework in the field is Margaret Boden's three-fold taxonomy, developed across several decades of work in the philosophy of mind and AI (Boden, 1998 [J], 2004 [B]). Boden distinguishes:

**Combinational creativity:** producing unfamiliar combinations of familiar ideas. A metaphor, a mashup, a recipe that combines ingredients from different cuisines. This is the most tractable form for AI: given a sufficiently large and diverse training corpus, a generative model can produce combinations that no human has previously articulated, simply because the space of possible combinations exceeds what any individual could explore. Most contemporary large language model outputs — novel phrasings, unexpected analogies, creative naming (as in Novy-Marx & Velikov, 2025) — fall into this category.

**Exploratory creativity:** exploring the limits of an established conceptual space — discovering what is possible within a well-defined style, genre, or domain. A jazz musician finding new possibilities within bebop conventions; a mathematician proving that a conjecture is true within an established formal system. AI systems have demonstrated genuinely exploratory creativity in constrained domains: AlphaGo's Move 37 in Game 2 of the March 2016 match against Lee Sedol — placing a stone in a position no professional player would consider, yet eventually decisive — became the canonical example. The move was not outside the rules of Go; it was outside the *known conceptual space* of Go strategy as developed by human players over centuries. Brinkmann et al. (2023), in their *Nature Human Behaviour* synthesis on machine influence on human culture, identify Move 37 as a paradigm case of an AI move that "broke with centuries of Go orthodoxy" and subsequently shifted the strategic repertoire of professional human play. The capacity to exceed the inherited conceptual space derives directly from the self-play architecture documented by Silver et al. (2017) for AlphaGo Zero — training without reliance on the human record — which is treated as a distinct developmental paradigm for AI capability in the present volume (see `v-ai-paradigms-development.md`, §3: Paradigm II — The Reward Hypothesis; Move 37 is the empirical anchor for that section's argument that self-play can generate strategy outside the human distribution).

**Transformational creativity:** changing the conceptual space itself — not exploring within a framework but transforming the framework. Copernicus replacing geocentrism with heliocentrism; Picasso's cubism abandoning perspectival representation entirely; Darwin reconceiving biological diversity as the product of descent with modification rather than design. Boden regards this as the most demanding and most genuinely creative form, and it is the one for which current AI systems have the weakest claim. Transformational creativity requires not just novelty within a space but the capacity to evaluate and reject the space itself — a meta-cognitive capacity that existing architectures do not clearly possess.

## 3. The novelty problem: recombination vs. genuine origination

A persistent critique of machine creativity is that AI outputs are, in a deep sense, *recombinative* rather than *originative* — that they are sophisticated interpolations within a learned distribution rather than departures from it. Epstein et al. (2023) frame this as the question of whether generative AI is a creative partner or a statistical mirror: a system that returns to us elaborated versions of what we have collectively produced, rather than contributing genuinely new cognitive content.

The critique has force. Diffusion models and autoregressive language models are trained to predict plausible continuations or reconstructions of training data; their outputs are, in a literal mathematical sense, points in the learned distribution of human-produced content. A poem generated by GPT-4 is, structurally, a high-probability sample from the distribution of poems in its training set. It can be novel in the combinational sense — no human has previously produced that specific sequence of words — but it cannot, by construction, be novel in the transformational sense: it cannot reject the conceptual framework of poetry as poetry, because it has no access to the meta-level perspective from which such a rejection would be possible.

Against this critique, three counter-arguments have traction. First, the distinction between recombination and origination may be less sharp for human creativity than the critique implies: cognitive scientists argue that human creativity is also largely combinatorial and exploratory (Simonton, 2010), and that the experience of "genuine origination" may be a post-hoc phenomenological impression rather than a structural feature of the creative process (Sawyer, 2012). Second, emergent properties — behaviors not deducible from the training objective or the training data — have been documented in large models (Wei et al., 2022), suggesting that scale can produce qualitative discontinuities, not just quantitative ones — though this interpretation is actively contested: Schaeffer et al. (2023) argue that the apparent discontinuities are artifacts of the choice of evaluation metric rather than genuine phase transitions in model capability, a finding that should temper reliance on emergence as evidence for machine creativity. Third, the most compelling cases of machine creativity (Move 37; AlphaFold's protein structure predictions, including structures in regions of fold space absent from its Protein Data Bank training set (Jumper et al., 2021; Akdel et al., 2022; Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2021); generative adversarial network outputs that fool domain experts (Elgammal et al., 2017)) involve systems that explore *beyond* the human-generated training distribution in demonstrable ways.

## 4. Three generations of machine creativity

**Rule-based systems (1950s–1990s):** Early AI creativity research focused on systems that generated outputs by traversing rule-defined spaces: AARON (Harold Cohen's drawing program, 1973–) (Cohen, 1995), BRUTUS (narrative generation) (Bringsjord & Ferrucci, 2000), and musical composition systems such as ILLIAC Suite (Hiller & Isaacson, 1959). These systems produced genuinely novel outputs within their defined spaces, but the conceptual space itself was entirely human-specified. Creativity was in the programmer, not the system.

**Evolutionary and generative systems (1990s–2010s):** Genetic algorithms (Holland, 1975), genetic programming, and later Generative Adversarial Networks (Goodfellow et al., 2014) introduced systems that could *search* a space rather than simply traverse it — and in the case of GANs, learn the space itself from data. Elgammal et al.'s (2017) Creative Adversarial Network (CAN) generated abstract art by training a generator to produce images that deviated from known artistic styles while remaining recognizable as art — an explicit operationalization of Boden's exploratory creativity. Blind human evaluators rated CAN outputs as more creative and inspiring than human-produced art at the same exhibition (Elgammal et al., 2017).

**Foundation model creativity (2020s–):** Large-scale generative models — DALL-E (Ramesh et al., 2022), Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022), Midjourney, Suno, Udio — operate at a qualitatively different scale and scope. They do not search a pre-defined space; they learn an implicit generative model of an entire domain from hundreds of millions of examples. Their outputs can exhibit combinational creativity at a level that consistently surprises and sometimes delights expert practitioners (Elgammal et al., 2017; Köbis & Mossink, 2021). The question of whether they exhibit exploratory creativity — whether they discover possibilities within the human domain that humans had not discovered — is actively contested; the question of transformational creativity is, for most researchers, not yet seriously in play.

## 5. The authorship question: intentionality and curation

If an AI system produces a painting, who is the artist? If it names a financial signal "Liquidity Leverage Intensity" and constructs a theoretically coherent paper around it (Novy-Marx & Velikov, 2025), is that creative writing? The authorship question has legal, philosophical, and practical dimensions that are unresolved and rapidly becoming urgent.

Legally, most jurisdictions do not recognize AI systems as authors; creative works require a human author to qualify for copyright protection. The US Copyright Office has consistently held that AI-generated content without human creative input is not copyrightable (U.S. Copyright Office, 2023; Thaler v. Perlmutter, 2023). Philosophically, authorship is typically connected to *intentionality* — the author's purpose, perspective, and meaning-making — and current AI systems lack intentionality in the philosophically robust sense (Searle, 1980; Shanahan, 2022): they manipulate symbols without understanding what the symbols mean.

Hertzmann (2018) argues that this framing misidentifies the creative agent: AI systems are *tools*, and the creativity lies in the human who designs, trains, prompts, selects, and curates. The photographer who frames a shot is creative even though the camera produces the image; the artist who generates and selects from thousands of DALL-E outputs is creative even though no single output required human mark-making. On this view, the relevant unit of creativity in generative AI is the *human-AI system*, not the AI component alone — which is precisely the hybrid intelligence framing.

The *curation* function is underappreciated in public discourse. Generative systems produce many outputs; creative humans select, refine, combine, and contextualise. The quality and creativity of the final product is substantially determined by the human's curatorial judgment (McGuire et al., 2024). This has implications for how hybrid intelligence systems should be designed: preserving the human's curatorial role may be more important than improving the AI's generative quality.

## 6. Evaluating machine creativity: the FACE and IDEA models

Evaluation is the central methodological challenge in computational creativity research. The field has proposed several frameworks:

The **FACE model** (Colton et al., 2011) evaluates creative systems along four dimensions: *Framing* (how the system presents its output), *Aesthetics* (the system's implicit aesthetic choices), *Concept* (whether the system operates with explicit conceptual representations), and *Expression* (how outputs are realized). A system that merely generates outputs without framing, aesthetic judgment, or conceptual representation is, on this view, less creative than one that demonstrates all four.

The **IDEA model** (Colton et al., 2011) focuses on the conceptual space within which a system operates, asking whether the system can represent, traverse, and — crucially — modify that space. Only systems that can modify their own conceptual space exhibit something approaching transformational creativity.

Wiggins (2006) presents a *Creative Systems Framework* that formalizes this notion more explicitly. In Wiggins' model, a creative system operates over a conceptual space *U* (the universe of possible artifacts), with a set of *R* production rules (the generative grammar or constraints), and an evaluation framework *T* (the taste or aesthetic criteria). Creativity emerges at three levels: applying R to generate novel elements within U; modifying R to enable previously unreachable regions of U (exploratory creativity); or reconceiving U itself (transformational creativity). The framework's power lies in making explicit the role of the evaluation function T — creativity is not just generation but generation *guided by values*. This differs in scope from FACE/IDEA: where FACE evaluates a system's output across four dimensions (Framing, Aesthetics, Concept, Expression) and IDEA assesses conceptual-space modification, Wiggins' framework emphasizes that the values embedded in T — the taste function — are themselves creative. A system cannot exhibit genuine transformational creativity without the capacity to evaluate whether a transformed space is *worth* inhabiting.

In practice, most evaluation of machine creativity relies on human judges assessing outputs, without awareness of whether the output was human- or machine-generated. Studies consistently find that: (a) human judges cannot reliably distinguish AI-generated poetry from human-written poems (Köbis & Mossink, 2021; Porter & Machery, 2024) or AI-synthesized faces from real photographs (Nightingale & Farid, 2022); evidence for similar indistinguishability in the music domain remains more mixed and domain-specific (Civit et al., 2022); (b) when informed that an output is AI-generated, human evaluators rate it lower on creativity and value, even if the output is identical (Ragot et al., 2020) — an attribution bias that raises important questions about how creativity is socially constructed rather than objectively assessed.

## 7. The Novy-Marx/Velikov case: creativity as industrial process

Novy-Marx and Velikov's (2025) AI-powered scholarship experiment provides a concrete and troubling illustration of combinational creativity at industrial scale. Their system generated 288 complete academic papers — each with a creative signal name, a theoretically coherent introduction, and citations to "existing (and, on occasion, imagined) literature" — in approximately 12 hours at a cost of $200–300 in API fees. The signal names ("Liquidity Leverage Intensity," "Accruals Oscillation Sequencing") are combinationally creative: plausible, economically evocative, previously non-existent. The theoretical framings are exploratory in a narrow sense: they select from the space of established economic theories and apply them to novel signals.

What is absent is any claim to transformational creativity, or to the intentionality that would make authorship meaningful. The papers are signed "I. M. Harking" — a joke embedded by the human researchers to name what the machine cannot itself perceive: the epistemic dishonesty of constructing theory post-hoc. The system has no access to the distinction between genuine inquiry and rationalization; it cannot evaluate whether its theoretical framing is honest or merely fluent.

This case illustrates both the reach and the limit of current machine creativity. The reach: at the combinational level, current AI systems can produce outputs that are indistinguishable from human creative work in well-structured domains. The limit: they have no access to the values and purposes that distinguish genuine creativity from sophisticated mimicry. The same architectural capacity—fluent combinatorial generation in structured domains—extends to adjacent fields: LLM-generated medical abstracts are becoming indistinguishable from authentic peer-reviewed abstracts (Gao et al., 2023), and legal hallucinations in AI-written briefs have begun appearing in actual court proceedings (Dahl et al., 2024). The Novy-Marx/Velikov experiment demonstrates proof-of-concept for industrial-scale epistemically hollow content; the infrastructure scales equally well to produce fraudulent medical claims or fabricated legal arguments.

## 8. Implications for hybrid intelligence

For hybrid intelligence design, the creativity literature suggests three practical implications.

First, **AI systems are most useful as generative engines, humans as creative evaluators**. The asymmetry between machine fluency and human judgment defines the optimal division of cognitive labour in creative hybrid systems. Systems that hand off generation to AI while preserving human evaluation, selection, and curation are better aligned with the actual distribution of capabilities than systems that attempt to automate either function entirely.

Second, **combinational creativity is not a lesser form**. The dismissal of AI creativity as "mere recombination" underestimates both the practical value of combinational creativity (most useful innovation is combinatorial) and the cognitive demands of high-quality combination (the space of possible combinations is vast, and quality selection requires genuine understanding). Hybrid systems that leverage AI combinational creativity alongside human evaluative judgment can produce work neither could produce alone.

Third, **the authorship question is not merely philosophical**. Decisions about attribution, credit, and accountability in hybrid creative systems have direct practical consequences for academic integrity, intellectual property, and the design of incentive structures in research, art, and commercial settings. The Novy-Marx/Velikov experiment is a proof-of-concept for industrialized academic fraud as much as for AI creativity; the same architecture that produces interesting financial anomaly papers could produce fraudulent medical claims or legal arguments at the same scale.

## Open questions

- **OQ1 — Move 37 as benchmark:** Is AlphaGo's Move 37 genuinely exploratory creativity, or is it better described as optimization finding a local maximum that human search heuristics had systematically avoided? What would distinguish the two?
- **OQ2 — Emergence and creativity:** Do emergent capabilities in large models constitute evidence for machine creativity, or do they merely show that statistical models can discover patterns humans had not noticed?
- **OQ3 — Curation as creativity:** If human curation is where creativity resides in human-AI systems, how should authorship, credit, and accountability be assigned in creative hybrid systems?
- **OQ4 — The pollution problem:** Novy-Marx/Velikov demonstrate that AI can generate academically fluent but epistemically hollow content at industrial scale. What governance mechanisms could prevent creative commons pollution?
- **OQ5 — Cultural specificity:** Is the Boden taxonomy itself culturally specific — a Western, analytical-philosophical account of creativity that may not map onto non-Western conceptions of creative practice? (Cross-ref Draft W.)
