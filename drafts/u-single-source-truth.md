## Overview and Chapter Position

Hybrid intelligence systems — where human cognition and machine reasoning interoperate — inherit a fundamental epistemological tension: the coexistence of competing worldviews alongside a shared need for verifiable facts. What is new is the speed and scale at which this tension now manifests, and the particular failure mode it produces in AI systems: **hallucination**. The political stakes are significant: Dahlgren (2018, *Javnost – The Public* 25(1–2), 20–27, DOI: 10.1080/13183222.2018.1418819) documents the deepening epistemic crisis of democracy driven by digital media transformation: the fragmentation of shared factual ground, the erosion of trust in authoritative knowledge sources, and the rise of post-truth discourse as a structural feature of the contemporary information environment. The subsequent emergence of hallucination-prone AI systems has substantially amplified this dynamic — making the provision of verifiable, trustworthy knowledge infrastructure not merely a technical problem but a precondition for functional democracy.

This draft argues that hallucination is not primarily a linguistic problem but an **epistemic infrastructure problem**. It emerges where the system lacks anchoring to a formal, authoritative, and navigable source of truth. The solution is not a single authoritative ledger, but a **democratic epistemology**: distributed, community-governed, adversarially reviewed, and permanently accessible — the very infrastructure that the open source, open science, and open data movements have been building for decades.

## 1. Epistemic Plurality and the Grounding Imperative

Hybrid intelligence systems inherit a tension that has occupied epistemologists for centuries: the coexistence of competing worldviews alongside a shared need for verifiable facts. What is new is the scale at which this tension now manifests and the particular failure mode it produces: **hallucination**.

When a large language model (LLM) generates a confident, syntactically coherent, and entirely false claim, the falsehood is structurally indistinguishable from truth without an external referent. Ji et al. (2023, *ACM Computing Surveys* 55(12), DOI: 10.1145/3571730) define *factual hallucination* — generation of content that contradicts verifiable world knowledge — and distinguish it from the more benign *intrinsic* inconsistency within a single document. Rawte et al. (2023, arXiv:2309.05922) extend this taxonomy to foundation models, distinguishing factuality, faithfulness, and contextual consistency as three orthogonal dimensions of LLM truthfulness. The crucial insight across both surveys is that hallucination is not primarily a linguistic problem but an **epistemic infrastructure problem**: it emerges precisely where the system lacks anchoring to a formal, authoritative, and navigable source of truth.

This does not mean that only one worldview can or should prevail. Competing interpretive frameworks — political, cultural, scientific — are a feature of democratic epistemology, not a bug. The distinction that matters is between *contested interpretation* (legitimate) and *contested fact* (resolvable, in principle, by appeal to evidence). Hybrid systems must be designed to hold these apart.

The problem becomes structurally acute in multi-agent hybrid systems. When a human and an AI agent collaborate on a knowledge task, the human cannot easily audit the AI's factual claims without an independent reference. The fluency problem identified in `i-llm-epistemic-limits.md` (Part 1) compounds the issue: LLMs produce confidently-structured text regardless of underlying accuracy, so the surface signal humans use to infer competence (articulateness, specificity, absence of hedging) is decoupled from the underlying epistemic state. A model cannot stammer.

When multiple AI agents collaborate, hallucination can compound: one agent's false claim becomes another agent's input, and the error propagates through the system without any mechanism for detection. The package-hallucination phenomenon documented by Spracklen et al. (2024, arXiv:2406.10279) — LLMs generating references to non-existent software packages at rates up to 20% — is a concrete example of how an unverifiable claim becomes an actionable instruction in an agentic workflow (cf. `g-agentic-hi-collaboration.md`, `e-llm-structural-vulnerabilities.md`).

The academic publishing domain shows the same dynamic at the scale of the entire epistemic infrastructure. Novy-Marx and Velikov (2025, NBER Working Paper 33363) used a commercial LLM to generate 288 complete academic finance papers in approximately 12 hours at a cost of $200–300, each citing "existing (and, on occasion, imagined) literature." The phrase "imagined literature" is structurally precise: the model produced citation strings — plausible authors, journal names, titles, and years — that do not correspond to real publications. The same generative dynamic that hallucinates software package names produces academic reference strings: both are high-probability completions of a familiar textual pattern, generated with no internal signal distinguishing real from invented sources. At $200–300 per 288 papers, industrial-scale contamination of the academic citation graph is economically feasible for any actor with a motive. Motivated adversarial actors — pharmaceutical interests suppressing adverse results (cf. Sismondo & Doucet, 2009, on ghost management of medical publications), financial interests fabricating literature support, state-level disinformation operations — represent foreseeable deliberate attack vectors against the citation graph; analogous operations targeting adjacent publishing contexts have been empirically documented, establishing plausibility for the AI-training-relevant citation graph as a future target. What *has* been documented at scale is the paper mill phenomenon: systematic factories producing fraudulent academic papers for pay, with thousands of fabricated articles infiltrating peer-reviewed journals annually (Else & Van Noorden, 2021; Sabel et al., 2023). The papers were internally signed "I. M. Harking" — a deliberate naming by the researchers of the epistemic failure the machine cannot perceive: **HARKing** (Hypothesizing After Results are Known; Kerr, 1998) is structurally invisible to a generative system because the distinction between genuine inquiry and post-hoc rationalization is a meta-cognitive judgment that operates at a level above the statistical prediction task. The academic citation graph — on which Wikipedia's reference infrastructure, systematic reviews, Wikidata assertions, and RAG retrieval systems all ultimately depend — can be flooded with academically fluent but epistemically hollow content at near-zero marginal cost. The SSOT problem has thus acquired a new dimension: the source of truth can now be polluted faster than it can be reviewed.

## 2. Retrieval-Augmented Grounding: The Technical Response

The dominant engineering response to hallucination has been **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**, first systematically proposed by Lewis et al. (2020, NeurIPS, arXiv:2005.11401) in *Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks*. RAG grounds generative output by dynamically retrieving relevant passages from a curated document corpus before generation. The model's output is conditioned on retrieved evidence, not solely on parametric memory.

What RAG makes explicit is something epistemologically significant: **the quality of AI reasoning is bounded by the quality of its sources**. A RAG system anchored to peer-reviewed literature behaves differently — and more reliably — than one anchored to social media. The architecture makes the source of truth a first-class design parameter.

Subsequent survey work by Gao et al. (2023, arXiv:2312.10997, *Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models: A Survey*; 194 influential citations on Semantic Scholar as of 2026-04) identifies the central unresolved challenge: source selection and trustworthiness. Not all retrievable corpora are equal, and the field lacks consensus on what constitutes an authoritative source for automated reasoning systems. This is not a technical problem — it is an epistemological and governance problem.

The RAG architecture also reveals a recursive dependency: the trustworthiness of a hybrid intelligence system is a function of the trustworthiness of its grounding infrastructure. Investing in open, well-governed knowledge infrastructure is therefore not merely an academic good — it is a precondition for reliable hybrid intelligence.

More recent work has pushed beyond document retrieval toward structured knowledge grounding. Li et al. (2023, arXiv:2305.13269, *Chain-of-Knowledge: Grounding LLMs via Heterogeneous Knowledge Sources*, ICLR; 11 influential citations) demonstrate an architecture in which LLMs query Wikidata, SQL databases, and SPARQL endpoints directly rather than retrieving unstructured text — a harder epistemological commitment that constrains generation to formally structured sources. Dziri et al. (2021, *Neural Path Hunter: Reducing Hallucination via KG Path Grounding*, EMNLP, DOI: 10.18653/v1/2021.emnlp-main.168; 9 influential citations) show a generate-then-refine approach against knowledge-graph paths, achieving a 20% faithfulness improvement over base generation. Both approaches share a key design principle: the source of truth is not a document to be retrieved but a formal structure to be queried — a much stronger epistemic anchor than free-text RAG.

## 3. Open Platforms as Distributed Truth Machines

The most consequential open-infrastructure responses to the grounding problem have emerged not from AI labs but from communities organized around collective knowledge production.

**Wikipedia** is the most studied. Vrandečić and Krötzsch (2014, *Communications of the ACM* 57(10), 78–85, DOI: 10.1145/2629489; 315 influential citations on Semantic Scholar), describing the launch of Wikidata, articulate a key architectural insight: factual claims benefit from structured, machine-readable representation with explicit provenance, rather than prose. Wikidata now serves as a backbone source of truth for numerous AI systems — including, most significantly, Google's Knowledge Graph, following the large-scale migration away from Freebase (Pellissier Tanon et al., 2016) — its value derives not from any central authority but from the institutionalized workflow of verification, citation, and dispute resolution that its community enforces. Jemielniak (2014, *Common Knowledge? An Ethnography of Wikipedia*, Stanford University Press) provides a rich ethnographic account of this governance, showing that Wikipedia's reliability is a social achievement as much as a technical one. As `s-open-knowledge-platforms.md` (Part 2 and Part 6.2) documents, Wikidata's CC0 licensing and 110+ million machine-readable items (Wikidata Foundation, 2024) make it the most epistemically precise of the four core platforms for AI training and grounding — even though Wikipedia is more commonly cited in popular accounts.

The epistemological structure of Wikipedia's truth-claims is important: Wikipedia does not itself claim to be a primary source of truth, but a secondary source that aggregates and cites primary sources. Its rules — verifiability, no original research, neutral point of view — are epistemological commitments, not merely editorial policies. A hybrid intelligence system that retrieves from Wikipedia inherits both the reliability and the limitations of this epistemological architecture.

**GitHub** plays an analogous role for code. When a library, model weight, or dataset is pinned to a specific commit hash, that hash functions as an immutable epistemic anchor — the code *was* this, at this moment, verifiably. The open nature of the platform means any agent can inspect, fork, or challenge. This is epistemology-by-diff: truth is established through traceable change history rather than declaration. As `s-open-knowledge-platforms.md` (Part 3) develops, the GitHub model also illustrates the dependency between the four open platforms and their commercial owners: Microsoft's acquisition of GitHub for $7.5 bn in 2018 (Microsoft Corporation, 2018) made the world's primary procedural-knowledge commons subject to single-firm strategic control — a structural vulnerability for any hybrid intelligence system that depends on it as ground truth (cf. `b-closed-models-risks.md`).

**Open government data** — shaped by frameworks such as the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) articulated by Wilkinson et al. (2016, *Scientific Data* 3:160018, DOI: 10.1038/sdata.2016.18; 604 influential citations on Semantic Scholar) — represents a third register. The FAIR Principles explicitly emphasise machine-actionability: data should be findable and reusable not only by humans but by automated systems, with rich metadata and persistent identifiers. When public institutions publish structured, versioned, openly licensed datasets that conform to FAIR, they create ground truth that AI systems can reliably cite. The political economy matters: FAIR data from a national statistical agency carries institutional accountability that a scraped webpage does not.

**Open-access peer review** rounds out the picture. The reproducibility crisis documented by the Open Science Collaboration (Nosek et al., 2015, *Science* 349(6251), aac4716, DOI: 10.1126/science.aac4716) — showing that fewer than 40% of replicated psychology effects matched original effect sizes — does not undermine science as a source of truth; it demonstrates that the community workflow of falsification is working. The crisis was identified *by the community*, using open data and open methodology. Science's strength is not immunity to error but the institutionalized means of detecting and correcting it.

## 4. The OSINT Model: Community-Verified Truth at the Edge

Perhaps the most instructive contemporary example of distributed epistemic infrastructure is **open-source intelligence (OSINT)** as practiced by organizations like Bellingcat. Founded in 2014 by Eliot Higgins and described in his 2021 book *We Are Bellingcat: An Intelligence Agency for the People* (Bloomsbury), the organization has established an investigative methodology that treats publicly available digital artifacts — satellite imagery, social media posts, flight records, archived web pages — as primary evidence, subject to community verification. Bellingcat's investigations of MH17, the Skripal poisoning, and the Russia–Ukraine war have established the methodology as a recognised epistemic practice (Hauter, 2021) with adoption in journalism, human rights monitoring (Amnesty International's Citizen Evidence Lab; Amnesty International, 2014), and international criminal investigation (OHCHR & UC Berkeley Human Rights Center, 2020).

What Bellingcat represents epistemologically is a **crowd-sourced adversarial review process** applied to high-stakes factual claims (war crimes, state disinformation, geolocation of events). Its methodology is openly published; its conclusions are reproducible by anyone with the same tools and sources. When a Bellingcat finding is challenged, the challenge must engage the evidence chain, not merely assert a counter-claim. This is, structurally, peer review applied to current events.

The OSINT model has several features directly relevant to hybrid intelligence design:

1. **Provenance-first epistemology**: every claim is accompanied by its evidence trail — not just the conclusion, but the path by which it was reached
2. **Adversarial verification**: the community actively attempts to falsify findings, not merely to confirm them
3. **Tool transparency**: the investigative tools (Google Maps, flight tracking databases, satellite imagery) are named and accessible to any verifier
4. **Temporal anchoring**: claims are tied to specific times and places, via archived sources, not floating assertions

For hybrid intelligence, the OSINT model points toward something important: **truth is not stored, it is procedurally established**. An AI system that can interface with such workflows, rather than merely scraping their outputs, would be epistemically far more robust. The OSINT methodology is in this sense a working answer to the problem identified in `i-llm-epistemic-limits.md` (Part 3): unknown unknowns can only be addressed by adversarial verification, not by introspection. A model cannot reliably know what it does not know — but a community engaged in active falsification can detect what no individual investigator could.

### 4a. OSINT as a Design Pattern for Agentic Verification

The practical implications for hybrid intelligence architecture are significant. A hybrid system tasked with fact-checking or sense-making should embed, not eliminate, the adversarial-verification loop. Rather than asking an AI agent to determine truth independently, the system should scaffold the agent's reasoning through structured adversarial questioning: what counter-evidence would falsify this claim? Which sources are explicitly attempting to deny this? What gaps remain in the evidence chain? This is formalized in the *dialectical argument* tradition (van Eemeren & Grootendorst 2004, *A Systematic Theory of Argumentation*, Kluwer Academic Publishers) and in empirical AI-human collaboration studies. Freeman et al. (2015, *Cognition and Instruction* 33(2), 111–147, DOI: 10.1080/07370008.2015.1028421) demonstrate that students who engage in peer argument and counter-argument develop stronger epistemic reasoning than those who receive corrective feedback alone. For AI agents, the implication is that adversarial interaction with human domain experts (or with other agents assigned the role of devil's advocate) produces more reliable outputs than monolithic generation. Direct experimental evidence from AI systems supports this: Du et al. (2023) show that multi-agent debate — where independent model instances propose and challenge each other's answers over multiple rounds — substantially improves factual accuracy and reasoning quality on knowledge-intensive benchmarks compared to single-model generation, providing an AI-native empirical anchor for the adversarial-verification design pattern.

More concretely, a hybrid intelligence system designed around OSINT principles would implement three architectural features:
1. **Evidence-chain transparency**: every factual claim the agent makes is accompanied not only by its conclusion but by the path of sources and reasoning used to reach it (provenance), as specified in the W3C PROV Data Model — see the Recommendation (Belhajjame et al. 2013, *PROV-DM: The PROV Data Model*, W3C Recommendation, 30 April 2013) and the canonical academic introduction by Moreau and Groth (2013, *Provenance: An Introduction to PROV*, Synthesis Lectures on the Semantic Web 7(2), Morgan & Claypool, DOI: 10.2200/S00528ED1V01Y201308WBE007; OA cit: 124). The PROV vocabulary's three core classes — Entity, Activity, Agent — and its relations (`wasGeneratedBy`, `used`, `wasAttributedTo`, `wasDerivedFrom`) provide a standardised way to express *who produced what, using which input, when, and on whose behalf* — exactly the structure required to make agent reasoning subject to community inspection. The PROV family interoperates with Linked Data publishing conventions (Bizer, Heath & Berners-Lee 2009, *Linked Data — The Story So Far*, International Journal on Semantic Web and Information Systems 5(3), 1–22, DOI: 10.4018/jswis.2009081901; OA cit: 4554), so a provenance graph emitted by an agent can be dereferenced by URI, joined with external datasets via SPARQL, and audited against open vocabularies — the same architecture that has supported scientific data publishing on the Linked Open Data cloud for over a decade.
2. **Structured falsification workflows**: the system should support explicit requests for counter-evidence, alternative interpretations, and acknowledged weaknesses in the current understanding — treating these not as defects but as required elements of epistemically trustworthy output.
3. **Tool transparency and reproducibility**: following Bellingcat's public methodology, the specific tools used to retrieve or verify information (APIs, databases, search parameters, timestamp) should be disclosed so that any user can independently verify the same sources.

These patterns are compatible with (not additions to) the RAG and structured knowledge-grounding approaches described in Section 2. What OSINT adds is the adversarial framing: grounding systems are epistemically stronger when they are designed to be challenged and revised, not merely when they cite sources.

## 5. Permanence and Provenance: Permalinks, Archive.org, and DOIs

A pervasive and underappreciated threat to epistemic infrastructure is **link rot** — the disappearance of cited sources over time. Zittrain, Albert and Lessig (2014, *Harvard Law Review Forum* 127, 176–199, "Perma: Scoping and Addressing the Problem of Link and Reference Rot in Legal Citations") found that approximately 50% of URLs in Supreme Court opinions and 70% in Harvard Law Review articles no longer resolved to their original content. The problem is structural: the web is architecturally present-tense.

Several infrastructure responses exist:

- **DOIs** (Digital Object Identifiers) provide stable, resolver-independent citation handles for academic publications, governed by CrossRef. A DOI is not merely a URL but a commitment to persistent resolution, backed by institutional accountability (Klein et al., 2014).
- **The Internet Archive** (archive.org), founded by Brewster Kahle, now holds over 916 billion web pages (Internet Archive, 2024) and provides Wayback Machine permalinks that fix a document's content at a specific timestamp. For AI systems and hybrid intelligence workflows, Archive.org permalinks offer a form of *temporal provenance*: not merely what a source says, but what it said, when.
- **Perma.cc**, developed specifically for legal scholarship, allows verified institutions to create tamper-evident, preserved citations.

For hybrid intelligence architectures, the implication is clear: sources cited by AI systems should carry permanent, timestamped, provenance-verified handles. A claim grounded in an Archive.org permalink is epistemically superior to one grounded in a live URL — the former cannot silently change.

This connects directly to the hallucination problem: a system that cites a live URL offers no guarantee that the cited content will match what was retrieved at inference time. Epistemic integrity in hybrid systems requires not just retrieval, but *archived retrieval with timestamp and hash*.

The challenge at the user interface level is equally important. Feng et al. (2023, arXiv:2303.12118, CSCW, DOI: 10.1145/3610061; 2 influential citations) ran a controlled experiment on C2PA-style provenance metadata in media contexts, finding that users do lower their trust in content flagged as deceptive — but crucially, they conflate *media credibility* (is this outlet trustworthy?) with *provenance credibility* (is the claimed origin verifiable?). For SSOT design in hybrid intelligence systems, this is a key finding: displaying a provenance chain is insufficient if users lack the conceptual frame to interpret it. Epistemic transparency must be accompanied by epistemic literacy — a design requirement that connects the technical infrastructure of permalinks and content hashes to the broader democratic-education agenda of the Velux context.

### 5a. EU AI Act Compliance and Provenance Infrastructure

The permanence and provenance principles articulated above carry direct regulatory implications in the European context. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 June 2024 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act), OJ L, 12 July 2024, imposes two record-and-disclosure obligations directly relevant to single-source-of-truth design: **Article 12** requires providers of high-risk AI systems to enable automatic logging of events ("logs") over the system's lifetime sufficient to ensure traceability of functioning, identification of risk situations, and post-market monitoring; **Article 50** imposes transparency obligations on providers and deployers of certain AI systems (including the obligation to mark synthetic audio/image/video/text as artificially generated in a machine-readable format and to disclose AI interaction to natural persons). Both obligations require organisations deploying AI systems to maintain and disclose detailed records of the data, methods, and decision processes involved in system operation. The PROV Data Model vocabulary cited in §4a — Entity, Activity, Agent and their associated relations — provides a machine-readable schema for encoding precisely this regulatory requirement. A hybrid intelligence system that implements PROV-compliant provenance logging satisfies both the technical epistemic requirement (sources are inspectable and traceable) and the regulatory compliance requirement (record-keeping obligations are mechanically enforceable).

The practical wiring of §4a (PROV-based agentic provenance) to EU AI Act compliance is beyond this chapter's scope — it requires legal-technical integration work that sits at the boundary between governance and architecture. The theoretical foundation is secure on two anchors. First, Bommasani et al. (2023, *The Foundation Model Transparency Index*, arXiv:2310.12941) document the *information* dimensions — data, compute, methods, evaluation, downstream impact — along which foundation-model providers should make claims auditable; their 100-indicator scoring framework is one explicit articulation of what "record-keeping" in the Article 12 sense would have to cover when applied to general-purpose AI. Second, Liesenfeld and Dingemanse (2024, *Rethinking open source generative AI: open washing and the EU AI Act*, ACM FAccT 2024, pp. 1774–1787, DOI: 10.1145/3630106.3659005; OA cit: 49) provide the peer-reviewed empirical anchor for the openness-claim vs. AI-Act-compliance argument: across 45+ generative AI systems they distinguish 14 dimensions of openness and document that many "open weight" claims withhold precisely the training-data and fine-tuning information that the AI Act's transparency provisions are designed to surface. Their framework operationalises the gap between marketed openness and regulatorily-disclosable provenance — exactly the gap that PROV-style logging would close. PROV supplies the technical schema; Bommasani's index supplies the audit-content checklist; Liesenfeld and Dingemanse supply the empirical baseline showing how far current practice is from either. Implementation of this integration is recommended as a priority for v0.7 (see Next).

## 6. Blockchain: Immutable Ledgers and Their Epistemic Limits

Blockchain technology has been proposed as a solution to the provenance and mutability problem. Nakamoto's 2008 Bitcoin whitepaper (*Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System*, bitcoin.org) established the core mechanism: a distributed, append-only ledger where each block cryptographically commits to all prior blocks via Merkle-tree hashing, making retroactive alteration computationally infeasible. The epistemic appeal is obvious — an immutable record of what was claimed, when, by whom.

The concept of cryptographic timestamping predates Bitcoin by 17 years. Haber and Stornetta (1991, *Journal of Cryptology* 3(2), 99–111, DOI: 10.1007/BF00196791) published the foundational paper *How to Time-Stamp a Digital Document* — establishing the technical foundation for immutable provenance long before blockchain became a cultural phenomenon. Their core insight: what matters for epistemic purposes is not who said something, but *proof that it was said when it was claimed to have been said*. Their original protocol used hash-chaining and trusted timestamping services; later work extended this to fully decentralized notarization, the conceptual ancestor of Bitcoin's design.

Applications to knowledge infrastructure include timestamping scientific claims (e.g. OriginStamp, ProvenanceDB), supply chain provenance, and credential verification.

However, blockchain's epistemic limitations are significant and often underappreciated. These are not implementation defects — they are structural properties of the architecture:

1. **Garbage-in, garbage-in (the truth-vs-record distinction)**: An immutable ledger records *that* a claim was made; it cannot establish the claim's truth. A false assertion committed to a blockchain is immutably false — and now permanently so. Blockchain provides *non-repudiation of writing*, not *truthfulness of content*. For a hybrid intelligence system, this distinction is critical: a citation chain anchored to a blockchain timestamp tells you that a claim existed at time T, not that the claim was correct at time T or remains correct now.
2. **Oracle problem**: Connecting blockchain records to real-world facts requires trusted intermediaries — oracles — which reintroduce the very trust problem the ledger was meant to dissolve (Caldarelli, 2020). Every published "fact" on Ethereum about, say, a temperature reading or an election result is only as trustworthy as the oracle that posted it. The cryptographic guarantees do not extend across the blockchain–world boundary.
3. **Governance is off-chain**: The hard forks of Bitcoin (2017, BCH) and Ethereum (2016, after the DAO hack) demonstrate that even immutable ledgers are subject to social consensus — when communities disagree, they split the chain. The Ethereum fork is the canonical example: confronted with $50M of stolen funds, the community chose to *rewrite* the supposedly immutable history (Atzei et al., 2017; Mehar et al., 2019). The truth, ultimately, was still decided by the community, not the protocol. What looked like immutability turned out to be a Schelling point that holds only as long as participants want it to.
4. **Accessibility and permanence**: Blockchain nodes can go offline; accessing historical blockchain state requires infrastructure that may not persist. The Internet Archive's non-blockchain approach has, ironically, proven more durable for web archiving purposes than most blockchain provenance projects of the 2010s, many of which now have unreachable endpoints.

By contrast, the traditional scientific publication system — for all its well-documented pathologies (publication bias, paywalls, replication failures, the reproducibility crisis documented by Nosek et al. 2015) — embeds truth claims in a social institution with explicit accountability mechanisms: authorship, peer review, retraction, citation. When a paper is retracted (e.g., the Wakefield MMR paper, Lancet 1998 / retracted 2010 (The Lancet Editors, 2010)), the scientific record updates. When a finding fails to replicate (e.g., the social-priming effects of the early 2010s) (Doyen et al., 2012), the scientific record responds — though not uniformly. Contrary to the intuitive assumption that non-replicable findings lose citation traction, Serra-Garcia & Gneezy (2021) found that papers reporting non-replicable results in top psychology and economics journals are cited *more*, not less, than replicable ones even after replication failures are published, suggesting that citation counts alone are not reliable signals of findings' epistemic standing. Nonetheless, meta-analyses and systematic reviews do incorporate replication failures, and the institutional capacity to revise prior consensus remains present. **The ability to be corrected is epistemically stronger than the inability to be changed.**

This is a counterintuitive but important result: in epistemology, revisability is a virtue, not a weakness. A system that can update its beliefs in response to evidence is more epistemically trustworthy than one that cannot. The immutability that makes blockchain attractive for finance — where you do not want anyone to retroactively edit the ledger — is precisely the property that makes it inadequate for knowledge, where you very much do want the record to update when the world is shown to differ from prior belief.

The synthesis: blockchain is a useful tool for *timestamping* and *provenance*, narrowly construed — proving that a claim existed in a specific form at a specific time. It is a poor tool for *truth* in the substantive sense. The Haber–Stornetta primitive (1991) is sound; the conflation of timestamp with truth in the Web3 era is not. A well-designed hybrid intelligence system can use blockchain timestamping for citation provenance while relying on community-governed scientific publication for truth-claim adjudication. The architectures are complementary, not substitutable.

## 7. Toward a Democratic Epistemology for Hybrid Intelligence

What emerges from this analysis is not a single source of truth but an **ecosystem of epistemically differentiated sources**, each with characteristic strengths, failure modes, and governance structures:

| Source Type | Strength | Failure Mode | Governance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peer-reviewed science | Adversarial review, replication | Slow, paywalled, publication bias | Editorial boards, community |
| Wikipedia / Wikidata | Machine-readable, maintained, multilingual | Vandalism, systemic bias | Community workflow |
| GitHub / open code | Exact, versioned, reproducible | Abandonware, supply chain attacks | Maintainer community |
| Open government data (FAIR) | Authoritative, structured | Political pressure, gaps | Institutional accountability |
| OSINT (Bellingcat model) | Real-time, adversarial, transparent | Misidentification, scalability | Community methodology |
| Archive.org permalinks | Temporal provenance, permanence | Dependent on single institution | Non-profit governance |
| Blockchain timestamps | Immutability, decentralization | Oracle problem, no truth guarantee | Protocol consensus |

The design implication for hybrid intelligence is that **epistemic robustness requires source pluralism with explicit provenance**. No single architecture of truth is sufficient. What is needed is not a single ledger or a single platform, but a democratic epistemology with four characteristic properties:

1. **Distributed authority**: no single institution adjudicates what is true. Multiple, partially-overlapping communities maintain partially-overlapping bodies of verified knowledge.
2. **Community governance with documented procedures**: each source's reliability rests on a workflow — Wikipedia's verifiability rules, peer review, GitHub's pull-request review, OSINT's evidence-chain methodology. The procedure, not the conclusion, is the epistemic warrant.
3. **Adversarial review**: claims are tested by people incentivised to falsify them, not only confirmed by people incentivised to validate them. This is what distinguishes science from public relations and OSINT from rumour.
4. **Permanent accessibility with provenance**: claims, evidence, and revision history are preserved with timestamped, content-addressed handles (DOIs, Archive.org permalinks, commit hashes, content hashes) so that any claim can be re-examined later under the same conditions.

This is, notably, what the open source, open science, and open data movements have been building — in code, in articles, in databases — for decades. They have produced what Benkler (2006, *The Wealth of Networks*) called "commons-based peer production": a third mode of organising productive activity, alongside markets and hierarchies, governed by neither price signals nor managerial command. Hybrid intelligence inherits this infrastructure whether or not it acknowledges the inheritance. The challenge is to make the infrastructure legible to automated systems without flattening the community workflows that give it its epistemic authority. A RAG system that retrieves from Wikipedia inherits the verifiability policy *only if it preserves the citations*; an AI agent that summarises a Bellingcat investigation inherits the methodology *only if it preserves the evidence chain*.

The philosophical grounding for this architecture is provided by Irzik and Kurtulmus (2019, *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 70(4), DOI: 10.1093/bjps/axy007; OA: philsci-archive.pitt.edu/15370/), who distinguish *basic epistemic trust* — deferring to a source because it says something — from *enhanced epistemic trust* — deferring to a source because one understands the process by which it arrives at claims. Open science, OSINT methodology, and FAIR data all belong to the enhanced category: their trustworthiness is warranted not by their authority but by the transparency of their procedures. For hybrid intelligence design, this distinction is operational: AI systems should be grounded in sources that support enhanced epistemic trust, meaning sources where the verification workflow is itself open and inspectable.

The failure cost of getting this wrong is not merely technical error — it is what Kay, Kasirzadeh and Mohamed (2024, arXiv:2408.11441, AIES) call *generative algorithmic epistemic injustice*: four distinct mechanisms by which LLMs corrupt collective knowledge ecosystems — not by lying, but by systematically amplifying some voices, suppressing others, and presenting epistemic closure where there should be genuine contestation. The SSOT problem is therefore also an epistemic-justice problem: whose knowledge gets to serve as ground truth for AI systems shapes whose view of the world those systems amplify.

The tension between *single source of truth* (engineering desideratum) and *many competing worldviews* (democratic reality) cannot be resolved by technology alone. It requires institutional design: governance structures that encode pluralism at the level of sources while maintaining verifiability at the level of facts. The four open platforms analysed in `s-open-knowledge-platforms.md` are, in this light, not just data sources for AI training — they are the institutional foundation for trustworthy hybrid intelligence. Their continued health is not a research footnote; it is a precondition for the entire enterprise.

This argument has direct policy consequences, particularly relevant in the European context of digital sovereignty (cf. the Velux Foundation democratic-sustainability framing in companion working papers): if open knowledge infrastructures are the substrate of trustworthy AI, then defunding them, paywalling them, or allowing their commercial capture is not a neutral economic decision — it is a decision against verifiable hybrid intelligence and, in the longer run, against the conditions for democratic deliberation in an AI-mediated information environment.

## Open Questions

1. **Can RAG systems distinguish contested interpretation from contested fact?** The current architecture retrieves and ranks — it does not adjudicate. What governance mechanisms are needed?
2. **What is the provenance standard for agentic HI?** If an AI agent cites a source, what metadata should accompany that citation — retrieval time, archive hash, confidence score?
3. **How does OSINT methodology scale to AI-assisted verification?** Bellingcat employs human investigators; can the adversarial verification model be partially automated without losing its epistemic properties?
4. **Blockchain as timestamp vs. blockchain as truth store**: the former is epistemically defensible; the latter is not. How do we maintain this distinction in practice?
5. **Democratic epistemology and digital sovereignty**: who governs the sources of truth for AI systems deployed in public institutions? This connects directly to the digital sovereignty argument in the Velux project context.

6. **Can the academic citation graph be sanitized once imagined literature enters at industrial scale?** Novy-Marx and Velikov (2025) demonstrate that hallucinated citations can be produced at under $1 per paper. What detection mechanisms — DOI-verification bots, citation-graph anomaly detection, mandatory machine-readable provenance metadata in journal submission systems — could prevent this at intake, before contaminated papers enter the databases that RAG systems retrieve from? And what does the answer imply for governance of preprint servers (arXiv, SSRN, bioRxiv) that lack pre-publication peer review?
