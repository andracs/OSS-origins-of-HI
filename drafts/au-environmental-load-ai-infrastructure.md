## Overview and Chapter Position

The AI boom arrived with a promise: intelligence at scale, on demand, for everyone. It arrived also with a bill. Between 2022 and 2026, the electricity consumption of data centres grew faster than at any point in the preceding two decades. The water required to cool the servers training and serving frontier models surpassed the annual freshwater consumption of mid-sized cities. The carbon embedded in the hardware supply chain — in the mining, refining, and fabrication of the chips that make inference possible — exceeded the operational emissions of entire national grid sectors.

This chapter documents that bill in concrete terms. It is the empirical companion to Draft Z (Frugal AI), which proposes design principles for reducing AI's environmental cost. Draft AU establishes what the cost actually is — drawing on institutional projections, corporate sustainability disclosures, and peer-reviewed lifecycle analyses — and examines why the pre-2022 trajectory of efficiency gains that kept data centre energy consumption roughly flat despite rapidly growing compute demand no longer holds in an era of foundation models trained at unprecedented scale and deployed at internet-wide inference volumes.

The chapter argues three things. First, the AI boom has broken the historical decoupling of compute growth from energy growth — a decoupling that characterised 2010–2020 and led many analysts to underproject current demand. Second, the environmental burden of AI is asymmetrically distributed: it is produced by a handful of hyperscalers in water-stressed regions and paid by populations with no say in deployment decisions. Third, current corporate and regulatory disclosure frameworks are structurally insufficient to hold the infrastructure accountable — in part because the most energy-intensive phase of AI (training) is opaque, and in part because the most rapidly growing phase (inference at scale) is distributed across products whose energy cost is invisible to end users.

## §1 — The Baseline: Data Centre Energy Before the AI Boom

### 1.1 The efficiency decade (2010–2020)

Global data centre electricity consumption grew remarkably slowly between 2010 and 2020, despite compute workloads increasing by a factor of six over the same period. The International Energy Agency (IEA, 2020) estimated total data centre electricity use at approximately 200–250 TWh per year in 2020 — roughly 1% of global electricity consumption — nearly unchanged from 2010 despite a vast expansion in cloud services, streaming, and internet infrastructure.

The explanation was efficiency operating across three simultaneous vectors. First, silicon efficiency: transistor density continued to improve, delivering more compute per watt even as absolute clock speeds plateaued. Second, utilisation efficiency: the migration from enterprise-owned hardware — typically running at 10–15% utilisation with a power usage effectiveness (PUE) ratio of 2.0–2.5, meaning two watts consumed for every watt of actual compute — to hyperscaler cloud infrastructure running at 60–70% utilisation with PUE approaching 1.1–1.2. PUE measures total facility power divided by IT equipment power; a PUE of 1.0 is theoretical perfection; Google reported a trailing twelve-month average of 1.10 across its global fleet in 2020. Third, workload consolidation: the shift to virtualisation and containerisation enabled more tasks to share physical resources that were previously idle. Together, these drove a roughly 6× improvement in compute-per-kWh over the decade (Shehabi et al., 2016).

This efficiency trajectory shaped decade-long projections. The IEA's 2022 estimates projected data centre energy consumption growing modestly to 260–340 TWh by 2026. Those projections did not anticipate foundation models.

### 1.2 What changed: the foundation model inflection

The training of GPT-3 in 2020 — consuming an estimated 1,287 MWh of electricity and producing approximately 502 tonnes of CO2 equivalent (Patterson et al., 2021) — was not in itself a global-scale energy event. It was a signal. Foundation models at GPT-3 scale were roughly 3 orders of magnitude more energy-intensive to train than BERT (Strubell, Ganesh & McCallum, 2019); models at GPT-4 scale were estimated to require 50–100× more compute than GPT-3 (Epoch AI, 2024 — public estimates only; OpenAI has not officially disclosed GPT-4 training compute). The economics of foundation models created structural incentives to scale training runs as rapidly as capital and cooling supply would permit.

The shift from training to inference at scale compounded the effect. The most energy-intensive LLMs benchmarked by Jegham et al. (2025) consumed up to 29 Wh per long prompt — over 65× the energy of the most efficient deployed systems — and even short queries (≈0.42 Wh) at 700 million queries per day equate to the annual electricity consumption of approximately 35,000 US homes. Earlier industry estimates that placed a single ChatGPT query at 2.9–10× the energy of a standard Google search query (Goldman Sachs, 2024) are consistent with the upper end of Jegham et al.'s range, though Google's own production data (Elsworth et al., 2025) reports a substantially lower median of 0.24 Wh per Gemini Apps text prompt — reflecting both architecture-specific efficiency and the variance Jegham et al. observed across deployments.

## §2 — Energy: Scale, Growth, and Projections

### 2.1 Current figures (2024–2026)

The IEA's *Electricity 2024* report (published March 2024) put global data centre electricity consumption at 460 TWh in 2022 — roughly 2% of global electricity demand — and projected it to exceed 1,000 TWh by 2026, with data centres, AI, and cryptocurrency together accounting for the doubling. The same report projected AI-specific demand to grow at least tenfold from its 2023 baseline by 2026 (IEA, 2024). The IEA's subsequent *Energy and AI* report (April 2025) revised the 2024 baseline to approximately 415 TWh and projected the trajectory to slow without reversing, reaching ~485 TWh in 2025.

The IEA's dedicated *Energy and AI* report (April 2025) — the most comprehensive authoritative assessment to date — projects data centre electricity consumption to more than double between 2024 and 2030, reaching approximately 945 TWh by 2030 and accounting for just under 3% of global electricity demand. The 2030 figure is roughly equivalent to Japan's current annual electricity consumption. The report further projects that the AI-specific share of data centre power use will rise from 5–15% currently to 35–50% by 2030, with AI-optimised facilities growing far faster than overall data centre capacity (IEA, 2025).

Goldman Sachs Research (2024) projected a 160% increase in data centre power demand between 2023 and 2030, driven primarily by AI inference workloads. The report identified data centre energy supply as a potential constraint on AI deployment timelines — not compute availability, but grid capacity and cooling water access (Goldman Sachs, 2024) [R].

### 2.2 Lifecycle energy: training versus inference

Early environmental analyses of AI focused on training costs — the one-time energy expenditure of producing a model. Strubell et al.'s (2019) foundational analysis estimated training a single large Transformer NLP model at 626,000 kg CO2e, comparable to the lifetime emissions of five American automobiles. Patterson et al. (2022) subsequently argued that training costs, while large, would plateau and shrink relative to inference costs as models matured and hardware efficiency improved.

The lifecycle picture is more nuanced. Elsworth et al. (2025), drawing on Google's full-stack production measurements (accelerator power, host systems, idle capacity, and data centre overhead), reported a median 0.24 Wh per Gemini Apps text prompt and documented a 33× reduction in energy consumption and a 44× reduction in carbon footprint per query over a single year — driven by a combination of architectural efficiency gains and renewable energy procurement. Morrison et al. (2025) reached complementary conclusions using a holistic lifecycle methodology that accounted for hardware embodied energy, operational energy across training and inference, and end-of-life disposal — finding that, when integrated across realistic operational lifetimes, inference energy can substantially exceed training energy.

The implication for environmental governance is significant: training costs, though spectacular, are a one-time expenditure per model version. Inference costs accumulate continuously with usage. A highly capable model deployed at internet scale may consume more energy in a single month of inference than its entire training run, but the *intensity* per query — as Elsworth et al.'s 44× year-on-year improvement shows — is also a moving target whose trajectory determines whether aggregate inference energy growth tracks or outpaces deployment growth.

### 2.3 Geographic concentration and grid stress

AI infrastructure is geographically concentrated in regions whose grids may not accommodate the projected growth without significant new generation capacity. Northern Virginia is the largest data centre market in the world, accounting for approximately 13% of global operational data centre capacity and 25% of capacity in the Americas (Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission of Virginia [JLARC], 2024) — older industry estimates that the region carries 70% of global internet traffic are widely cited but were specifically not credited by JLARC's analysts on review. The state's grid operator Dominion Energy filed its 2024 Integrated Resource Plan (Virginia State Corporation Commission Docket PUR-2024-00184) projecting that data-centre-driven demand would *double* unconstrained Virginia electricity demand within ten years — the most dramatic load-growth projection from any major US grid operator since the post-war industrial expansion (JLARC, 2024). Virginia electricity demand had been essentially flat from 2006 to 2020 before this inflection.

Ireland, which hosts European facilities for Google, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon, saw data centres account for 21% of national metered electricity consumption in 2023 — up from 5% in 2015, with absolute consumption rising from 1,238 GWh to 6,334 GWh, a 412% increase over eight years (Central Statistics Office Ireland, 2024). The trajectory had already prompted the national grid operator EirGrid to impose a *de facto* moratorium on new data centre connections in the Dublin region from 2022. The Netherlands imposed its own partial moratorium in the Amsterdam area in 2022. Singapore suspended new data centre approvals from 2019 to 2022 for identical grid-stress reasons. The pattern is consistent: data centre demand is outrunning grid infrastructure and local regulatory capacity simultaneously in every major hub.

The geographic concentration problem has a secondary dimension: many high-compute AI data centres are sited in regions chosen for cheap land, low taxes, and political stability — not for renewable energy availability. Texas and Northern Virginia have largely fossil-dependent grids; Arizona and New Mexico, which host growing AI infrastructure, face both carbon intensity problems and water stress simultaneously. Conversely, the Nordic countries (Norway, Sweden, Finland, Iceland) and the Pacific Northwest offer hydroelectric or geothermal grids with carbon intensities below 50 gCO2e/kWh — roughly 10–20× less carbon-intensive than coal-dependent grids. The gap in grid carbon intensity between the cleanest and dirtiest data centre siting options exceeds an order of magnitude.

This concentration also creates acute interdependency: a single transformer failure or grid constraint event in Northern Virginia has outsized global consequences for AI service availability. The vulnerability is as much geopolitical as it is environmental.

### 2.4 The renewable energy paradox: additionality and 24/7 matching

Major hyperscalers claim, in aggregate, to be powered by renewable energy. Amazon, Google, and Microsoft each publish annual renewable energy coverage figures that, on paper, approach or exceed 100%. These claims warrant scrutiny.

The standard mechanism is a renewable energy certificate (REC) or power purchase agreement (PPA): a company agrees to purchase the output of a wind or solar farm, claiming that electricity as its own. The problem is temporal and geographic matching. A certificate purchased for 1 MWh of solar generation at noon in California does not guarantee that a data centre in Virginia at midnight was powered by renewables; the grid draws on whatever generation is cheapest and available at that moment. Market-based accounting (using certificates) and location-based accounting (using the actual carbon intensity of the grid at time of use) can differ by a factor of 2–5 for the same facility.

Google has committed to operating on 24/7 carbon-free energy (CFE) — meaning every megawatt-hour of electricity consumed is matched, hour by hour, to a megawatt-hour of renewable generation in the same grid region — and has published granular progress data toward this goal. This is substantially more stringent than annual matching and more accurately reflects actual operational carbon intensity. Google's *2024 Environmental Report* (covering FY2023) reported "a global average of 64% CFE" for the year, with 10 of its grid regions achieving at least 90% CFE and others substantially lower (Google, 2024). The 64% global figure rose to roughly 66% in 2024 following 2.5 GW of new clean-energy capacity additions, but the year-on-year increase in absolute energy demand outpaced the percentage improvement — Google's *2025 Environmental Report* documented a 27% year-on-year rise in data centre electricity consumption.

A further complication is *additionality*: a PPA is only environmentally meaningful if it funds the construction of new renewable capacity rather than redirecting existing generation. Evidence that hyperscaler demand has directly caused new renewable plants to be built is mixed — and the pace of new renewable commissioning in the US and Europe has not kept pace with the acceleration of data centre demand that began in 2022.

The practical implication is that corporate claims of "100% renewable" data centres describe a financial accounting position, not a physical reality. For users seeking to minimise their AI energy footprint, the operational carbon intensity of the specific region and time window of a query matters more than a provider's headline renewable percentage. Tools such as Electricity Maps and WattTime publish near-real-time grid carbon intensity data at the level of individual grid zones — the infrastructure now exists for carbon-aware query routing; the question is whether providers and users will use it.

## §3 — Water: The Invisible Footprint

### 3.1 Cooling water at scale

Data centre cooling requires water in two forms: direct cooling water (evaporated to remove heat from server rooms) and indirect water (consumed upstream to generate the electricity powering the facility). Large hyperscaler facilities can consume millions of litres of freshwater per day in direct cooling alone.

Li et al. (2023) provided the first systematic analysis of AI's water footprint, estimating that training GPT-3 in Microsoft's US data centres "can directly evaporate 700,000 litres of clean freshwater" — a figure that, while large in absolute terms, was dwarfed by the projected inference demand. Li et al. estimated that a ChatGPT conversation of 20–50 exchanges consumes approximately 500 ml of water equivalent — roughly a standard water bottle per moderately sized conversation — and projected that global AI demand could account for 4.2–6.6 billion cubic metres of water withdrawal by 2027, exceeding the annual total water withdrawal of Denmark or roughly half that of the United Kingdom (Li et al., 2023) [P] [OA cit: 151]. Jegham et al. (2025) extended the inference-side analysis: at 700 million daily queries, evaporative freshwater consumption alone equates to the annual drinking-water needs of approximately 1.2 million people.

### 3.2 Corporate disclosures and water stress

Corporate sustainability disclosures document the AI boom's water impact with unusual directness. Microsoft's *2025 Environmental Sustainability Report* (covering FY24) shows water replenishment commitments scaling almost in lockstep with the company's AI infrastructure build-out — from 13.2 million m³ contracted in FY22, to 25.5 million m³ in FY23, to 50.0 million m³ in FY24 (a near-doubling each year), reported alongside Microsoft's stated commitment to become water positive by 2030 (Microsoft, 2025) [R]. The replenishment scaling implies a comparable trajectory for underlying consumption: the company's own reporting frames the FY24 figure as a response to operational water use that has grown faster than originally projected. Google's *2024 Environmental Report* recorded total operational water consumption of 6.4 billion gallons (≈ 24.2 million m³) in 2023, with 95% (6.1 billion gallons) attributable to data centre cooling — and reported water-stewardship-replenishment projects covering only 18% of that 2023 freshwater consumption. Google's *2025 Environmental Report* (covering FY2024) updated total water consumption to approximately 8.1 billion gallons (≈ 30.7 million m³), a year-on-year increase consistent with the documented 27% rise in data centre electricity demand (Google, 2024; Google, 2025) [R].

The water stress dimension is particularly acute in arid regions where data centre development has concentrated for land cost and tax incentive reasons: Arizona, New Mexico, and parts of the American Southwest — regions already experiencing multi-year drought conditions — host significant AI infrastructure whose cooling needs compete directly with agricultural and municipal water supplies.

## §4 — Carbon: Operational, Embodied, and Supply-Chain Emissions

### 4.1 Operational carbon

The carbon intensity of AI infrastructure's operational energy depends critically on the grid mix at the data centre location. Google's data centres, co-located with renewable energy purchase agreements, report Scope 2 emissions (purchased electricity) at near-zero carbon intensity in some facilities. Microsoft and Amazon similarly report significant shares of renewable energy coverage.

However, Google's own 2024 Environmental Report revealed that Scope 2 emissions had *increased* significantly year-on-year, attributing the increase to AI infrastructure growth outpacing renewable energy procurement capacity. The gap between committed renewable energy purchase and actual grid draw — during periods when renewable generation is insufficient and the grid falls back on fossil generation — is a systematic accounting gap in corporate sustainability claims (Elsworth et al., 2025).

### 4.2 Embodied carbon and hardware supply chain

Operational energy is the visible portion of AI's carbon footprint. Embodied carbon — the emissions produced in mining the materials, fabricating the chips, and assembling the servers — may represent a comparable or larger share of lifecycle emissions for AI hardware, particularly given the 12–18 month replacement cycles driven by the rapid pace of AI chip development.

The fabrication of NVIDIA H100 and H200 GPUs — the dominant chips in frontier AI training — occurs in TSMC facilities in Taiwan using energy that, despite TSMC's renewable commitments, remains substantially fossil-intensive due to Taiwan's grid composition. A single H100 GPU contains materials — tantalum, cobalt, rare earth elements — whose supply chains extend through mining operations in the Democratic Republic of Congo and other regions with significant environmental and human rights exposure (Crawford, 2021).

### 4.3 Pre- and post-boom carbon trajectories

Luccioni and Hernandez-Garcia (2023) surveyed factors influencing ML emissions across 95 models in NLP and computer vision, examining how reported emissions evolve with model performance and proposing a centralised emissions-tracking repository to address the fragmentation in current reporting practice [OA cit: 26]. Jegham et al. (2025) extended this analysis to LLM inference specifically, benchmarking 30 commercially deployed models and finding substantial variance in energy and carbon intensity across architectures and deployment configurations — with the most energy-intensive models exceeding 29 Wh per long prompt, more than 65× the most efficient systems benchmarked. Their methodology combined public API performance data with company-specific environmental multipliers and Data Envelopment Analysis to rank deployments by efficiency.

The contrast with pre-boom projections is stark. In 2020, the IEA projected data centre emissions would stabilise or decline through 2030 as renewable energy penetration grew. By 2026, the combination of accelerated infrastructure expansion, hardware replacement cycles driven by AI chip generations, and inference-at-scale energy demand had reversed that trajectory in absolute terms, even as carbon intensity per unit of compute continued to fall.

## §5 — Authoritative Projections and Governance Responses

### 5.1 Institutional projections

The IEA (2025) *Energy and AI* report represents the most authoritative synthesis of current data and projections. Its central (Base Case) scenario projects total data centre electricity consumption rising from approximately 415 TWh in 2024 to ~945 TWh by 2030 — a 530 TWh increase equivalent to current Japanese national demand. AI-optimised data centres are projected to grow much faster than overall data centre consumption, with AI's share of data centre power use rising from 5–15% currently to 35–50% by 2030 (IEA, 2025). For purposes of long-range planning, the variance around the central case is driven primarily by inference efficiency trajectories and deployment rates; representative scenarios include:

- **Efficiency-led**: improvements in inference efficiency (quantisation, mixture-of-experts, hardware utilisation) constrain energy growth to 300–400 TWh by 2030
- **Business-as-usual**: current growth rates continue, reaching 700–800 TWh by 2030
- **Acceleration**: AI deployment grows faster than efficiency improvements, reaching 900–1,100 TWh by 2030

The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's *2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report* (Shehabi et al., 2024) — produced under the Energy Act of 2020 as the congressionally mandated update to the 2016 LBNL report — provides the most granular US-specific analysis, distinguishing AI workloads from general cloud and enterprise computing and tracking efficiency trends at the facility level. The report estimates historical US data centre electricity consumption back to 2014 and projects scenario ranges of demand to 2028.

### 5.2 Governance gap

Corporate voluntary disclosure is the dominant accountability mechanism for AI infrastructure's environmental impact in 2026. The EU AI Act and the EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) impose disclosure obligations, but neither specifies methodology for AI-specific energy or water reporting — leaving hyperscalers free to define scope, boundary, and intensity metrics in ways that minimise apparent impact.

Hacker (2024) argues that sustainable AI regulation requires mandatory lifecycle energy disclosure, carbon labelling for AI products analogous to energy labelling for appliances, and supply-chain due diligence obligations extending to hardware manufacturing. As of 2026, no major jurisdiction has implemented these requirements. The gap between the pace of AI infrastructure expansion and the pace of regulatory response represents a governance failure with cumulative environmental consequences.

## §6 — Reducing the Footprint: Strategies for Users and Organisations

The preceding sections establish that AI infrastructure carries a substantial and growing environmental cost. This section addresses what individual users and deploying organisations can do to reduce that cost — and where the structural limits of individual action lie.

The starting point for any honest treatment is a proportionality caveat. Elsworth et al. (2025) measured the full-stack production footprint of Google's AI services and found that per-query carbon intensity fell 44× in a single year — driven overwhelmingly by architectural improvements and renewable procurement decisions made at the infrastructure level, not by user behaviour. Individual query decisions are small compared to the systemic factors. Nevertheless, user and organisational choices aggregate; they constitute a meaningful signal to providers; and they determine whether individual organisations and institutions can credibly account for their AI-related environmental impact. Schwartz et al. (2020) — whose "Green AI" manifesto in *Communications of the ACM* is the most-cited call for efficiency alongside performance in AI research — argue that reporting efficiency metrics is a prerequisite for improving them, and that the same logic applies at every level from researcher to deployer to end user [J] [OA cit: 1168].

### 6.1 Right-sizing the model to the task

The single highest-impact user-controllable variable is model selection. Jegham et al. (2025) benchmarked 30 commercially deployed LLMs and found a 65× range in energy consumption between the most and least efficient systems for equivalent tasks. The most energy-intensive models in their benchmark consumed over 29 Wh per long prompt; the most efficient consumed under 0.5 Wh. For the majority of business and research tasks, a smaller model produces output that is functionally indistinguishable from a frontier model while consuming a fraction of the energy.

The practical principle is task-model matching:

- **Simple classification, extraction, and short summarisation** are well-served by small models (GPT-4o-mini, Claude Haiku, Gemini Flash, Mistral-7B served locally). These tasks rarely benefit from the scale that distinguishes GPT-4o, Claude Opus, or Gemini Ultra.
- **Repeated, standardised workloads** — document ingestion pipelines, code linting, entity extraction at scale — are candidates for fine-tuned small models or even classical ML methods, which may be orders of magnitude more efficient than a general frontier model for a narrow task.
- **Local inference** eliminates the network transfer and data centre overhead entirely for workloads where the required capability is available on consumer hardware. Tools such as Ollama and LM Studio make local inference with 7–13B parameter models practical on standard professional laptops. The environmental case for local inference is strongest for repetitive, offline, or privacy-sensitive tasks.
- **Prompt efficiency**: five iterative refinement rounds produce the same environmental cost as five separate queries. Investing time in a well-specified, unambiguous prompt is both a quality and an environmental choice.

Organisations can embed task-model matching into procurement and workflow design — defining which capability tier is appropriate for each use category and configuring default model choices accordingly, rather than defaulting to the most capable (and most energy-intensive) available model for every task.

### 6.2 Carbon-aware computing: when and where to run

Dodge et al. (2022), measuring software carbon intensity across sixteen Microsoft Azure regions, found that the same BERT training job (8 V100s, 36 hours) produced approximately 7,000 g CO2e in the most efficient region versus 26,000 g CO2e in the least efficient — a ~3.7× difference within a single hyperscaler's footprint — while intra-region diurnal variation reached up to ~8% on the same machine (FAccT 2022) [C] [OA cit: 186]. The cross-region range visible across the global grid is substantially wider than Dodge et al.'s Azure subset — §2.3 documents an order-of-magnitude gap between Nordic/Pacific Northwest hydroelectric grids and coal-heavy Asian or US Midwestern grids — but the Dodge experiment is the cleanest controlled evidence that *geographic and temporal routing of AI workloads* is a meaningful lever, even when the choice set is constrained to one provider.

**Temporal shifting** is available to any workload that is not latency-sensitive. Batch inference, training runs, large-scale embeddings, and document-processing pipelines can be scheduled to run during windows of high renewable generation — typically midday (solar peak) in solar-heavy grids, or overnight in regions where hydroelectric dominates and demand is low. Microsoft has already applied this principle at OS scale: its carbon-aware Windows Update feature shifts background downloads and installations to low-carbon grid windows, using WattTime's carbon intensity data. The same logic applies to organisational AI workloads.

**Geographic routing** is available to multi-cloud users and to users of providers whose inference infrastructure spans multiple regions. A user in Europe querying a model can often choose between a Frankfurt (German coal mix), a Stockholm (predominantly hydropower and nuclear), or a Dublin (mostly gas but with growing wind) region for inference. The carbon difference between the cleanest and dirtiest European cloud region can exceed 5×.

Tools enabling this routing are now publicly available:
- **Electricity Maps** (electricitymaps.com) — real-time and historical grid carbon intensity at zone level, open API, covers 50+ countries.
- **WattTime** — forecast-capable carbon intensity data used by major cloud providers for their own carbon-aware features.
- **Google Cloud's Carbon Footprint** dashboard — region-level carbon intensity data per product.

Rolnick et al. (2022), in their comprehensive review of AI's potential for and against climate change, note that carbon-aware scheduling of data centre workloads could reduce operational emissions by 20–45% without any hardware changes — one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost interventions available (ACM Computing Surveys) [J] [OA cit: 193].

### 6.3 Measuring before managing: emissions accounting for AI workloads

A precondition for systematic reduction is measurement. An organisation that cannot attribute energy and carbon costs to specific AI workloads cannot prioritise reduction efforts, cannot make informed model selection decisions at scale, and cannot report credibly to stakeholders.

Several tools are available for workload-level measurement:

- **CodeCarbon** — an open-source Python library (Courty et al., 2023) that instruments ML training runs and inference calls to measure and log CO2-equivalent emissions, accounting for local grid carbon intensity, hardware power draw, and duration. It integrates with major ML frameworks and outputs standardised CSV reports suitable for inclusion in model cards and sustainability reports.
- **ML CO2 Impact calculator** (mlco2.github.io) — a web tool implementing the framework developed alongside CodeCarbon; takes training duration, hardware type, and cloud region as inputs and returns a CO2e estimate with comparators.
- **Dodge et al. (2022) cloud instance methodology** — provides a research-grade framework for attributing carbon intensity to cloud API calls, accounting for region-level grid intensity and instance-level power draw. The methodology is designed for comparison across providers and is the basis for several cloud sustainability reporting tools.
- **Provider-level reporting** — an increasing number of inference API providers publish per-request energy or carbon data. This is currently limited to a subset of providers and is often available only in aggregate sustainability reports rather than per-query; organisational advocacy for per-endpoint energy transparency is a meaningful lever.

Imperfect measurement is preferable to no measurement. The primary value of emissions accounting at the workload level is not decimal-place accuracy; it is making the cost visible, enabling ranking of workloads by footprint, and creating an institutional norm of environmental accountability for AI use.

### 6.4 Procurement decisions and organisational policy

Organisations procuring AI infrastructure or API services can embed environmental criteria into selection and contract processes in ways that aggregate user choices cannot. Schwartz et al. (2020) argue that the field of AI has optimised almost exclusively for benchmark performance, treating efficiency as a secondary concern — "Red AI" in their framing — and that rebalancing toward "Green AI" requires structural incentives, not just awareness [J].

At the organisational level, relevant practices include:

- **Vendor evaluation criteria**: include energy efficiency per task (not just benchmark scores), renewable energy commitment quality (additionality, 24/7 matching vs annual certificates), and availability of per-request carbon data in procurement scoring.
- **Asynchronous and batched inference**: real-time, low-latency inference is more energy-intensive than batched processing — serving many requests in a single forward pass is substantially more efficient per query than one-at-a-time serving. For tasks that tolerate seconds or minutes of latency (document analysis, overnight report generation, bulk embeddings), batch APIs should be the default.
- **Prompt caching**: repeated use of the same long system prompt or document context creates redundant computation each time. Prompt caching — available via major API providers — stores the processed key-value representation of repeated content, dramatically reducing per-query compute for high-repetition patterns.
- **Contractual carbon commitments**: organisations with sufficient purchasing power can negotiate contractual provisions for carbon reporting, clean-energy additionality commitments, or SLA terms tied to carbon intensity targets — analogous to existing supplier diversity and human rights due-diligence provisions.

### 6.5 The structural limits of individual and organisational action

User-side strategies are meaningful, measurable, and should be pursued. They are also structurally insufficient. The environmental cost of AI infrastructure is driven primarily by infrastructure-level decisions — data centre siting, grid sourcing, hardware replacement cycles, training run scale — that are made by a handful of hyperscalers and are largely opaque to end users.

The governance gap identified in §5.2 is the binding constraint. Without mandatory lifecycle energy disclosure, per-product carbon labelling, and supply-chain due diligence requirements, users lack the information needed to make meaningful comparisons between providers. Without these requirements, providers have no structural incentive to prioritise energy efficiency over performance in a competitive market. Hacker (2024) explicitly argues that carbon labelling for AI products — analogous to the EU energy label for appliances — combined with mandatory disclosure of training and inference energy use, would transform the information environment for both individual and organisational choices.

Individual and organisational pressure for transparency is itself a form of action: demanding carbon data from providers, asking for per-request energy reporting in API contracts, and including energy efficiency in public procurement criteria collectively constitutes a market signal. That signal is most powerful when it is coordinated through policy advocacy and procurement standards rather than exercised atomistically. The most effective user contribution to sustainable AI is, ultimately, political as much as behavioural: supporting the regulatory infrastructure that makes infrastructure-level accountability possible.
