## Overview and Chapter Position

This chapter examines what "general intelligence" means — as a psychometric concept, as a computational target, and as a contested claim about AI systems. It is positioned early in the volume because the term is used pervasively but rarely defined: researchers speak of AGI, of models "approaching human-level intelligence", of hybrid intelligence as a synthesis — all without settling what "general" means or how it would be measured.

The chapter does not argue for or against any particular AI system being generally intelligent. Instead it maps the conceptual terrain: where the concept comes from, how it has been operationalised, why those operationalisations are disputed, and what the debate implies for hybrid intelligence.

## Part 1: General Intelligence in the Psychometric Tradition

### 1.1 Spearman's g factor

Charles Spearman's "*General Intelligence,' Objectively Determined and Measured*" (1904, *American Journal of Psychology*) is the empirical foundation of psychometric intelligence research. Working with school children's grades and laboratory-administered sensory discrimination tasks, Spearman observed that performance on any cognitive task correlated positively with performance on every other cognitive task — a phenomenon he termed the *positive manifold*. He then proposed a two-factor model: each measured ability reflects a general factor common to all cognitive performance (*g*) plus a task-specific factor (*s*). The technique he developed for estimating *g* — extracting the first principal component from a matrix of inter-test correlations — became factor analysis. More than a century later, the positive manifold remains one of the most robust empirical regularities in psychology: across the dozens of test batteries, age groups, and cultures studied, the cognitive tasks that correlate negatively with each other can essentially be counted on one hand (Spearman, 1904; Carroll, 1993).

A common conflation worth heading off: *g* and IQ are not the same construct. IQ is a standardised composite score from a specific test battery (Stanford-Binet, Wechsler, etc.) calibrated to a population mean of 100 and standard deviation of 15. *g* is the latent statistical factor extracted from such batteries — it is what those batteries are designed to estimate, but the score itself is the estimate, not the construct. A test that loads heavily on *g* (e.g., Raven's Progressive Matrices) produces an IQ-like score whose validity comes from being a *g*-loaded measurement, not from being an IQ test as such. This distinction matters for AI evaluation because the question "does this LLM have *g*?" is conceptually distinct from "what would this LLM score on a Wechsler subtest?"

### 1.2 Fluid and Crystallised Intelligence

Raymond Cattell's "*Theory of fluid and crystallized intelligence: A critical experiment*" (1963, *Journal of Educational Psychology*) refined Spearman's single-factor view into a two-factor model that has proven more durable. *Fluid intelligence* (Gf) is the capacity to reason about and adapt to novel problems without reliance on prior knowledge — pattern completion in unfamiliar contexts, inductive inference from sparse data, manipulation of abstract symbols. *Crystallised intelligence* (Gc) is the accumulated knowledge a mind has built up through learning and experience — vocabulary, factual knowledge, learned procedures, cultural conventions. Horn & Cattell's (1966) follow-up — "*Refinement and test of the theory of fluid and crystallized general intelligences*" — used adult cross-sectional data to demonstrate the empirical separation: Gf and Gc develop on different lifespan trajectories, with Gf peaking in early adulthood and declining gradually thereafter while Gc continues to accumulate well into old age (Salthouse, 2009). This dissociation in trajectory is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the two factors index distinct cognitive systems rather than artefacts of the same underlying ability.

The Cattell–Horn distinction is directly load-bearing for the evaluation of large language models. LLMs are trained on internet-scale text corpora, which makes them, by construction, repositories of crystallised knowledge — they can recite definitions, recall historical dates, summarise published research, and translate between hundreds of languages. On Gc-heavy benchmarks (MMLU, TriviaQA, factual recall) frontier models routinely achieve scores comparable to expert humans (Hendrycks et al., 2021; OpenAI, 2023). Their Gf performance is far more contested: Chollet (2019) argues that benchmark performance on tasks similar to training data does not constitute fluid reasoning, since solving an in-distribution problem by interpolating from training examples is precisely what Gc accumulation does. The harder question — whether LLMs perform genuine novel-task reasoning when the task is sufficiently far from training distribution — drives the design of benchmarks such as ARC-AGI (§2.3) explicitly intended to measure Gf rather than Gc. Cross-ref Draft I for the related question of *epistemic* limits when Gc-style retrieval is mistaken for understanding.

### 1.3 The Stability and Heritability of g

One of the strongest arguments that *g* is a real psychological trait — and not merely a mathematical artefact of factor analysis — comes from behavioural genetics and longitudinal research. Plomin & Deary (2015) synthesise five robust findings from decades of twin, adoption, and genome-wide association studies. First, all measured cognitive abilities are heritable: no replicated study has found a cognitive trait with zero heritability. Second, heritability increases across the lifespan: genetic influences on *g* account for roughly 40% of variance in childhood but rise to 50–80% in adulthood (Haworth et al., 2009), a counterintuitive pattern explained by gene-environment correlation accumulating over development. Third, high heritability does not imply environmental determinism — it means that *differences* between individuals in a given environment are substantially attributable to genetic differences, leaving ample room for environmental intervention to shift absolute levels. Fourth, *g* is more heritable than narrower ability factors: the variance shared across all cognitive tests is more genetically determined than the variance specific to any one test, suggesting that the common factor is closer to a biological substrate than the specific factors are. Fifth, genes associated with intelligence also predict educational attainment and health outcomes, indicating that *g* sits within a broader genetic network rather than being isolated to test performance (Plomin & Deary, 2015).

## Part 2: Computational Definitions of General Intelligence

### 2.1 The Turing Test and Its Limits

Turing (1950) proposed the *Imitation Game* as a behavioural proxy for intelligence — not a definition, but an operational test. The test conflates language fluency with intelligence, which has made it both influential and contested.

The most influential philosophical challenge to Turing's behavioural criterion is Searle's (1980) *Chinese Room* thought experiment. Searle imagines a person in a room who follows rules for manipulating Chinese symbols without understanding Chinese: the room's outputs are indistinguishable from those of a native speaker, yet no understanding occurs inside it. The argument is that syntax — the formal manipulation of symbols — is insufficient for semantics, i.e., genuine meaning. A system that passes the Turing Test may be doing nothing more than the room operator: executing syntactic rules that produce the right outputs without the right kind of mind behind them. Searle's conclusion is that symbol manipulation is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for understanding.

A structurally distinct challenge comes from the behaviourist-critique tradition. Block (1981) introduces the *Blockhead* thought experiment: a system that passes the Turing Test not through any form of processing but by brute-force lookup — a stored enumeration of correct responses to every possible conversational input up to some finite length. Blockhead has no internal computation in any cognitively interesting sense; it merely retrieves. The counterexample demonstrates that behavioural tests radically under-determine internal architecture: the Turing Test cannot distinguish between a system that processes and a system that looks up (Block, 1981). This under-determination is directly relevant to evaluating LLMs, which approximate a vast statistical lookup over training data — making the question of whether anything cognitively significant occurs inside the system genuinely open, not answerable by behavioural evidence alone.

Harnad (1990) extends this challenge with the *symbol grounding problem*. Pure symbol systems — including formal logic and classical AI architectures — define symbols in terms of other symbols: the meaning of "cat" is explained via "mammal", "whiskers", "meows", and so on, in an unanchored web of further symbols. Harnad argues that symbols can only have intrinsic meaning if they are grounded in non-symbolic sensorimotor representations — direct perceptual contact with the objects they denote. An LLM trained exclusively on text is, in Harnad's terms, a pure symbol system: its "understanding" of "cat" is constituted entirely by statistical relations among tokens. Whether this amounts to meaning in any cognitively robust sense — rather than efficient compression of human-produced symbol sequences — is one of the foundational open questions the Turing Test cannot resolve.

### 2.2 Universal Intelligence: The Legg-Hutter Measure

Legg & Hutter (2007) proposed a formal, mathematically grounded definition: intelligence as performance across all computable environments, weighted by their complexity (Kolmogorov complexity). This is theoretically elegant but practically uncomputable — it specifies what general intelligence would mean without providing a usable test.

The uncomputability of the Legg-Hutter measure is not incidental — it is structurally derived from the AIXI model, the ideal agent that would maximise the measure. AIXI, formalised by Hutter (2005), is an agent that uses Solomonoff induction to maintain a Bayesian mixture over all computable environment models, weighted by their Kolmogorov complexity, and selects actions to maximise expected discounted reward over all possible futures. It is the theoretically optimal general reinforcement learning agent. But Solomonoff induction is itself incomputable — no physical computer can enumerate and weight all computable hypotheses in finite time — and optimising over all future action sequences requires solving the halting problem. AIXI therefore cannot be instantiated on any physical machine; it is a mathematical idealisation, not an engineering blueprint. The Legg-Hutter measure inherits this incomputability directly: measuring an agent's intelligence against it requires computing performance across all computable environments, which is exactly as tractable as running AIXI itself. The measure's value is normative rather than empirical — it defines a theoretical ceiling for general intelligence rather than a practical yardstick (Hutter, 2005; Legg & Hutter, 2007).

### 2.3 Chollet's Challenge: Intelligence as Efficient Skill Acquisition

François Chollet (2019, *On the Measure of Intelligence*) argued that benchmark performance and intelligence are not the same thing. A system that has been trained on a distribution can score well on in-distribution tests by pattern matching — this is not evidence of general intelligence. Chollet defined general intelligence as *efficient skill acquisition on novel tasks* — the ability to generalise to genuinely new situations with minimal additional data.

This distinction is directly relevant to evaluating LLMs: high scores on MMLU, MATH, or coding benchmarks may reflect training-data coverage rather than general reasoning capacity. Chollet operationalised his definition in the ARC-AGI benchmark (Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus), which requires applying a small set of core knowledge priors — objectness, basic topology, elementary integer arithmetic — to novel visual puzzles never seen during training. The benchmark is deliberately constructed so that each task is unique and unmemorisable, so that solving it cannot reduce to retrieval from training data, and so that ordinary humans without specialised training reach near-ceiling performance (97–99% on the original private set; Chollet et al., 2024).

The empirical record bears out the Gf/Gc distinction sharply. Despite a ~10,000× scaling of base LLMs between 2020 and early 2024 (Sevilla et al., 2022), the state-of-the-art on the ARC-AGI-1 private evaluation set climbed only from 20% (2020 Kaggle winner) to roughly 33–34% — substantial absolute gain but far below the ~85% target and well below estimated human performance (Chollet et al., 2024). The ARC Prize 2024 competition then produced a discontinuous jump: the top eligible submission (the ARChitects team) scored 53.5% on the private set, the MindsAI submission scored 55.5% (but was ineligible for the open-source-only prize), and a preview version of OpenAI's o3 model — using test-time adaptation rather than pure scaling — reached 76% on the semi-private evaluation at an estimated $200 per task and 88% at an estimated $20,000 per task, surpassing the nominal human baseline for the first time (Chollet et al., 2024; OpenAI, 2024). The architectural lesson, on Chollet's own reading, was that "moving beyond simply scaling the formerly prevailing deep learning paradigm" was necessary, and that test-time adaptation methods emerged as a strategy adopted by all top teams (Chollet et al., 2024). The interpretive question, however, is whether this represents progress on fluid intelligence or whether it represents progress on a different problem — solving a benchmark whose construction has become well understood through thousands of leaderboard submissions over five years.

ARC-AGI-2 (Chollet et al., 2025, arXiv:2505.11831) addresses the latter concern directly. It preserves the input-output grid format and the unmemorisability constraint, but introduces tasks specifically designed to require higher-level fluid reasoning — and it deliberately re-establishes the human-AI gap: humans recruited for first-party testing solve the benchmark comfortably while frontier models scored below 5% at release. The cycle — saturation, redesign, new saturation — is itself the data point §4.3 returns to: if every benchmark eventually saturates and the gap is then reopened by harder benchmarks, the property being tracked is not a fixed quantity called "general intelligence" but a moving target reflecting the field's evolving understanding of where current systems are weakest.

## Part 3: The AGI Debate

### 3.1 Weak vs. Strong AI

Searle's (1980) distinction between *weak AI* (systems that simulate intelligent behaviour) and *strong AI* (systems that genuinely have minds, understanding, and intentionality) frames the philosophical debate. The standard textbook framing of the discipline records that the field's productive research programme has been weak AI — building systems that perform tasks intelligently while bracketing the question of whether they genuinely have minds — with most working researchers treating the weak-AI hypothesis as a tacit methodological assumption rather than an empirical claim about the systems they build (Russell & Norvig, 2021, §27.2). The question recurs nonetheless as systems grow more capable.

### 3.2 Marcus and the Limits of Pattern Matching

Gary Marcus has consistently argued that deep learning — including LLMs — does not constitute general intelligence because it lacks compositionality, systematic generalisation, and causal reasoning (Marcus, 2020; Marcus & Davis, 2019; Lake & Baroni, 2018). His critique targets precisely the Gc/Gf distinction: current systems have abundant crystallised knowledge but brittle fluid reasoning.

Marcus (2020) *The Next Decade in AI: Four Steps Towards Robust Artificial Intelligence* sharpens the critique into a positive programme. Marcus declines to frame the target as AGI, arguing instead for *robust artificial intelligence* — intelligence that "can be counted on to apply what it knows to a wide range of problems in a systematic and reliable way, synthesising knowledge from a variety of sources such that it can reason flexibly and dynamically about the world, transferring what it learns in one context to another, in the way that we would expect of an ordinary adult" (Marcus, 2020, p. 3). The framing is deliberately modest by AGI standards but demanding by the standards of current systems, since transferable, reliable cross-domain reasoning is precisely what evaluations like ARC-AGI (§2.3) are designed to isolate. The "four steps" toward this goal are: (i) **hybrid architecture** combining neural learning with symbolic representation; (ii) **large-scale knowledge, some of which is abstract and causal**, including world knowledge that cannot plausibly be acquired by gradient descent over text alone; (iii) **reasoning** capable of supporting inferences that go beyond pattern completion; and (iv) **cognitive models** of the entities and relations the system reasons about, sufficient to support counterfactual prediction and causal intervention. The empirical anchor for compositional failure is Lake & Baroni's (2018) SCAN benchmark, which demonstrates that sequence-to-sequence neural networks generalise well when test cases share structure with training data but break down systematically when required to compose familiar primitives in unfamiliar ways — a Gf-typical demand that brittle pattern-matching architectures fail under. The Marcus position is therefore not that scaling cannot continue to improve benchmark performance, but that the specific capability the Gf concept points to — transferable systematic reasoning — is unlikely to emerge from any of the routes that scaling alone provides.

### 3.3 LeCun's Autonomous Machine Intelligence

Yann LeCun's "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence" (LeCun, 2022) proposes a systems-level architecture — world models, intrinsic cost, hierarchical planning — as the path to machine general intelligence. This is not a critique of current systems so much as a research agenda that treats them as insufficient starting points. LeCun's document is an OpenReview preprint ([P] status — not peer-reviewed in a traditional journal or conference), a distinction worth noting given the handbook's source-quality standards. The architecture's core concept — the Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) — was subsequently instantiated in peer-reviewed work: Assran et al. (2023) demonstrated that a JEPA-based self-supervised model (I-JEPA) learns more abstract, semantic representations than pixel-level generative models, providing the first empirical validation of LeCun's architectural thesis in a conference setting (CVPR 2023).

LeCun's architecture is best understood in the context of Draft V's Paradigm III — embodied, world-model-based intelligence that moves beyond Paradigm I's scaling of internet text and Paradigm II's self-play on formal structures. The world-model framework itself is not novel to LeCun's 2022 proposal; Ha & Schmidhuber (2018) established the world-model architectural pattern in reinforcement learning with their *World Models* paper (NeurIPS 2018), demonstrating that agents trained with learned latent representations of environments could master visual control tasks. Where those paradigms learn from language co-occurrence and game-theoretic feedback, LeCun's programme requires a system that builds predictive models of the physical world through interaction: the JEPA design principle — predicting representations of masked inputs rather than reconstructing raw pixels — is precisely the mechanism intended to produce such a world model without the computational overhead of generative reconstruction. Bardes et al. (2024) extend this programme to video in V-JEPA, demonstrating that self-supervised prediction in the joint-embedding space of video sequences yields temporally coherent, semantically grounded features that support downstream action-recognition and motion-understanding tasks without pixelwise reconstruction — providing peer-reviewed empirical corroboration for LeCun's architectural thesis in the video domain (Bardes et al., 2024).

## Part 4: General Intelligence in the Context of Hybrid Intelligence

### 4.1 Does the Concept Travel?

The psychometric concept of g was defined over human individual differences. Does it make sense to ask whether AI systems have g? Chollet's answer is essentially no — we need a different concept (efficient skill acquisition) (Chollet, 2019). Others (e.g. Bubeck et al. 2023 on GPT-4) argue LLMs display "sparks" of AGI. This claim has attracted substantive peer-reviewed critique: Mitchell (2021) identifies four conceptual fallacies that generate systematic overconfidence in AI capability assessments, arguing that framing benchmark performance as evidence of general intelligence conflates narrow pattern-matching with flexible transfer.

Bubeck et al. (2023) report that GPT-4 displays "sparks" of general intelligence across mathematics, coding, vision, medicine, law, psychology, and theory of mind, and frame the system as "an early (yet still incomplete) version of an artificial general intelligence (AGI) system." The paper is a Microsoft Research preprint (arXiv:2303.12712) — `[P]` status — but is among the most cited LLM evaluation papers of the era. Critical responses are extensive: Mitchell (2021) anticipates the failure mode (conflating benchmark performance with general intelligence as "fallacy 1: narrow intelligence is on a continuum with general intelligence"); Marcus (2020) provides the prior architectural critique; Schaeffer et al. (2023) demonstrate that apparent emergent abilities — the benchmark-level discontinuities on which "sparks"-style claims rest — are artefacts of nonlinear evaluation metrics rather than genuine phase transitions in model capability, removing a key evidential pillar from the "early AGI" framing.

### 4.2 Hybrid Intelligence and the Distribution of g

If g is a property of individual minds, hybrid intelligence may not need any one component to be generally intelligent. The claim may be that the *system* — human + AI in appropriate configuration — has flexible, broad problem-solving capability that neither component has alone. This reframes the question: not "is this AI generally intelligent?" but "is this HI system generally capable?"

This reframing draws on the extended-mind thesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998), which argues that cognitive processes are not bounded by the skull: when external tools are reliably integrated into an agent's epistemic routines, they become part of the cognitive system. A coupled human-AI system may satisfy this criterion if the AI component becomes a stable, trusted partner in reasoning rather than an occasional lookup tool. Hutchins (1995) provides the distributed-cognition complement: complex cognitive work — navigation, medical diagnosis, complex engineering — is routinely accomplished by systems distributed across individuals, tools, and artefacts, with no single locus of competence. On this account, *g*-equivalent flexibility may emerge at the system level even when neither the human nor the AI component possesses it individually. The canonical empirical case is AlphaFold (Jumper et al., 2021): neither the AI system alone — lacking the domain understanding to formulate the biological problem — nor the structural biologists alone — constrained by the computational intractability of ab initio folding — could have achieved near-complete coverage of the protein structure space; the breakthrough emerged from the human-AI system as a whole, with human researchers providing problem framing, training data curation, and biological validation while the AI contributed computational search capacity that no human team could replicate. See Draft R for extended treatment of the extended-mind framing, Draft G for agentic collaboration dynamics, and Draft H for the human augmentation and co-evolutionary dimension.

### 4.3 Benchmarks and Their Discontents

MMLU, BIG-Bench, and ARC-AGI-1 each were designed to be immune to benchmark saturation and each saturated faster than anticipated (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Srivastava et al., 2023; Chollet et al., 2024); Humanity's Last Exam is too new to assess — frontier models scored below 10% at January 2025 release — but is following the same construction-and-redesign trajectory (Phan et al., 2025). This benchmark inflation problem suggests that general intelligence, whatever it is, is not captured by any fixed test battery. Raji et al. (2021) provide a structural critique of this dynamic: benchmark datasets inherit the conceptual limitations and distributional biases of the communities that built them, so "solving" a benchmark is often better understood as exploiting its construction assumptions than as achieving the capability the benchmark nominally measures.

Mitchell's (2021) four-fallacies analysis sharpens this point conceptually. The first fallacy — "narrow intelligence is on a continuum with general intelligence" — is the load-bearing one for the §2.3 ARC-AGI debate: progress on a benchmark designed to test fluid reasoning does not, on its own, license inferences about general fluid capability, because the system may have learned to exploit benchmark-specific structure rather than the underlying competence the benchmark was intended to track. The §2.3 cycle of saturation-and-redesign — ARC-AGI-1 saturating under test-time adaptation, ARC-AGI-2 re-establishing the gap — is exactly the empirical signature Mitchell's fallacy 1 predicts: each generation of frontier systems closes the gap on the *current* benchmark while leaving the next, harder benchmark untouched. A counter-position, articulated by Bowman (2023), holds that progressive closure across qualitatively distinct benchmark designs — MMLU, then BIG-Bench, then ARC-AGI, then HLE — is itself evidence of genuinely broadening capability rather than benchmark-specific overfitting; the saturation-and-redesign pattern documented here is consistent with both readings, and benchmark scores must therefore be interpreted alongside the construction history that shapes what each benchmark measures. The Humanity's Last Exam record fits the same pattern: assembled from expert-contributed questions across 100+ disciplines and designed to resist saturation, frontier models scored below 10% at January 2025 release (Phan et al., 2025) — but the design of HLE is itself an artefact of where the field judged the gap to lie at that moment, and the same dynamic of measured progress meeting redesigned tests is likely to play out on it as on its predecessors. The conclusion is not that benchmarks are useless but that benchmark scores must be reported alongside the construction history that shapes what they measure — including any subsequent designer-led re-targeting in response to model progress.

## Key Open Questions

1. Is general intelligence a meaningful concept for AI systems, or does it only apply to biological agents shaped by evolutionary pressure for broad adaptability?
2. Does hybrid intelligence dissolve the question — replacing "is this agent generally intelligent?" with "is this system generally capable?"?
3. Can Chollet's definition (efficient skill acquisition on novel tasks) be used to evaluate HI systems, not just individual agents?
4. How does the Gf/Gc distinction map onto current LLMs, and what does this imply for HI system design?
