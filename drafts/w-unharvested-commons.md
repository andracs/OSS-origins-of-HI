# Draft W — The Unharvested Commons: Oral Traditions, Indigenous Epistemologies, and the Case for Non-Digital Knowledge in AI Training

## 1. The unexamined bias in the AI knowledge commons

Contemporary AI systems are trained on data. The open-source movement and decades of digital publishing have created an enormous and accessible corpus — text, code, images, structured databases — that foundation models consume at scale. But this commons has a profound and underappreciated selection bias: it overwhelmingly reflects the knowledge of *digitally connected, literate, and predominantly Western societies*.

Of the approximately 7,000 languages spoken by humans today, fewer than 100 are meaningfully represented in the training corpora of leading language models (Joshi et al., 2020 [taxonomy of NLP language inclusion; pre-LLM baseline]; Ahuja et al., 2023 [multilingual benchmark coverage — note: taxonomy and benchmark coverage are proxy measures for training-corpus inclusion; direct training-data audits of FineWeb and Dolma corpora (Penedo et al., 2024; Soldaini et al., 2024) provide more precise measurement; Blasi et al., 2022 [systematic ACL peer-reviewed analysis quantifying language technology performance inequalities across the world's languages — empirical anchor for the coverage-gap claim at global scale]). English accounts for an estimated 46% of internet content; the top ten languages together represent over 90% (W3Techs, 2024; see also Kornai, 2013). Even DeepSeek and Qwen — China's leading open-source models — are trained primarily on Chinese and English text (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024), leaving the epistemological diversity of the world's oral traditions, non-literate cultures, and indigenous knowledge systems entirely outside the training distribution.

This is not merely a technical gap. It is a structural asymmetry with deep implications for what kinds of intelligence AI systems can represent, whose worldviews they encode, and whose cognitive patterns they systematically exclude. Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010) demonstrated that behavioural science research was dominated by participants who were Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic — coining the acronym WEIRD to name this sampling bias. The same critique applies with equal or greater force to AI training data: current AI systems are, in this sense, *WEIRD machines* (Atari et al., 2023).

The bias is not corrected when training pipelines move beyond the open-knowledge commons to the much larger behavioural corpora held by commercial social platforms. As Draft AV documents, the data assets of Meta, Google/YouTube, X, LinkedIn, TikTok, and Reddit dwarf every open research dataset combined — but the populations whose interactions constitute those assets are *also* WEIRD-skewed, oversampling digitally connected, urban, smartphone-equipped users of high-income economies and middle-class enclaves within middle-income economies. The half of humanity whose knowledge is most distinctive — those whose practices remain primarily oral, primarily embodied, or primarily transmitted outside the platforms — is not present in the platform corpus either. Social platform extraction (Draft AV) is therefore a *second* layer of WEIRD-biased data, not a corrective for the first; its volume can obscure rather than redress the structural asymmetry diagnosed here.

## 2. The Bartók method: collecting untouched knowledge

The argument for deliberately collecting knowledge from non-digitalized societies is not new — it has a powerful historical precedent in the work of Béla Bartók. In the 1900s–1930s, Bartók travelled to Hungarian, Romanian, Bulgarian, Turkish, and North African villages to record folk music that had never been notated, never been influenced by Western music theory, and existed only in the embodied memory of communities living outside the literate, urban musical tradition. He brought wax cylinder recorders into remote settlements — the ethnographic fieldwork equivalent of data collection — and assembled a corpus of approximately 10,000 folk songs that represented what he called the "musical mother tongue": an intelligence encoded in melody, rhythm, and structure that Western conservatory music had long suppressed or forgotten (Bartók, 1931; Suchoff, 1976; Nettl, 2005).

Bartók's method demonstrated several things simultaneously. First, that enormous cognitive value existed in communities that produced no written record. Second, that collecting this knowledge required *going to it* — it could not be scraped from libraries. Third, that the collected knowledge, once integrated into compositional practice, produced work that was qualitatively different from what was possible within the Western tradition alone.

The parallel for AI is direct. If current AI training is analogous to Bartók reading the Western classical canon, then the unharvested oral traditions, indigenous knowledge systems, and pre-digital epistemologies of the world's non-connected communities represent a potential commons of radically different cognitive material — world models, causal beliefs, ecological knowledge, social ontologies — that no amount of additional English-language web data can substitute for.

## 3. What is at stake: cognitive diversity and the limits of WEIRD AI

The concern is not merely linguistic inclusivity, though that matters. It is epistemological. Different languages and cultural traditions do not merely translate the same underlying concepts into different words — they encode fundamentally different models of time, causation, agency, number, and social relation (Boroditsky, 2001; Everett, 2005). The Pirahã people of the Amazon, studied extensively by Everett, have no words for exact numbers, no tense in their grammar, and a cosmology grounded in immediate experience rather than abstract recursion — though Everett's specific grammatical claims (absence of recursion and tense) have been contested in linguistics; Nevins, Pesetsky, & Rodrigues (2009) argue the empirical data does not support the stronger claims about recursion absence. The broader point that the Pirahã represent a radically different cognitive and cosmological orientation remains illustrative of the chapter's argument regardless of these unresolved technical disputes. Whorfian arguments — once dismissed — have gained substantial empirical support: language shapes habitual thought in measurable ways (Boroditsky, 2001; Regier & Kay, 2009).

An AI trained exclusively on WEIRD-language corpora will have deeply internalized WEIRD cognitive patterns: linear time, individual agency, abstract propositional reasoning, market-mediated social ontologies. It will perform poorly — or perform in subtly misleading ways — when deployed in cultural contexts that do not share these assumptions. More fundamentally, it will be incapable of representing alternative epistemologies that might be not merely different but in some domains *better adapted* — indigenous ecological knowledge, for example, encodes centuries of empirical observation about local ecosystems in forms that Western science is only beginning to recognize as rigorous (Berkes, 2008). The Whorfian transmission mechanism — how language-shaped cognitive defaults end up inside trained models — is examined in detail in Draft J (language as substrate), which documents empirical evidence that LLMs exhibit *typologically aligned* attention patterns that mirror the causal-ordering preferences of their training languages (Wang et al., 2025); the WEIRD commons gap is therefore not merely a coverage deficit but a structural bias propagated through the linguistic substrate of the training corpus itself.

Bender et al. (2021) document how large language models produce "stochastic parrots": systems that manipulate linguistic form without grounded meaning, with a particular risk that the dominant cultural forms of the training data are reproduced and amplified as defaults. The underrepresentation of non-WEIRD knowledge is not simply a gap — it is an active distortion, because what is absent from training data becomes literally unthinkable for the trained system.

## 4. Arguments for deliberate collection of non-digital knowledge

**The epistemic diversity argument:** A plurality of world models makes AI systems more robust, less prone to systematic cultural blind spots, and more capable of genuine cross-cultural reasoning. The insight from ensemble methods in machine learning — that diversity among weak learners produces stronger aggregate predictions — applies at the epistemological level: cognitive diversity in training data should produce more generalisable intelligence (Hong & Page, 2004; Page, 2007).

**The scientific discovery argument:** Non-Western and indigenous knowledge systems have repeatedly proven scientifically valuable when they have been taken seriously. Ethnobotany, traditional ecological knowledge, and indigenous astronomical observations have yielded discoveries that eluded Western science (Fabricant & Farnsworth, 2001; Berkes, 2008; Tengö et al., 2014; Ruggles, 2015). An AI system trained on these knowledge bases could cross-reference them with formal scientific literature in ways that no individual researcher could manage, potentially accelerating discovery in ecology, medicine, materials science, and climate adaptation.

**The cultural preservation argument:** Of the world's ~7,000 languages, approximately half are expected to disappear before the end of this century (UNESCO, 2003; Bromham et al., 2022). Each language death represents an irreversible loss of cognitive tools, narrative traditions, and embodied knowledge. Deliberate collection — on the model of Bartók, the Endangered Archives Programme, and the ELDP (Endangered Languages Documentation Programme) — creates a permanent record that can seed AI systems with knowledge that would otherwise be lost. The AI training argument adds urgency and practical motivation to what has historically been an underfunded cultural preservation effort.

**The AI alignment argument:** A value-aligned AI cannot be value-aligned to *all* of humanity if it has only ever encountered the values of a WEIRD minority (Cao et al., 2023). Genuine alignment with human flourishing requires exposure to the full range of human moral intuitions, social arrangements, and conceptions of the good life — not merely those that happen to be overrepresented in digitized text.

## 5. Methods: how non-digital knowledge could be collected

The methods exist; the motivation and coordination have been lacking. Several approaches are technically and ethically feasible:

**Oral history recording:** Structured fieldwork using audio and video recording, following the methods developed by oral history movements and linguistic fieldwork traditions. The PARADISEC (Pacific and Regional Archive for Digital Sources in Endangered Cultures) project has grown to over 150 terabytes of audio and video material representing 1,312 languages from Papua New Guinea and the Pacific (Thieberger & Harris, 2022). Mozilla's Common Voice project has collected speech in over 100 languages through community contribution (Ardila et al., 2020; Mozilla Foundation, 2024). These are scalable models.

**Community-controlled transcription pipelines:** Indigenous communities retain sovereignty over their knowledge; any collection must be governed by consent and benefit-sharing. Automatic speech recognition, even imperfect, can dramatically accelerate transcription of oral recordings into text (Pratap et al., 2024). Community members serve as validators, correctors, and curators — creating a model of participatory AI development rather than extractive data harvesting.

**Participatory knowledge mapping:** Ethnographic methods adapted for AI training — structured interviews, story-telling sessions, and knowledge mapping exercises designed to elicit tacit and embodied knowledge from community members. The Aarhus University tradition of participatory design is directly applicable here (Kyng, 1991; Greenbaum & Kyng, 1991).

**Integration of existing endangered language corpora:** AIATSIS (Australian Institute of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Studies) maintains one of the world's largest collections of indigenous cultural materials (AIATSIS, 2024). First Languages Australia and equivalent bodies in other regions have assembled significant corpora that are not currently represented in AI training pipelines.

## 6. The sovereignty problem: against data colonialism

The strongest objection to this proposal is ethical: it risks reproducing the extractive logic of colonialism at the level of knowledge. Couldry and Mejias (2019) coin the term *data colonialism* to describe the appropriation of people's data by corporate platforms without meaningful consent or benefit-sharing. Applying this critique to indigenous knowledge collection: the history of ethnographic fieldwork is replete with cases where communities had their knowledge recorded, published, commodified, and benefited others — without compensation, consent, or ongoing control.

The answer is not to abandon collection but to govern it differently. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics — provide a framework developed *by* indigenous data governance bodies (Carroll et al., 2020). Applied to AI training, they require: that collected knowledge benefits the source community; that the community retains authority over how its knowledge is used, adapted, and attributed; that collectors take responsibility for harm prevention; and that the entire process is conducted with respect for indigenous rights and self-determination.

Community-controlled AI development projects — such as the Māori language technology initiatives in New Zealand, or the indigenous AI frameworks developed by groups like the Indigenous Protocol and Artificial Intelligence Working Group (Lewis [Ed.], 2020) — demonstrate that this is not merely aspirational. Meaningful governance of AI training at the community level is technically feasible and ethically necessary.

## 7. Implications for hybrid intelligence

The Bartók argument for AI training data has a direct implication for hybrid intelligence design. If AI systems are to genuinely augment human intelligence — rather than merely amplifying the cognitive patterns of the WEIRD fraction of humanity — then the human knowledge they draw on must be genuinely diverse. Hybrid intelligence systems deployed in non-Western, rural, or indigenous contexts will fail not because their AI components are technically deficient but because those components have never encountered the cognitive world of the people they are meant to work with.

The feedback loop described in this chapter's core argument (see Draft A) — open source code → AI training → capable models → human-AI collaboration → new high-quality data — currently operates almost entirely within WEIRD contexts. Extending the loop to include non-digital knowledge would require a different kind of collection infrastructure, different governance models, and a fundamentally different understanding of what "data" can be. But it would also produce AI systems capable of a qualitatively broader form of hybrid intelligence — one that draws on the full range of human cognitive diversity rather than a narrow, historically contingent slice of it.

Draft BF (§7, *after authorship*) develops a parallel framing under the heading of *corpus biodiversity* — adapting ecological biodiversity metrics (Shannon entropy, species richness, keystone species, genetic bottlenecks) to characterise the diversity of training corpora across epistemic tradition, linguistic register, and argumentative style. The unharvested commons described in this draft is precisely what BF §7 calls a *keystone* category: a body of cognitive material whose absence has disproportionate effects on the downstream resilience of the model ecosystem. The two drafts converge on the same governance question from opposite directions: BF approaches it from the *downstream* risk that AI-generated text progressively homogenises the corpus (a self-inflicted bottleneck); this draft approaches it from the *upstream* gap that even the human-authored corpus underrepresents most of the human cognitive distribution to begin with. A corpus-biodiversity metric of the kind BF §7 proposes would, if computed, register the unharvested commons as one of its largest empirical deficits.

## Open questions

- **OQ1 — Scale of the gap:** What proportion of human cognitive diversity is actually captured in current LLM training corpora? Are there empirical estimates of the language/culture coverage gap?
- **OQ2 — Bartók analogy limits:** Bartók's collection was still mediated by his Western categories of transcription. Does AI training on collected oral data face the same translation problem — that the act of digitization imposes WEIRD structure on non-WEIRD content?
- **OQ3 — Governance precedents:** What are the most successful existing models of community-controlled knowledge collection that could serve as templates for AI training data governance?
- **OQ4 — Commercial incentives:** Who has the incentive to fund the collection of non-digital knowledge for AI training? Is this a public goods problem requiring state or foundation funding?
- **OQ5 — Feedback loop:** If an AI system were trained on non-WEIRD knowledge, would it produce outputs that the source communities find genuinely useful — or would the architecture itself impose WEIRD cognitive structure regardless of training data?
