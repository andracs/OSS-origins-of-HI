## Overview and Chapter Position

This draft addresses a dimension of the chapter's three-stage model that the other drafts treat as a boundary condition rather than a subject: the feedback loop does not only run from human cognition → open source commons → AI training → augmented tools → back to human cognition. It also runs *into* human biology and culture over longer timescales. The question is how AI augmentation tools — tools that sharpen perception, extend memory, accelerate problem-solving, or scaffold reasoning — change the humans who use them, and what those changes imply for the humans who don't.

Three timescales require distinct analytical frameworks:
- **Individual (years–decades)**: cognitive adaptation, skill atrophy, extended mind, neuroplasticity
- **Population (decades)**: differential access and stratification; divergence between augmented and non-augmented populations
- **Generational (centuries–millennia)**: niche construction, relaxed selection, gene-culture coevolution

The three-stage model in the chapter (Accumulation → Mining & refinement → Augmentation & feedback) captures the first timescale. The second and third require evolutionary and cultural-evolutionary frameworks that current hybrid intelligence literature has not yet integrated.

## Part 1: The Extended Mind — Individual Cognitive Augmentation

**Anchor:** Clark & Chalmers (1998); Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011)

**The Extended Mind thesis** (Clark & Chalmers, 1998, *Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19): cognitive processes are not bounded by the skull. When a tool reliably performs a cognitive function — storing information, performing calculation, tracking relationships — and the human reliably delegates that function to the tool, the tool becomes part of the cognitive system. The canonical example: a notebook used by an Alzheimer's patient to remember appointments is functionally equivalent to biological memory; denying it the status of "cognition" is an arbitrary boundary. Applied to AI tools: a coding agent that reliably searches the API documentation, a language model that reliably generates first-draft text, or a retrieval system that reliably surfaces relevant prior work are, on the Clark/Chalmers view, extensions of the programmer's cognitive apparatus rather than mere instruments.

**The offloading effect** (Sparrow, Liu & Wegner, 2011, *Science*, 333(6043), 776–778): "Google Effects on Memory." Subjects told that information would be saved to computer were less likely to remember the information itself — but better at remembering *where* to find it. The brain encodes retrieval paths rather than content when an external store is available. This is adaptive at the individual level (why memorize what you can look up?) but raises a question about what happens to the cognitive infrastructure that was previously used to hold that content: does it atrophy, or does it redeploy to other tasks?

**Neuroplasticity as mechanism**: Maguire et al. (2000, *PNAS* 97(8), 4398–4403) demonstrated that London taxi drivers develop measurably larger hippocampal posterior regions from years of spatial navigation — a direct structural consequence of cognitive demand. If AI tools systematically reduce demand on certain cognitive capacities, the same mechanism operates in reverse.

**Empirical evidence on AI-assisted cognitive change**: Bastani et al. (2025) provide a direct empirical test in a high-school mathematics context: in a field experiment with nearly a thousand 9th–11th-grade Turkish students, the group given unrestricted GPT-4 access ("GPT Base") during practice scored 17% *lower* on subsequent unassisted exams than controls who never had AI access; a parallel arm given a guardrailed tutor ("GPT Tutor") with prompts designed to provide hints rather than answers showed the negative learning effect substantially mitigated (Bastani et al., 2025, *PNAS*, 122(26), e2422633122). The 48% (Base) and 127% (Tutor) in-session improvements during AI-assisted practice did not transfer to unassisted performance, suggesting that AI assistance reduces skill consolidation rather than substituting for it only during the assisted phase — the brain encodes retrieval paths to the AI tool rather than encoding the problem-solving procedure itself, so tool-dependent competence does not persist when the tool is withdrawn (Storm & Stone, 2015). The Bastani et al. result also localises the atrophy mechanism: it is not AI use *per se* but unscaffolded delegation that produces the deficit. This is the offloading-without-redeployment failure mode: cognitive resources freed by the tool are not redirected to higher-order reasoning but simply go unexercised. The Becker et al. (2025) finding — experienced developers 19% slower with AI tools on mature codebases — may reflect a comparable atrophy signal at task level in a professional context.

## Part 2: The Glasses Thought Experiment — Niche Construction and Relaxed Selection

**Anchor:** Odling-Smee, Laland & Feldman (2003); Laland, Odling-Smee & Myles (2010)

**The glasses analogy**: Corrective lenses have been in widespread use since the 13th century (Ilardi, 2007). Across the centuries since, selection pressure against myopia has relaxed in literate populations: individuals with poor uncorrected vision survive, reproduce, and contribute genes at rates approaching the non-myopic population — reflecting both the high heritability of refractive error (~86%; Hammond et al., 2001) and the progressive relaxation of selection against myopia as optical correction became the norm (Wojciechowski, 2011). The result — gradual upward drift in myopia prevalence — is not a failure of glasses but a predictable consequence of niche construction: humans modify their environment (here: the visual environment) in ways that change the selection pressures acting on them.

**Niche construction theory** (Odling-Smee, Laland & Feldman, 2003, *Niche Construction: The Neglected Process in Evolution*, Princeton UP): organisms actively modify their selective environments, feeding back to change the evolutionary pressures on themselves and their descendants. This is not Lamarckian inheritance (the glasses do not change the genome); it is selection relaxation and redirection. The construction of fire, clothing, cooking, and agriculture are the canonical human cases — each one a tool that relaxed selection on a prior physiological constraint.

**Applied to AI augmentation**: A cognitive tool that reliably compensates for limitations in working memory, pattern recognition, or information retrieval relaxes selection pressure on the underlying biological capacity. Over sufficient generations, the population's distribution of natural (unaided) cognitive capability would shift — not because AI changes genes directly, but because it changes which cognitive profiles are reproductively neutral versus disadvantaged. The precise analog: just as glasses make good uncorrected vision less necessary for survival and reproduction in literate societies, a reliable AI reasoning scaffold makes good unaided reasoning less necessary — at least in the specific task domains the tool covers.

**The myopia prevalence data**: The glasses case itself is empirically instructive and measurable. Myopia prevalence in the United States increased from approximately 25% (1971–1972) to 42% (1999–2004) (Vitale, Sperduto & Ferris, 2009), with the acceleration correlating to increased screen-based work and near-vision demand (Holden et al., 2016, *Ophthalmology*, 123(5), 1036–1042). [J] [OA cit: 5278]. Critically, this is not genetic drift under selection pressure for myopia genes per se, but rather a relaxation of selection *against* myopia once vision correction became reliable and socially expected. The population now tolerated myopic genotypes that would have faced reproductive disadvantage in pre-spectacles or non-literate contexts. The mechanism is niche construction: literate societies created a visual niche (small text, indoor work, near-field focus) that relaxed selection against myopia. The shift over 40 years (25% → 42%) is a snapshot of how quickly a population's phenotypic distribution can change when selection pressure relaxes. AI augmentation would operate via the same logic, but across cognitive rather than sensory domains — and possibly on shorter timescales, because individual cognitive adaptation happens within a lifetime, whereas genetic distribution shifts across generations.

**Gene-culture coevolution and cognitive timescales** (Laland, Odling-Smee & Myles, 2010, *Nature Reviews Genetics*, 11(2), 137–148): cultural innovations alter selection pressures on genes; the classic case is lactase persistence following the domestication of cattle, which spread across Eurasian populations in roughly 7,000–10,000 years (Bersaglieri et al., 2004; Itan et al., 2009). The co-evolution operates on a timescale of hundreds to thousands of years. But the *structure* of the effect is predictable from the existing framework. The key insight from Laland et al. is that culture and genes are not separable evolutionary processes; cultural practices create the selective environment that then shapes which genetic variants increase in frequency. Applied to AI: if a population's children grow up in an environment where certain cognitive capacities are systematically offloaded to machines, the selection pressures on the biological capacity to perform those functions without aid would relax. This does not require genetic change; it only requires reproductive success to become decoupled from unaided cognitive performance in that domain. On the myopia analogy, the relevant timescale is not genomic evolution but *cultural embedding*: within a single generation, a tool can shift from rare to ubiquitous, changing which competencies are required for economic or educational success. The phenotypic distribution of human cognitive traits could shift within decades if the selection environment changes fast enough — not through genome change, but through differential reproductive success and cultural transmission of adapted cognitive strategies.

**What is actually selected across cognitive domains?** The mechanism differs subtly from vision. Myopia is a well-defined physiological trait with a clear genetic basis and a simple relationship to the corrective tool (glasses correct the optical error directly). Cognition is more distributed. When an AI tool handles working memory load, the selected-against trait is not "capacity to hold 7 items in mind" but the *developmental outcome* of engaging in memory-intensive work: metacognitive awareness of what one forgets, strategies to compensate, and the ability to work across longer temporal horizons. These are downstream consequences of exercising the underlying capacity, not the capacity itself. If AI offloads this exercise, the population may shift not in raw working memory *capacity* (which is a stable individual-difference construct closely related to fluid intelligence; Engle et al., 1999) but in the *repertoire of cognitive strategies* that require sustained practice to acquire. Educational and professional selection would then shift to reward whichever traits are *reinforced* by AI-augmented workflows — likely judgment, goal-setting, and interpersonal coordination — at the expense of traits that are offloaded.

## Part 3: Population-Level Effects — Access, Stratification, and the Cognitive Divide

**Anchor:** Daniotti et al. (2025); Hargittai (2002); van Dijk (2020)

**Differential augmentation**: The tools discussed in this chapter — frontier coding agents, large language model assistants, AI-enhanced scientific search — are not uniformly accessible. Cost, language coverage, hardware requirements, and the tacit knowledge required to use them effectively all create differential distributions of augmentation across populations. Daniotti et al. (2025) find that approximately 29% of Python functions in US-authored commits were AI-generated by end-2024, with 23–24% in Germany and France, ~20% in India (after rapid catch-up from 2023 onward), and substantially lower shares in China and Russia, where GitHub is partly displaced by Gitee and adoption is slower — a snapshot of differential augmentation at the developer-population level as of end-2024.

**The second digital divide** (Hargittai, 2002; van Dijk, 2020): the first digital divide was access to hardware and connectivity; the second is the skill divide — differential ability to *use* digital tools effectively. AI augmentation tools introduce a third layer: the *cognitive complement divide* (a synthesis term introduced here, extending van Dijk's (2020) third-level access-divide framework to the specific dynamic of AI-tool amplification by prior expertise — see van Dijk, 2020, Chapter 4). AI tools amplify the capabilities of users who already have strong domain knowledge, metacognition, and prompt literacy (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023). They provide smaller returns to users with weaker foundations (as the Becker et al., 2025, experienced-developer-skew finding suggests at the individual level). Population-level, this implies that AI augmentation may *widen* capability gaps between populations rather than narrow them, at least in the medium term.

**The compounding mechanism**: augmented users produce higher-quality outputs → these outputs enter training data → future AI models are disproportionately shaped by augmented-user cognition (Shumailov et al., 2024) → tools become better calibrated to augmented-user styles and needs → non-augmented users face tools increasingly misaligned with their cognitive profiles. This is the population-level analog of the feedback loop the chapter describes for the open source commons. The effect is reinforced by data composition: Daniotti et al. (2025) document that frontier markets (US, Western Europe) have higher AI-commit ratios, meaning their code-generation patterns are over-represented in public training corpora. Models trained on this skewed data may be better calibrated to workflows common in high-tool-access regions — a hypothesis consistent with Cassano et al.'s (2023) MultiPL-E benchmark, which documents systematic pass-rate gaps between high-resource programming languages (Python, JavaScript) and low-resource ones (Cassano et al., 2023). Lozhkov et al. (2024) attribute such gaps specifically to training-data composition, demonstrating in the StarCoder 2 / The Stack v2 analysis that a programming language's share of the pre-training corpus correlates with downstream model performance on that language — supporting the inference that the MultiPL-E differentials reflect data underrepresentation rather than irreducible model-capability limits (Lozhkov et al., 2024). Whether this training-data explanation extends to regional workflow-calibration misalignment remains an open empirical question, not directly established by either source. For developers in regions with lower initial tool access, these models may be less aligned with local development contexts, programming language distributions, or domain conventions. This creates a positive feedback: high-access populations get progressively better-fitted tools, which generate even higher-quality training data, which further optimizes the next generation of models for those populations.

The Shen & Tamkin (2026) finding on AI-assisted learning illustrates the consequence at the individual level. Developers who learn new technical skills *without* AI assistance show better conceptual understanding and debugging ability than those assisted by AI — but the gap is not uniform. Shen & Tamkin report heterogeneity by cognitive engagement pattern rather than by prior-knowledge level directly; three of six AI interaction patterns they identify preserve learning outcomes by keeping the learner cognitively engaged. The interpretation that learners with stronger prior foundations can selectively engage without conceptual loss while those with weaker foundations cannot is consistent with the redeployment hypothesis, but a direct prior-knowledge moderator test remains outstanding. Over a generation, this differential could shape the distribution of domain expertise: without intentional intervention, AI tools may accelerate the expertise divide rather than democratize it, because the populations most capable of deriving maximal benefit from the tools are those who already have the highest baseline capability.

Governance implications follow: if augmentation tools consolidate expertise in already-advantaged populations, and those populations' outputs then shape training data for the next generation of tools, we face a dynamic where initial inequalities become stable over successive iterations. This is distinct from the one-time wealth concentration of the digital divide; it is a *structural* inequality that can self-perpetuate through the training loop.

## Part 4: The Cognitive Niche — Cultural Evolution and What Gets Transmitted

**Anchor:** Henrich (2016); Tomasello (1999); Boyd, Richerson & Henrich (2011)

**Cumulative cultural evolution** (Henrich, 2016; Boyd, Richerson & Henrich, 2011): human cognition evolved specifically to acquire, store, and transmit culturally learned knowledge and skills. The human brain is not a general-purpose problem solver; it is a cultural learning device, shaped by selection to absorb the accumulated know-how of prior generations. The "secret of our success" is not individual intelligence but the ability to build on what others learned — the ratchet effect of cumulative culture.

**What AI changes in this transmission**: human cultural transmission has historically been constrained by what can be articulated and demonstrated. Tacit knowledge (Polanyi, 1966 — see Draft G, Open Question #7) is the hardest to transmit; it requires apprenticeship, extended observation, and embodied practice. AI tools that *externalize* tacit knowledge (Collins, 2010) — expert systems that encode diagnostic reasoning, coding agents that embody best practices — change the transmission channel. What required a human expert to transmit can now be partially encoded in a tool. Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) demonstrate this empirically: an AI assistant trained on patterns from top-performing customer-service agents improved novice worker productivity by 15% on average, with the largest gains concentrated among the lowest-skill workers — those most lacking access to the tacit expertise the tool had encoded. This accelerates the cultural ratchet but also changes what humans need to hold internally.

**Tomasello's shared intentionality** (Tomasello, 1999, *The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition*, Harvard UP): the foundation of cumulative culture is joint attention and the ability to understand others as intentional agents with goals. This capacity is distinctively human and underlies collaborative learning. If AI agents increasingly participate in the collaborative knowledge-building that was previously human-to-human, the shared intentionality infrastructure may be partially bypassed. The downstream cultural evolutionary consequence is not yet fully understood, but early empirical work suggests AI systems replicate and may amplify human-like content biases in transmission chains (Acerbi & Stubbersfield, 2023) and that AI participation in cultural production is already reshaping the character of machine-mediated culture (Brinkmann et al., 2023). The fraud vector compounds this: a systematic test of 13 major LLMs found all of them capable of generating fabricated academic papers on request (Gibney, 2026), a capacity confirmed in peer-reviewed research showing AI readily produces structurally plausible but fabricated medical manuscripts (Majovsky et al., 2023), meaning AI tools can introduce junk science into the scholarly record at scale — degrading the quality of the corpus that future models will be trained on and directly threatening the integrity of the cumulative cultural ratchet.

## Part 5: Skill Atrophy vs. Capability Extension — The Contested Empirical Question

**Anchor:** Carr (2010); Becker et al. (2025); Maguire et al. (2000)

The empirical question at the center of this draft: does AI augmentation expand human capability, or does it replace it — leaving the underlying capacity to atrophy? The two positions:

**The redeployment hypothesis** (Risko & Gilbert, 2016): offloading routine cognitive tasks to AI frees attention and working memory for higher-order work — judgment, creativity, value specification, interpersonal coordination. The tool handles the substrate; the human handles the architecture. On this view, AI augmentation is analogous to writing (which offloaded memory but freed cognition for more complex reasoning) or calculation tools (which offloaded arithmetic but freed mathematicians for structural thinking).

**The atrophy hypothesis**: the cognitive capacity to perform a task is maintained only through practice. The strongest neuroimaging evidence comes from the longitudinal taxi-driver study (Woollett & Maguire, 2011, *Current Biology*, 21(24), 2109–2114): trainee taxi drivers who acquired "the Knowledge" of London's streets over four years showed selective increases in posterior hippocampal grey matter; those who failed to qualify showed no structural change. This establishes that sustained cognitive demand drives structural brain change in adults — and implies its absence relaxes that demand. Javadi et al. (2017, *Nature Communications*, 8, 14652) showed that hippocampal activity during navigation specifically indexes topological network complexity encountered in real London streets, suggesting the hippocampus is doing computationally active work that GPS substitution would bypass. Delegating text composition to LLMs may analogously reduce the cognitive processes associated with structuring argument and finding precise language (Kosmyna et al., 2025; Tankelevitch et al., 2024). Carr (2010, *The Shallows*, Norton) argues that internet use has already begun to reshape attentional architecture — not by reducing intelligence, but by shifting cognitive style toward shallow, hyperlinked processing at the expense of deep linear reading. The peer-reviewed synthesis is Firth et al. (2019), who document across multiple experimental paradigms that internet use is associated with altered sustained attention, accelerated but shallower memory encoding, and shifts in social cognition — consistent with Carr's attentional-architecture claim but grounded in controlled cognitive-science evidence rather than cultural criticism.

The Becker et al. (2025) finding is the most direct current evidence from the AI domain: experienced open-source developers were 19% *slower* with frontier AI coding tools on mature-codebase tasks. This is consistent with either: (a) a workflow friction cost that will diminish with practice, or (b) a signal that the tool is substituting for cognitive processes that, when expropriated, degrade overall performance. Distinguishing these requires longitudinal data not yet available.

More direct evidence for the atrophy side comes from Shen & Tamkin (2026, "How AI Impacts Skill Formation," arXiv:2601.20245), who ran randomized experiments in which developers learned a new asynchronous programming library either with or without AI assistance. The key finding: AI-assisted learners showed impaired conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging abilities compared to unassisted learners — without significant efficiency gains on average. The mechanism is consistent with the atrophy hypothesis: when AI handles execution, the cognitive work of encoding the library's structure is bypassed, and the skill does not form. This is the closest available experimental analog to the GPS/hippocampus story: just as GPS users who never engage topological reasoning do not develop the hippocampal representations that support independent navigation (Dahmani & Bohbot, 2020), developers who never engage debugging do not develop the mental models that support independent diagnosis. Shen & Tamkin identify three interaction patterns involving cognitive engagement that *preserve* learning outcomes despite AI assistance — suggesting the relevant variable is not AI use per se but whether the cognitive process is engaged or delegated.

A distinct but related cognitive cost — not atrophy but *acute overload* — emerges in multi-agent configurations where execution speed exceeds the human supervisor's comprehension capacity. Bedard, Kropp, Hsu et al. (2026, HBR) describe this as "brain fry": a form of cognitive fatigue arising when agentic systems operate faster than the human can form a coherent model of what is happening. This is separable from the atrophy question (which concerns what happens over time when cognitive demand falls) but belongs to the same family of costs: both represent ways in which AI augmentation, rather than simply extending human capability, changes the cognitive demands placed on the human in the system — sometimes reducing them below what maintains competence, and sometimes exceeding what can be sustained.

## Open Questions

1. **Is the glasses analogy apt?** Glasses correct a sensory deficit without altering cognitive architecture. AI tools alter how cognition is *organized* — which tasks are done internally vs. externally, which skills are practiced vs. delegated. Does the niche-construction framework apply equally to cognitive architecture as to sensory acuity, or does the complexity of cognitive systems make the analogy break down?

2. **What is the unit of augmentation?** Individual (a single developer with Claude Code), team (a GitHub organization using Copilot), population (all English-language developers), or species? The biological and cultural evolutionary frameworks operate at different units; hybrid intelligence governance frameworks need to specify which unit they are optimizing for.

3. **What cognitive traits are being relaxed vs. reinforced by current AI tools?** This is an empirical question currently unanswerable due to tool novelty. But the question is tractable in principle: identify the cognitive demands of pre-AI vs. AI-augmented versions of a task; measure changes in those demand profiles across cohorts with different years of AI tool use.

4. **Does accelerated cultural transmission via AI tools change what gets transmitted?** If AI agents can now transmit expert-level tacit knowledge without a human expert, the selection pressure on human experts to develop and hold that knowledge changes. Does this accelerate capability accumulation (more people can access expert-level knowledge) or erode it (fewer humans develop the deep foundations that make expert knowledge improvable)?

5. **How does differential augmentation interact with the chapter's commons-extraction argument?** If AI tools disproportionately amplify already-advantaged populations, and those populations' outputs disproportionately enter training data, the commons-as-fuel story may compound existing inequalities over successive training rounds. This connects to `f-commons-extraction-inequality.md` (Draft F, Section 1.3 — value asymmetry in the commons).

6. **Is there a point of no return?** The glasses analogy suggests that once a tool is widely adopted and generational selection-relaxation occurs, the population becomes *dependent* on the tool in a way that cannot be reversed without significant fitness cost. If AI cognitive augmentation tools become comparably embedded over generations, their withdrawal would impose cognitive costs on a population that has adapted to their presence. The economic literature on path dependence and technological lock-in provides a formal model for this irreversibility: increasing returns in adoption create a ratchet mechanism that makes alternatives uncompetitive after a critical threshold is crossed (Arthur, 1989). Whether cognitive and biological lock-in follows the same dynamics as economic lock-in remains an open question — but the structural parallel is instructive for governance design. What would governance of that dependency look like?
