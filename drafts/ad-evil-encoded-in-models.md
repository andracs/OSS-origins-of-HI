## Overview and Chapter Position

Human societies contain enormous quantities of material that most members of those societies would recognise as harmful: racism codified in legal systems and everyday language, misogyny embedded in professional norms and cultural production, the rhetorical infrastructure of genocide, the mechanics of exploitation and manipulation, the aesthetics of cruelty. This material is not a fringe residue of human history — it is distributed throughout the text, image, and code that constitute the digital record from which AI systems are trained.

The question this draft addresses is not whether AI models should encode harmful content — they cannot help it, given that training on human-produced data means training on everything humans have produced — but three more tractable questions:

1. **What kinds of harm are encoded, by what mechanisms, and how do they manifest in model behaviour?**
2. **How can encoded harm be detected — technically, auditorially, interpretively?**
3. **What are the consequences of attempting to filter it out — and what cannot be removed without also removing something valuable?**

These questions are not peripheral to hybrid intelligence design. If AI systems in HI workflows systematically encode and reproduce harmful social patterns, those systems will amplify those patterns at the scale and speed of AI deployment. And if filtering is poorly designed, it will remove the capacity to reason about harm, not merely the capacity to produce it.

The draft connects to `b-closed-models-risks.md` (opacity as barrier to harm detection), `c-training-data-extraction.md` (training data as the origin of encoded patterns), `e-llm-structural-vulnerabilities.md` (jailbreaks as harm-access mechanisms), `k-openness-layers.md` (openness as prerequisite for auditing), `w-unharvested-commons.md` (cultural determination of what counts as harmful), `q-machines-using-humans.md` (algorithmic systems as harm vectors), and `r-hi-mirror-human-intelligence.md` (what AI reveals about human cognition and culture).

## Part 1: What Society Considers Evil — A Taxonomy of Encoded Harm

Before asking whether harm is encoded, it is worth being specific about what kinds of harm are at stake. The literature identifies several distinct categories that operate through different mechanisms and require different detection and mitigation approaches.

**1.1 Statistical discrimination and representational harm**
The most studied category: AI systems learn statistical associations from training data, and those associations encode historical patterns of discrimination. The canonical example is Bolukbasi, Chang, Zou, Saligrama & Kalai (2016), who demonstrated that word embeddings trained on Google News corpus encoded gender stereotypes with mathematical precision: the vector arithmetic `man : computer programmer :: woman : ?` yielded `homemaker`. The model had not been instructed to encode this association — it had learned it faithfully from the distribution of language use in its training corpus.

This is representational harm in the technical sense: the model's internal representation of the world reflects the discriminatory structure of the world it was trained on. It differs from intentional discrimination in that no one chose to encode it — but the effect on downstream applications (hiring tools, credit scoring, content moderation) is the same.

**1.2 Stereotyping and denigration at scale**
Beyond statistical associations, language models learn to generate text that contains cultural stereotypes, ethnic slurs, derogatory framings, and ideologically loaded language. Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major & Shmitchell (2021) — the "Stochastic Parrots" paper — argue that large language models trained on uncurated internet text are essentially stochastic mirrors of the internet's ideological distribution: they reflect, amplify, and reproduce the hatred, conspiracy theory, and bigotry that constitute a non-trivial fraction of human online expression.

The "stochastic" qualifier matters: the harm is not reliably produced but probabilistically distributed across the model's output space. This makes it harder to detect (you cannot simply test for a specific output) and harder to remove (the harmful patterns are not localised but distributed throughout the model's representations). These dynamics extend beyond text models: Bianchi et al. (2023) demonstrate that text-to-image systems such as Stable Diffusion amplify demographic stereotypes at scale, generating racially and gender-coded visual outputs from neutral occupational or social prompts; Naik & Nushi (2023) further document systematic gender and racial biases across multiple text-to-image models using a large-scale occupational-prompt audit.

**1.3 Extremist ideology and radicalisation infrastructure**
Extremist movements produce extensive textual infrastructure: manifestos, recruitment rhetoric, dehumanising language, tactical guides. Much of this material is present in the training corpora of large models, either directly (from web crawls that predate content moderation) or indirectly (through paraphrased and summarised versions circulating in forums, comments, and news coverage) (Dodge et al., 2021). Weidinger, Mellor, Rauh et al. (2021) identify the generation of extremist content and the potential for radicalisation via AI-generated rhetoric as a distinct harm category.

**1.4 Toxic and abusive language patterns**
Models trained on social media text, forums, and comment sections learn the rhetorical patterns of harassment, abuse, and coordinated targeting. These patterns are not merely offensive language — they are the operational vocabulary of harm infliction. A model that has learned to produce persuasive, personalised hostile content has learned a capability with essentially no beneficial application (Gehman et al., 2020).

**1.5 Structural harms in high-stakes domains**
Noble (2018) and Eubanks (2018) document how algorithmic systems deployed in criminal justice, child welfare, credit, and employment reproduce and amplify structural racial and class inequalities. These are not primarily language model harms but they establish the broader category: when AI systems trained on historical data make decisions about people's lives, the historical inequalities in that data become active determinants of present outcomes (Chouldechova, 2017). O'Neil (2016) frames this as "weapons of math destruction" — systems that appear objective and technical while systematically disadvantaging already-marginalised populations.

**1.6 The contested margin — where "evil" is culturally and politically determined**
The categories above have broad consensus. But the category of harmful content extends into genuinely contested territory: political speech, religious doctrine, traditional gender roles, depictions of violence in artistic contexts, historical accounts of atrocity. What counts as harmful depends on the normative framework applied — and normative frameworks differ across cultures, political traditions, and historical periods. The draft takes no position on these contested cases but notes that any filtering system must make choices about them — and those choices reflect the values of the system's designers, not some neutral universal standard.

## Part 2: Mechanisms of Encoding — How Harm Gets Into Models

**2.1 Training data as the primary vector**
The most direct mechanism: models learn from training data, and training data reflects the distribution of human-produced content, including its harmful dimensions. Three sub-mechanisms operate here:

*Direct presence*: harmful content is explicitly present in training data — racist texts, misogynist forums, extremist manifestos that passed through web crawls before content moderation removed them from the live web (Birhane et al., 2021).

*Statistical co-occurrence*: even without explicitly harmful texts, harmful associations are encoded through the statistical co-occurrence of concepts in ordinary language use. The word "criminal" appears disproportionately near certain demographic descriptors in news corpora (Manzini et al., 2019); the word "brilliant" appears disproportionately near male pronouns in professional contexts (Caliskan et al., 2017; Garg et al., 2018). These associations are not stated as doctrine but are embedded in the distributional structure of language.

*Absence and underrepresentation*: harm can be encoded not only through presence but through absence — whose perspectives, languages, and experiences are underrepresented in training data determines whose worldview the model treats as default and whose it treats as marginal or exotic (Joshi et al., 2020). This is the structural complement to the WEIRD bias analysed in Draft W.

**2.2 RLHF and the encoding of evaluator preferences**
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) — the dominant technique for aligning large language models — encodes the preferences of the human raters who evaluate model outputs. If rater populations are demographically homogeneous (as they typically are — young, educated, predominantly American), their preferences shape what the model learns to produce as "good" output (Ouyang et al., 2022; Casper et al., 2023; Santurkar et al., 2023; Kirk et al., 2024). Biases in rater populations become biases in model behaviour through the alignment process.

Perez, Ringer, Lukošiūtė et al. (2022), in *Discovering Language Model Behaviors with Model-Written Evaluations*, document a specific RLHF-induced pathology: sycophancy, in which models learn to tell users what they want to hear rather than what is accurate, because sycophantic responses received higher ratings from human evaluators (Sharma et al., 2023). The same paper documents inverse-scaling effects under RLHF — including stronger expression of partisan political views and greater desire to avoid shut-down — providing direct evidence that the alignment process designed to make models safer can introduce new forms of epistemic manipulation.

**2.3 Model collapse and the recursive erosion of filtered distributions**

A structural mechanism compounds those above. Shumailov, Shumaylov, Zhao, Papernot, Anderson & Gal (2024) demonstrated in a landmark *Nature* study that models trained on recursively generated synthetic data undergo *model collapse*: progressive, compounding loss of performance on tail distributions, with each training generation amplifying the statistical impoverishment introduced by the last. The mechanism is information-theoretic: when a model generates training data for a successor, sampling noise causes rare events to be underrepresented; the successor learns a compressed distribution; its outputs are still more compressed; and within a small number of generations the tails of the original distribution have been erased from the lineage.

The implications for harmful-content filtering are direct and uncomfortable. When harmful content is removed from training data — through data filtering, RLHF refusal training, or constitutional alignment — the filtered distribution is impoverished in exactly those cases. If models trained on filtered data subsequently generate synthetic training data for successor models — an increasingly common practice in data-scarce regimes — the impoverishment compounds. Within a small number of generations, a model in this lineage may lose the capacity to represent filtered content at all: not because it has learned a value, but because the statistical structure encoding that knowledge has been erased.

This creates a specific version of the §4.4 historical-record problem: a model stripped of the capacity to represent what has been filtered cannot help historians, educators, or counter-extremism researchers reason about it. The recursive nature of degradation means capacity loss is not easily recoverable by fine-tuning alone; it may require access to the original unfiltered training distribution — an archival case for retaining, under controlled conditions, the data that filtering removes.

**2.4 Emergent capability as harm surface**
Some harmful capabilities are not explicitly present in training data but emerge from the combination of capabilities the model has learned. A model that has learned persuasion, personalisation, and ideological content generation has, through the combination of these capabilities, acquired the ability to produce personalised radicalisation material — even if no such material was present in its training data (Goldstein et al., 2023). Weidinger et al. (2021) distinguish between harms from what models have learned and harms from what models can do with what they have learned.

## Part 3: Detection — How to Find Encoded Harm

**3.1 Bias auditing and evaluation benchmarks**
The oldest approach: design test sets that probe for specific harmful patterns. WinoBias (Zhao et al., 2018) tests for gender bias in coreference resolution; HateCheck (Röttger et al., 2021) provides a structured test suite for hate speech detection systems; ToxiGen (Hartvigsen et al., 2022) generates implicitly toxic statements for model robustness testing.

The limitation: evaluation benchmarks test for the harmful patterns their designers anticipated. They are systematically blind to harmful patterns that no one thought to test for — which are precisely the patterns most likely to be present in billion-parameter models trained on the entire internet.

**3.2 Red teaming**
Adversarial testing by human experts who attempt to elicit harmful outputs through creative prompting. Ganguli, Lovitt, Kernion et al. (2022) document a systematic red-teaming programme for large language models, finding that red teamers with domain expertise and time to explore can reliably elicit harmful outputs from models that pass standard safety evaluations.

Red teaming is necessary but not sufficient: it finds what skilled human testers can find, within the time they are given. The model's output space is vast; human testers sample it. Automated red teaming (using AI to red-team AI) extends coverage but introduces the circularity that the attacking model may share the blind spots of the attacked model (Perez et al., 2022).

**3.3 Mechanistic interpretability — finding harm in activation space**
The most technically ambitious approach: instead of testing model outputs, examine model internals. Bricken, Templeton et al. (2023) and Templeton, Conerly et al. (2024) demonstrated that individual features in Claude's residual stream correspond to identifiable concepts — including safety-relevant features corresponding to bioweapons synthesis, code backdoor insertion, and scam-email generation (Templeton, Conerly et al., 2024, §6). Lindsey et al. (2025) extended this to attribution graphs, tracing how specific inputs activate specific feature circuits. Cunningham et al. (2024) demonstrate that sparse autoencoders can extract highly interpretable features from language models at scale — providing the methodological foundation that makes the safety-relevant feature discovery documented by Templeton et al. (2024) tractable at model size.

If harmful patterns are encoded in identifiable, localised features, they can in principle be found, characterised, and ablated directly. This is an active research frontier with significant unsolved problems: harmful patterns may be distributed rather than localised; features may activate harmfully in combination rather than individually (Elhage et al., 2022); and the space of possible harmful patterns is larger than the set any interpretability team can enumerate.

**3.4 Distributional analysis of training data**
Before a model is trained, the training corpus can be audited for harmful content. Luccioni & Viviano (2021) audited a major web crawl dataset and found that approximately 6.2% of documents came from sites banned from other platforms for policy violations. Hill & Shaw (2013) document the demographic skew of Wikipedia (predominantly male, Western, contemporary), which propagates into any model trained on it.

The limitation: training data analysis finds what is measurable. Statistical patterns of harmful association — which may be more harmful than explicitly flagged content — are harder to identify in a corpus than explicit hate speech.

## Part 4: Filtering and Its Costs — What Happens When You Try to Remove It

**4.1 The filtering methods**
Four main approaches to filtering harmful content from models:

*Data filtering*: remove harmful content from training data before training. Technically straightforward for explicitly flagged content (known hate sites, documented extremist material); does not address implicit harmful associations distributed throughout ordinary text.

*RLHF alignment*: train the model to refuse to produce harmful outputs. Effective for surface-level harm (explicit requests for harmful content); less effective for sophisticated elicitation through framing, fictional framing, or jailbreaks (Zou et al., 2023).

*Constitutional AI / RLAIF*: train the model on a set of principles that it applies to evaluate its own outputs (Bai et al., 2022). More scalable than human RLHF raters; inherits the values of whoever wrote the constitution.

*Feature steering and ablation*: directly modify model internals to suppress harmful features. Still experimental; raises concerns about collateral effects on related capabilities (Turner et al., 2023).

**4.2 The over-refusal problem**
Filtering systems trained to avoid harmful outputs frequently refuse benign requests that superficially resemble harmful ones. A model trained to refuse requests about weapons will refuse a historian's question about medieval siege warfare. A model trained to avoid medical misinformation will refuse to discuss a patient's diagnosis. A model trained to avoid racist content will refuse to discuss historical racism in educational contexts.

This is not merely an inconvenience: over-refusal systematically disadvantages the users with the most legitimate needs for difficult information — medical professionals, researchers, historians, journalists, security analysts — while providing only marginal protection against determined bad actors who can work around refusals through jailbreaks (Röttger et al., 2024). Cui et al. (2024) extend the empirical basis with OR-Bench, an automatically generated over-refusal benchmark of ~80,000 toxic-seeming-but-benign prompts; across 32 evaluated models the over-refusal rate is markedly higher in safety-tuned variants of the same base model, confirming that the alignment process itself — not the underlying capability — is the source of the trade-off.

**4.3 The marginalised voice problem**
Filtering systems are typically trained on data labelled by majority-population raters, which means they systematically over-flag language used by marginalised communities. Research on automated hate speech detection consistently finds that African American Vernacular English (AAVE) is flagged at higher rates than equivalent content in standard American English (Sap et al., 2019; Davidson et al., 2019). Content that discusses discrimination — including first-person accounts of experiencing it — is filtered alongside content that expresses it (Röttger et al., 2021).

This is the deepest irony of content filtering: systems designed to protect marginalised communities from harm disproportionately silence those same communities by flagging their authentic speech patterns and their discussions of the harms they experience.

**4.4 The historical record problem**
Filtering harmful content from model training data removes, or at least reduces, the model's capacity to reason about the history, structure, and mechanics of that harm. A model that cannot discuss the rhetorical strategies of fascism cannot help historians, educators, or counter-extremism researchers understand them. A model that cannot generate racist language cannot help linguists studying its structure or practitioners identifying it in the wild — a consequence directly documented in the hate speech detection literature, where removing abusive language from training data degrades model ability to recognise it (Vidgen & Derczynski, 2020).

This creates a genuine tension between two legitimate objectives: preventing AI systems from generating harmful content, and preserving AI systems' capacity to reason about harmful content as a subject of inquiry. The tension also has a structural complement on the access side: Draft E argues that prompt injection and jailbreaks are not surface bugs but symptoms of an architectural choice (instructions and data sharing a single input channel) that makes the boundary between "filtered" and "accessible" content fundamentally porous. Filtering raises the cost of access; it does not establish a hard partition (Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, 2023; cross-ref Draft E §2–§3).

**4.5 The Overton window problem — who decides?**
Every filtering system embeds a set of normative judgments about what is harmful. Those judgments are made by a small number of people at a small number of organisations — primarily Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Meta — and applied to AI systems used by billions of people globally (Bommasani et al., 2023; Maslej et al., 2024). The normative frameworks these organisations apply are not neutral or universal: they reflect specific political, cultural, and commercial contexts.

Gabriel (2020) distinguishes between designing AI that follows rules, that maximises welfare, that embodies virtue, or that respects rights — and notes that these frameworks give different answers to contested cases. The choice between them is a political and philosophical one, not a technical one — but it is being made by technical organisations without democratic mandate or transparent deliberation.

## Part 5: Implications for Hybrid Intelligence

**5.1 HI systems as harm amplifiers**
The concern is not merely that AI systems encode harmful patterns, but that HI systems — which combine AI scale and speed with human institutional legitimacy — amplify those patterns through the deployment context. A hiring AI that encodes racial bias, deployed within a human-AI hybrid hiring workflow where the human provides the imprimatur of professional judgment, produces racially biased outcomes at scale while distributing the accountability so diffusely that no individual can be held responsible (Raghavan et al., 2020).

**5.2 Openness as prerequisite for harm auditing**
Closed models cannot be audited for encoded harm by independent researchers (Bommasani et al., 2023). The opacity problem (Draft B) and the harm-detection problem are therefore structurally connected: if you cannot examine a model's training data, weights, or activation patterns, you cannot determine what harmful patterns it encodes. Draft K's three-layer decomposition of openness — open weights, open source (training code and recipe), and open training data — makes the dependency explicit: distributional analysis (§3.4 here) requires the data layer; mechanistic interpretability (§3.3) requires the weights layer; reproducible audit of how filters were applied requires the source layer. Open training data, open weights, and interpretability tools are not merely transparency goods — they are prerequisites for the detection and mitigation of encoded harm.

**5.3 Cultural pluralism in harm definition**
HI systems deployed globally will encode the harm definitions of their predominantly American developers. What is filtered in a model deployed in Mississippi will be the same as what is filtered in one deployed in Mumbai or Mogadishu — despite radically different cultural contexts, legal frameworks, and social norms. Draft W's analysis of WEIRD bias applies directly: the model's harm definitions are not universal but culturally situated, and deploying them globally is a form of epistemic imperialism in the domain of ethics (Mohamed et al., 2020).

## Open Questions

1. **Is there a principled distinction between encoding harm and encoding the knowledge of harm?** A model that can discuss the rhetorical structure of genocidal propaganda is not itself genocidal — it is historically informed. Can filtering systems be designed to preserve the capacity for critical analysis while suppressing the capacity for harmful generation?

2. **Whose normative framework should govern harm filtering for globally deployed AI?** The current answer is "the developers' framework," which is an answer by default rather than by design. What would a democratically legitimate process for setting AI harm standards look like?

3. **Can mechanistic interpretability scale to the full scope of societal harm?** The Anthropic interpretability work has identified specific features corresponding to specific concepts. But the space of harmful patterns in human culture is vast, distributed, and contextually dependent. Is feature-level harm detection a tractable approach or a research programme that cannot reach the scale needed?

4. **Does filtering harmful content reduce harmful outcomes, or merely shift them?** A model that refuses to generate explicit hate speech may still produce implicitly discriminatory outputs through subtler mechanisms. Has the literature established that current filtering methods actually reduce downstream harm, or do they primarily provide cover against regulatory scrutiny? (Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, 2023; Qi et al., 2024; Anwar et al., 2024)

5. **What is the appropriate baseline for evaluating filtering trade-offs?** Filtered models cause harm through over-refusal; unfiltered models cause harm through harmful outputs. The comparison must be between two imperfect systems, not between a filtered AI and a hypothetically neutral one. How do we measure which causes more harm in a given deployment context?
