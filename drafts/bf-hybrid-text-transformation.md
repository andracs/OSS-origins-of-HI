## Overview and Chapter Position

Something is happening to text. Not to its production mechanisms — that story is told in Draft AX (the curation turn in training data) and Draft S (the open platforms that built the commons). This draft is about the downstream transformation: what happens to the *character*, *texture*, and *function* of text in the spaces where humans communicate, collaborate, and build knowledge, as AI increasingly participates in producing that text?

Four domains are examined: websites and public web content; academic papers and peer review; software development communication (commit messages, code reviews, GitHub discussions, pull requests); and community discussion spaces (mailing lists, forums, Q&A platforms). In each domain, AI participation is already significant and accelerating. The chapter synthesises what is already observable and offers three scenarios for each domain over a 10–20 year horizon.

The argument is not that AI-generated text is worse than human-generated text. In many cases it is more polished, more consistent, and less prone to surface errors. The argument is that it is *different* in ways that matter — and that the differences compound across the feedback loop in which today's AI-generated text becomes tomorrow's training data.

**The central claim:** AI participation in digital text production creates a progressive homogenisation pressure — a gravitational pull toward the statistical mean of the training distribution — that will affect each domain differently depending on how much that domain's value depends on signal diversity, epistemic surprise, and authentic intent.

## §1 — Scale: From Consumers to Producers of Text

Until approximately 2022, AI systems were consumers of human text. They were trained on web pages, papers, code, and forum posts that humans had written. Since 2023, AI systems have become significant producers in the same spaces they were trained on. This inversion is underway simultaneously across all major text domains.

**Code and software communication:** Daniotti et al. (2026), now published in *Science*, document that approximately 29% of new Python functions on GitHub in the US were AI-generated by end-2024, with a "modest and shrinking lead" over the five comparison countries the authors track (China, France, Germany, India, Russia). The classifier was trained on ~30 million Python commits by ~170,000 developers. This figure measures only code content. The share of AI-assisted commit messages, pull request descriptions, code review comments, and issue tickets is likely higher, as these require less precision and are more amenable to templated generation.

**Academic output:** Liang et al. (2024) estimate that approximately 17% of sentences in submitted papers at major NLP conferences in 2024 showed signs of AI generation, with variation by section (abstract and introduction higher, results lower). A systematic test of 13 major LLMs found all of them capable of generating fabricated academic papers on request (*Nature*, 2026 — cited in Draft H). The Nature News report on hallucinated citations in social sciences preprints (May 2026) identifies AI-assisted writing as a proximate driver of citation pollution.

**Web content:** Direct measurement is difficult; estimates of the AI-generated share of publicly indexed web content range from 10% to over 50% depending on domain and methodology. The SEO content industry, which has historically produced low-quality text at scale for search engine placement, pivoted rapidly to LLM-based production in 2023–2024, with significant effects on search result quality. Perplexity AI and similar retrieval-augmented systems now generate responses that may be cached and re-indexed, accelerating the feedback loop.

**Community discussion:** Stack Overflow banned AI-generated answers in December 2022 following a flood of plausible-but-incorrect responses; it partially reversed this policy in 2023 under traffic pressure. Reddit's 2023 API changes and subsequent user base shifts altered the quality and character of discussion in ways that training data pipelines have not yet fully absorbed. Wikipedia maintains an explicit policy against AI-generated content but faces systematic attribution challenges in detecting it. Draft AV (social platforms as training data) analyses the *upstream* mirror of this dynamic: the same community discussion spaces that BF examines as text-production environments are also the platforms whose behavioural-surplus corpora train the generative systems now producing the AI-styled content under analysis here. The BF–AV connection identifies the closed loop: platform users produce text → platforms license or scrape the corpus → models trained on it generate new text → that text re-enters the platform → quality dynamics shift on the platform.

The aggregate picture: AI is not a marginal contributor in any of these domains. It is already a major co-author of the digital text ecosystem.

## §2 — Software Communication: GitHub, Mailing Lists, Code Review

### 2.1 What AI participation changes in code communication

Code is the best-measured domain. Daniotti et al.'s (2026) *Science* GitHub data establishes the scale. But code itself — the executable artifact — is only part of what circulates on software development platforms. The communicative infrastructure of software development includes:

- **Commit messages**: the record of intent behind each change
- **Pull request descriptions**: the argument for why a change should be merged
- **Code review comments**: the negotiation of quality standards and architectural choices
- **Issue discussions**: the collaborative diagnosis of problems and the specification of solutions
- **Mailing list threads**: the long-form governance discussions of large open-source projects (Linux kernel, CPython, Rust, Debian)

These communicative artifacts serve a function distinct from the code itself: they encode the *reasoning* of the development community — why things were done the way they were, what alternatives were considered, what tradeoffs were accepted. They are the primary channel through which tacit knowledge is transmitted in software development communities (Collins, 2010; Polanyi, 1966 — see Draft G, Open Question #7).

When AI generates or assists in generating these texts, several things change:

**Signal fidelity degrades.** A commit message reading "refactored authentication module for improved performance" is useful only if it reflects actual developer intent. An AI-generated commit message for the same change — formally correct, grammatically polished — may accurately describe the surface change while omitting the context that made it necessary (a specific bug, a security concern, a future architectural direction). Over time, the signal function of commit messages — their usefulness to future maintainers trying to understand the history of a codebase — degrades.

**Code review homogenises.** AI-assisted code review produces more consistent feedback but potentially less diverse feedback. Human reviewers bring domain-specific knowledge, aesthetic preferences, and accumulated experience that introduces productive heterogeneity into review. AI review, calibrated to the distribution of "good review" in training data, tends toward standard patterns. The Linux kernel development community, which maintains one of the most intensive human review cultures in software, has so far explicitly resisted AI review tools — a revealing data point about what veteran developers believe is at stake.

**Mailing list culture shifts.** The governance discussions of large open-source projects — particularly the formal, technical mailing list culture of projects like the Linux kernel or Python development — have historically been distinguished by high-signal, high-accountability communication: participants sign their names to specific technical positions, which creates reputation stakes that constrain low-effort contributions. AI-assisted contributions may lower the barrier to participation in these discussions, which could be democratising — or could dilute the quality signal that high-reputation mailing list culture depends on.

### 2.2 Scenarios for software communication

**Scenario A — Stratification (10-year horizon):** A stable two-tier system emerges. AI handles boilerplate communication (routine commit messages, standard PR templates, first-pass review checklists). Human communicative effort concentrates on the architecturally and politically significant decisions. The mailing list culture of major projects develops explicit norms distinguishing AI-assisted from human-authored contributions. Signal diversity is preserved in the tier that matters; signal-to-noise in routine communication may actually improve.

**Scenario B — Erosion (10-year horizon):** The homogenising pressure accumulates gradually. Commit message quality degrades uniformly — no single change is alarming, but the archive of reasoning becomes progressively thinner. New contributors, learning code review norms from AI-generated examples, produce AI-like reviews that miss the tacit dimensions. The mailing list culture of governance discussions becomes increasingly dominated by well-formed but shallow contributions. Tacit knowledge transmission slows.

**Scenario C — New forms (10-year horizon):** The communicative artifacts adapt. New conventions emerge — structured formats that separate the AI-assisted summary from the human-authored rationale. Semantic version control tools that preserve reasoning chains explicitly rather than relying on unstructured commit messages. The loss of the old communicative infrastructure is real but partially compensated by new infrastructure designed for the hybrid environment.

## §3 — Academic Papers and Peer Review

### 3.1 The structure of AI participation in scholarly communication

Academic communication has a more complex structure than software communication: it includes the paper itself, but also the peer review, the conference discussion, the citation network, and the informal channels (preprints, Twitter/X threads, Slack discussions in research communities) that increasingly mediate how findings circulate before formal publication.

AI participation is unevenly distributed across these layers:

**Paper writing:** AI assistance is heaviest in sections with high formulaic content — methods sections following established protocols, literature review synthesis, discussion sections framing contributions relative to existing literature. Results sections, which report specific empirical findings, show lower AI-generation rates (Liang et al., 2024). The differential is important: the parts of a paper most amenable to AI assistance are precisely the parts whose quality readers have previously used as proxies for other quality signals.

**Hallucinated citations as structural risk:** The phenomenon of AI systems confabulating plausible-sounding but nonexistent references (Ji et al., 2023; see Draft U) creates a specific risk in academic communication. A hallucinated citation in a paper is not merely an error — it propagates through the citation network as a false signal of support. Downstream papers may cite the original paper "via" the hallucinated reference, creating a phantom chain of evidence. The Nature News reports on hallucinated citations in social sciences preprints (April and May 2026) identify this as a systemic problem already affecting the scholarly record.

**Peer review:** AI-assisted reviewing is now widespread across disciplines. The consequences are contested. Studies of AI-generated reviews find they are more consistent and cover formal checklists more thoroughly than average human reviews, but miss the evaluative dimensions that require deep domain expertise — the sense of whether a result is surprising given what the reviewer knows about the field, or whether a methodology is sound given tacit knowledge about where it has previously failed. The detection problem is severe: reviewers cannot currently verify whether submitted reviews are human-authored, and editors cannot easily distinguish AI-assisted from AI-generated reviews.

### 3.2 The citation network as epistemic infrastructure

The citation network is the primary formal infrastructure through which knowledge claims are connected, evaluated, and propagated in science. It functions by assuming that each citation represents a human reader's judgement that the cited work is relevant and credible. AI participation breaks this assumption in two ways: by generating false citations (hallucination) and by generating citation lists optimised for plausibility rather than genuine intellectual engagement.

Over a 10–20 year horizon, if a significant share of citations in a field are AI-generated — even if each individually plausible — the citation network loses its epistemic function. The bibliometric signals used to evaluate research quality (citation counts, h-indices, journal impact factors) are calibrated against a world in which each citation represents a human judgement. In a world where many citations are AI-generated, these metrics measure something different and less useful.

This is the academic-domain instance of the more general problem: AI participation in text production degrades the signal function of textual artifacts that were previously reliable proxies for human cognitive investment.

### 3.3 Scenarios for academic communication

**Scenario A — Formalised hybrid authorship (10-year horizon):** Major journals adopt disclosure and verification requirements for AI-assisted writing. Peer review protocols develop structured formats distinguishing AI-assisted checklist evaluation from human expert judgement. The citation network develops verification infrastructure (cryptographic signing, automated retraction propagation) that partially restores its epistemic function. The adjustment is costly but the field adapts.

**Scenario B — Quality signal collapse in low-prestige venues (10-year horizon):** A bifurcated landscape emerges in which high-prestige venues (flagship journals, top conferences) develop robust verification infrastructure while lower-prestige venues are flooded with AI-assisted content of variable quality. The citation network in high-prestige venues retains epistemic function; in lower-prestige venues it degrades. This widens the gap between elite research communities and the broader scholarly periphery.

**Scenario C — Preprint bypass (10-year horizon):** The formal publication system becomes less relevant as research communities develop alternative quality-signalling mechanisms: curated preprint reading groups, credentialed reviewer endorsement systems, and replication bounties. AI participation is highest in the parts of the formal system (methods sections, literature reviews) that were already most formulaic; the intellectually significant parts of research communication migrate to informal channels where AI participation is lower and accountability is higher.

## §4 — Websites, Web Forums, and the Public Information Ecosystem

### 4.1 The SEO-to-AI content pipeline

The public web has always contained a significant volume of low-quality, programmatically generated text produced for search engine optimisation rather than reader value. The SEO content industry — which at its peak employed large numbers of low-paid writers producing templated articles to rank for specific search queries — is being rapidly displaced by AI generation that produces the same material at lower cost and higher volume.

The consequences for the public information ecosystem are compounding:

**Search result quality:** Search engines optimised against human-generated content quality signals face adversarial AI content at scale. The signal arms race between search engines and SEO content producers, which was already degrading search quality in specialised domains (product reviews, health information, financial guidance), has been substantially accelerated by AI generation.

**Homogenisation pressure:** AI-generated web content clusters around the mean of the training distribution in style, structure, and argument. Pages that cover the same topic from AI generation tend to be more similar to each other than human-authored pages on the same topic. At scale, this reduces the diversity of the web as a source of perspectives — the topical coverage may be broad but the range of framings, emphases, and arguments narrows.

**The Wikipedia anomaly:** Wikipedia has maintained explicit policies against AI-generated content and has thus far preserved its character as human-authored. This makes Wikipedia increasingly anomalous — a large, stable, human-authored repository in a web that is rapidly becoming majority AI-generated. Its value as training data and as a ground-truth reference will depend on maintaining this anomaly.

### 4.2 Forum and Q&A platform dynamics

Stack Overflow's experience with AI-generated answers is instructive. In December 2022, the platform banned AI-generated content following a flood of plausible-but-incorrect answers. The problem was not simply incorrectness — Stack Overflow had always contained incorrect answers. The problem was that AI-generated incorrect answers were *confidently styled* in a way that made them harder to identify and flag, and they were produced at a volume that overwhelmed the community's moderation capacity. In 2023, Stack Overflow partially reversed the ban amid declining traffic — a signal that the platform's community moderation model was under stress even without AI content.

This trajectory — initial resistance, policy reversal under traffic pressure, gradual transformation of community character — is likely to recur across other forum and Q&A platforms as moderation capacity is outpaced by AI-assisted content volume.

## §5 — The Model Collapse Risk: When AI Trains on AI

The feedback loop from AI-generated text back into training data poses a structural risk to the quality trajectory of future AI systems. Shumailov et al. (2024, *Nature*) demonstrate empirically that models trained on AI-generated data without adequate admixture of human-generated data exhibit *model collapse* — a progressive degradation in which output diversity decreases, tail distribution content disappears, and the model progressively converges toward a narrow, low-entropy output distribution. The mechanism: AI-generated text underrepresents the rare but informative examples that populate the tails of the human-generated distribution; training on AI-generated text systematically prunes these examples, and each training generation prunes further.

Applied to the domains discussed in this chapter: if GitHub commit messages, academic paper prose, and web content are increasingly AI-generated, and these artifacts form part of the training corpora for the next generation of models (as they currently do — see Draft C and Draft S), then the model collapse dynamic will progressively degrade the quality and diversity of the baseline text that models are calibrated against. The result is a ratchet: each generation of AI models is trained on a higher share of AI-generated text, producing slightly less diverse and slightly more mean-converging output, which enters the next training cycle at higher volume.

Draft AX's "curation turn" analysis is directly relevant here: the curation strategies developed for training data quality (FineWeb, DCLM-Baseline, synthetic data with quality filters) represent an emerging institutional response to this risk. But curation strategies applied to training data do not address the depletion of human-generated signal in the source domains themselves — if GitHub is 50% AI-generated, aggressive filtering of the GitHub corpus still produces a filtered-AI-text output rather than a human-generated output.

The model collapse risk is the most structurally concerning long-run implication of AI participation in text production. It is also the one most underweighted in current AI governance discussions, which focus on individual harms rather than systemic degradation.

## §6 — Synthesis: The Homogenisation Pressure

Across all four domains, a common dynamic is visible: AI participation exerts a *homogenisation pressure* that reduces the diversity of text along multiple dimensions — style, argument structure, epistemic framing, and tonal register. This pressure operates not through any single mechanism but through the statistical tendency of language models to produce output near the mean of their training distribution.

The consequences depend on how much each domain's value depends on signal diversity:

- **Code communication** derives value partly from diversity of reasoning and judgment — homogenisation erodes the tacit knowledge transmission function.
- **Academic communication** derives value from epistemic surprise and authentic expertise — homogenisation erodes the signal function of citations and review.
- **Web content** derives value partly from the diversity of human perspectives — homogenisation degrades the web's usefulness as an information retrieval system.
- **Community discussion** derives value from authentic human engagement and reputation-staked argument — homogenisation degrades the accountability mechanisms.

This is a structural, not a moral, argument. AI-generated text is not "bad" in any simple sense. It is stylistically competent and often informative. The problem is systemic: it is calibrated to resemble average high-quality human text, and at scale it replaces the distribution of human text — including the tails of that distribution where the most novel, surprising, and domain-transforming contributions live.

The long-run risk is not that AI text is worse than human text on average — it may be better on average. The risk is the progressive loss of the variance that makes text ecologies informationally rich.

## §7 — Corpus Biodiversity and Epistemic Resilience

### 7.1 The ecological analogy

Ecosystems with high species diversity are more resilient to perturbation, recover faster from shocks, and maintain function under changed conditions than monocultures. The Irish potato famine (1845–1852) is the canonical case: a single-variety crop, optimised for productivity under stable conditions, was devastated by a pathogen to which it had no variance-based resistance. The Cavendish banana, which replaced the Gros Michel as the global commercial variety after Panama disease, is under the same threat today. Agricultural monocultures are not failures of productivity — they maximise yield under predictable conditions. They are failures of resilience.

The analogy to training corpora is not merely rhetorical. A corpus constituted from diverse epistemic traditions, linguistic registers, argumentative styles, and knowledge domains provides:

**Robustness to domain shift:** Models trained on diverse corpora generalise better to novel domains than models trained on narrower but deeper corpora. When real-world conditions shift — a new field emerges, a cultural context changes, a technical domain evolves — the diverse corpus provides analogues that the narrow corpus does not.

**Resilience to targeted attack:** A corpus with high diversity is harder to manipulate by flooding it with content from a single source or in a single style, because the manipulated content represents a smaller fraction of the total. The monoculture corpus is more vulnerable to the "invasive species" problem described in §8.

**Capacity for paradigm shift:** This is the most important dimension. Paradigm shifts — the genuinely novel ideas that restructure a field or discipline — do not come from the centre of the distribution. They come from the margins: from researchers working in minority traditions, from practitioners in domains not yet integrated into mainstream discourse, from thinkers using conceptual vocabularies that the mainstream regards as eccentric. A corpus that accurately represents the full distribution of human thought — including its tails — is a corpus that can generate the conditions for encountering genuine novelty. A homogenised corpus, calibrated to the mean, systematically removes these conditions.

### 7.2 Three dimensions of corpus biodiversity

**Epistemic biodiversity:** The diversity of *what is claimed and how claims are justified*. This includes variation in epistemological frameworks (empiricist, rationalist, phenomenological, indigenous), in standards of evidence (statistical significance, case study, oral tradition, formal proof), and in the relationship between claim and evidence (deductive, inductive, abductive). Current large training corpora are heavily weighted toward Western academic and professional English-language epistemological conventions. Draft W's analysis of the "unharvested commons" — oral traditions, indigenous knowledge systems, non-Western scholarly traditions that never made it into digitised form — identifies the most severe epistemic biodiversity loss: these knowledge systems were never in the corpus to begin with, and their absence means that homogenisation further amplifies an already severe initial underrepresentation.

**Linguistic biodiversity:** The diversity of languages, registers, dialects, and styles in which knowledge is encoded. A model that has encountered a wide range of linguistic variety is better calibrated to diverse human communicative contexts. But linguistic diversity in training corpora has been declining: the share of English and a small number of high-resource languages in frontier model training corpora has grown relative to the global distribution of languages, not because of deliberate exclusion but because high-volume digitised text skews toward high-literacy, high-internet-penetration populations.

**Stylistic and argumentative biodiversity:** The diversity of *how* knowledge claims are constructed and communicated — narrative versus analytical, dialectical versus expository, polemic versus cautious. This dimension is the most directly threatened by the homogenisation pressure identified in §6. AI-generated text clusters around a recognisable stylistic mean: grammatically clean, formally structured, hedged with appropriate epistemic markers, tending toward the synthesis of existing positions rather than the assertion of novel ones. The idiosyncratic voice — the argumentative style that is distinctive, risky, even abrasive — is exactly what the mean-converging generation removes.

### 7.3 Keystone species: the disproportionate importance of marginal knowledge

In ecology, keystone species are those whose removal has effects on the ecosystem disproportionate to their biomass or abundance. The sea otter is the canonical example: its near-extinction through fur hunting triggered a trophic cascade (urchin population explosion → kelp forest collapse → loss of fish habitat) that reshaped entire coastal ecosystems. The otter was not the most abundant organism; it was the critical regulator of a key relationship.

The training corpus equivalent: certain rare, idiosyncratic, or marginal forms of text may function as keystone contributions — their presence sustains connections and capabilities that would be lost if they were absent, connections that are not visible from the frequency distribution of the corpus alone. Candidate examples:

- **Edge-case documentation in open-source projects:** Detailed, idiosyncratic documentation of unusual edge cases and failure modes may be rare by frequency but crucial for generating robust code. An AI code model that has encountered a well-documented failure mode for a cryptographic library is in a qualitatively different position from one that has not, even if such documentation constitutes a tiny fraction of the corpus.

- **Minority scientific traditions:** Unorthodox but eventually vindicated scientific claims are, at the time they are made, marginal and underrepresented. A corpus that preserves heterodox minority positions maintains the capacity to support reasoning along lines that are later proven correct. A corpus that amplifies consensus positions loses this capacity.

- **Non-Western epistemological frameworks:** As Draft W argues, indigenous and non-Western knowledge systems encode relationships between phenomena — ecological, social, therapeutic — that Western scientific frameworks have sometimes been unable to formalise but which turn out to be empirically sound. Their absence from training corpora is an epistemic loss not only for those communities but for the systems trained on those corpora.

### 7.4 The genetic bottleneck problem

Population genetics identifies *genetic bottleneck* events — episodes in which a population's size contracts sharply, reducing the genetic diversity of subsequent generations even after the population recovers. The cheetah's famously low genetic diversity traces to a bottleneck approximately 10,000 years ago; the low diversity makes the species disproportionately vulnerable to infectious disease.

The training corpus equivalent of a genetic bottleneck is the scenario in which a generation of AI models is trained primarily on AI-generated text derived from a narrow original base. Even if the volume of training data is large, its effective diversity is low — it represents recombination of a restricted set of patterns rather than genuine sampling from the full space of human thought. Subsequent model generations inherit this narrowed basis; the bottleneck compounds with each generation. Shumailov et al.'s (2024) model collapse finding is the empirical instantiation of this dynamic: the collapse manifests first at the tails — the rare, the unusual, the idiosyncratic — precisely the content that constitutes the corpus's epistemic biodiversity.

The governance implication is structural: maintaining corpus biodiversity requires active curation, not just avoiding deliberate monoculturing. It requires something analogous to seed banks — explicit preservation of the rare, the marginal, and the idiosyncratic against the homogenisation pressure. Draft AX's curation turn addresses quality filtering; the biodiversity dimension requires an additional curation criterion: *diversity preservation*, which is not captured by standard quality metrics.

## §8 — Harmful Content in the Training Loop: Amplification Scenarios

### 8.1 The amplification mechanism

Model collapse (§5) describes a failure mode in which AI-generated text causes training-loop degradation toward a low-entropy, low-diversity output distribution. The concern in this section is related but distinct: not the degradation of quality in general, but the *directional amplification* of specific harmful patterns. The mechanism is the same — AI-generated text enters the training corpus — but the concern is not that the output becomes blander but that it becomes systematically worse along specific dimensions.

The amplification mechanism operates as follows: a harmful pattern (demographic bias, a false claim, a harmful framing) is present in the training corpus at frequency *f*. AI models trained on this corpus generate text that reflects the pattern at frequency *f* or higher (due to over-representation of majority patterns relative to their training frequency). This AI-generated text enters the web, the code base, the academic record. When the next generation of models is trained, the corpus now contains the original harmful pattern at frequency *f* plus the AI-generated version at additional frequency *Δ*. The next generation trains on *f + Δ*, generating text at *f + 2Δ*. The pattern amplifies monotonically with each training generation.

Unlike model collapse, which is a decay process affecting all output dimensions, amplification is directional: it preferentially strengthens whatever is already present. This makes it both more dangerous (targeted harm grows) and harder to correct (the harm is encoded more deeply with each generation).

### 8.2 Scenario 1 — Bias amplification loop

*Starting condition:* A large language model trained on corpus *C₀* produces text with a measurable demographic bias — for instance, over-associating certain professional roles with specific genders in generated text, or systematically under-representing non-Western cultural contexts in generated examples. This is not a hypothetical: Bender et al. (2021) document systematic demographic bias in large language models as a structural consequence of corpus imbalances; multiple subsequent studies confirm the finding across model families and domains (see Draft AD for the technical encoding mechanism).

*Generation 1:* AI-assisted text production begins flooding the training domains discussed in this chapter. Code review comments generated with AI assistance carry the demographic bias of the original model. Academic paper methods sections generated with AI assistance reflect the cultural framing of the original corpus. Web content generated with AI assistance over-represents the demographic and cultural defaults of the first-generation models.

*Generation 2:* The next generation of models is trained on a corpus that contains both the original human-generated text (with bias *f*) and a large volume of first-generation AI text (with bias *f + ε*). The effective bias in the training corpus is now *f + (volume-weighted share) × ε*. If the AI-generated text constitutes a substantial fraction of the corpus, the bias increase may be significant.

*Generation 3:* The pattern continues. Each generation, the corpus contains a higher share of AI-generated text, and the bias accumulates. After several generations, the bias may be substantially higher than in the original corpus even though no individual decision to introduce or amplify bias was made. The harm is emergent from the feedback loop.

*Governance implications:* Standard bias auditing and post-hoc debiasing interventions applied to individual models do not address the inter-generational amplification. They reduce bias in the current generation's outputs but do not prevent those outputs from entering the training corpus and amplifying bias in the next generation. Addressing this requires corpus-level interventions: tracking the provenance share of AI-generated text in training corpora, applying bias-aware filtering, and maintaining diverse human-authored corpora as de-biasing anchors. None of these interventions are standard practice at the current frontier.

### 8.3 Scenario 2 — Misinformation laundering

*Starting condition:* AI systems generate false or misleading claims — either through hallucination or through deliberate adversarial prompting. A specific false medical claim, a fabricated historical statistic, a plausible-sounding but incorrect technical assertion. These claims appear in web content, in academic preprints, and potentially in AI-assisted reviews or forum posts.

*Propagation:* The false claim, styled with the characteristic confidence and polish of AI-generated text, circulates. It is cited — by AI-assisted papers that surface it through literature search, by web content generators that find it in search results, by forum participants who have encountered it. Citation, even by AI systems, is the mechanism through which a claim in the academic ecosystem acquires apparent epistemic authority: a claim cited in three papers occupies a different status than a claim cited in none, regardless of whether the citations represent genuine human evaluation.

*Training corpus entry:* The false claim, now appearing in multiple documents and carrying citation weight, enters the training corpus of the next generation of models. The model is not in a position to distinguish between "claim that is widely believed" and "claim that is true" — it learns the distribution of claims in the corpus, not their correspondence to the world. The false claim is now encoded as part of the model's world model, with the apparent authority of frequency and citation density.

*Amplification:* Second-generation models generate text that includes the false claim with higher frequency. More web content, more AI-assisted papers, more review comments cite or assume it. The claim has been laundered: it entered the corpus as a hallucination or adversarial insertion; it exits subsequent generations as apparent conventional wisdom.

*The scientific consensus manipulation variant:* This scenario has a deliberate version. Corporate or ideological actors with the capability to generate large volumes of AI-assisted content can flood preprint servers, web forums, and GitHub discussions with content supporting a particular position. The volume of AI-generated supporting content creates a false consensus signal. Models trained on this corpus encode the manufactured consensus. This is not science fiction: the tobacco industry's manufactured scientific controversy over smoking harms was a pre-AI version of the same strategy. AI generation reduces the cost of this strategy by orders of magnitude.

### 8.4 Scenario 3 — Normalization of harmful discourse

*Starting condition:* Training corpora contain text from the full range of human discourse, including hate speech, extremist content, and harmful ideological framings. Alignment training (RLHF, Constitutional AI) reduces model tendency to generate such content, but alignment is imperfect and adversarial prompting can elicit it. Some AI-generated text contains harmful discourse that passes through content filters.

*Normalization mechanism:* Harmful discourse that appears in AI-generated content with a characteristic style — syntactically clean, argumentatively structured, stripped of the obvious markers of inflammatory intent — circulates differently from human-generated hate speech, which carries the register markers that content moderation systems are calibrated to detect. AI-styled harmful discourse may evade automated detection while spreading in community discussion spaces, comment sections, and mailing lists.

*Training corpus effect:* The normalized, style-cleaned version of harmful discourse enters the corpus at higher frequency. Future models encounter it as part of the normal distribution of discourse rather than as an outlier. The Overton window — the range of discourse that models treat as normal — shifts. Models generate content that treats previously marginal harmful positions as part of the discussable spectrum.

*The echo chamber variant:* In community discussion spaces with demographic concentration (politically homogeneous forums, niche technical communities), AI-assisted content generation may accelerate the echo chamber dynamic: users who generate AI-assisted responses to community discussion are more likely to generate content that matches community norms, because they are sampling from a model calibrated to the average of the community's prior discourse. This creates a feedback loop within the community's own communication: the community's average position becomes more extreme with each generation of AI-assisted discussion, and that extreme content re-enters the broader corpus. The dynamic is amplified when the community sits on a commercial social platform whose recommender system already amplifies engagement-maximising content (Draft AV §2): the platform's algorithmic amplification compounds with the model's mean-converging generation, and the joint effect is a corpus drift that neither component would produce alone.

### 8.5 Scenario 4 — Code vulnerability propagation

*Starting condition:* Pearce et al. (2022) demonstrate that GitHub Copilot generates code with security vulnerabilities in approximately 40% of scenarios when tested against a set of security-sensitive coding tasks. This finding is not surprising: the model is trained on the distribution of code on GitHub, which contains a significant volume of insecure code. The model learns to reproduce the distribution of code practice, including its security failures.

*Propagation:* AI-generated insecure code appears in public repositories, in Stack Overflow answers, in tutorial content. It is plausible, functional for non-adversarial inputs, and passes basic testing. Developers copy it or generate similar patterns. It is cited as examples in documentation. It propagates through the ecosystem of code sharing.

*Training corpus effect:* The next generation of code models is trained on a corpus with a higher share of AI-generated code, which carries the security failure patterns of the previous generation. The models learn these patterns as normal practice. The share of AI-generated code with security vulnerabilities may increase even if the models are more sophisticated in other respects.

*The specificity of the harm:* Unlike bias amplification or discourse normalization, the code vulnerability scenario produces harms that manifest at specific, detectable events: a deployed system is compromised, a vulnerability is exploited, data is stolen or infrastructure is damaged. The harm is not diffuse but concentrated at the point of deployment. This makes the amplification scenario more tractable to study — vulnerability rates in deployed AI-generated code are measurable — but the systemic inter-generational amplification remains difficult to disentangle from other factors affecting code security.

## Open Questions

1. **Is the homogenisation pressure domain-specific or universal?** Different domains vary in how much they reward conformity to average quality versus deviation from it. Code in well-specified problem domains (sorting algorithms, database queries) may be genuinely served by AI convergence on best practices. Scientific hypothesis generation may require the opposite. A domain-by-domain analysis of the value of variance is needed.

2. **What is the half-life of human-authored text's epistemic authority?** If AI-generated text increasingly dominates each domain, the human-authored residue becomes both more valuable (as authentic signal) and harder to identify. How long before the provenance advantage of human-authored text can no longer be verified at scale, and what institutional mechanisms could extend that half-life?

3. **Does model collapse risk imply that AI text-production has a natural ceiling?** Shumailov et al.'s analysis suggests that an AI ecosystem training primarily on its own output will degrade. If this is correct, the growth of AI text production has an endogenous limit — unless training data governance keeps the human-generated signal share above some threshold. What is that threshold? Is it domain-specific?

4. **Can community governance models adapted from Wikipedia's anti-AI content policy scale to other domains?** Wikipedia's policy is maintained by a volunteer community with strong identity and accountability norms. Stack Overflow's experience suggests these norms are under stress at lower levels of community cohesion. What governance models are viable for preserving authentic human text in domains where community enforcement capacity is lower?

5. **What does stratification between AI-text infrastructure and premium human text mean for access inequality?** If AI-generated text becomes the default public information layer and human-curated/authored text migrates to premium or credentialed access tiers, the information inequality implications are significant. This connects to Draft H's cognitive complement divide and Draft W's unharvested commons analysis.

6. **How does the academic fraud risk change scientific discourse dynamics?** If the cost of generating a plausible fake paper approaches zero, what equilibrium does the scientific community reach? Does verification infrastructure scale with fraud capacity, or is there a threshold beyond which the scholarly record loses credibility as an epistemic institution?

7. **Is a corpus biodiversity metric technically feasible?** Ecosystem biodiversity is measured through indices (Shannon entropy, species richness, evenness). An analogous metric for training corpora — capturing diversity across epistemic tradition, linguistic register, and argumentative style — would provide a governance-relevant quantity. What would such a metric measure, and could it be computed at the scale of frontier training corpora?

8. **What threshold of human-authored text is needed to prevent harmful amplification?** The bias amplification and misinformation laundering scenarios suggest that maintaining a sufficient share of diverse, authentic human-generated text in training corpora is protective. But what share is sufficient? Is there a phase transition below which amplification becomes self-sustaining? This is an empirical question that requires longitudinal data on model generations not yet available.

9. **Can the deliberate corpus poisoning scenario be regulated?** The scientific consensus manipulation variant of §8.3 describes a deliberate adversarial strategy. Is it addressable through regulation (disclosure requirements for AI-assisted academic content), through technical means (provenance tracking, cryptographic signing), or does it require a structural response (alternative epistemic infrastructure that is more resistant to flooding)?

10. **How does accidental contamination interact with deliberate attack?** The accidental contamination scenarios in §10 (context stripping, translationese, benchmark leakage) degrade the corpus in ways that make deliberate attacks harder to detect — a poisoned document is less anomalous if the corpus already contains a high background rate of malformed, decontextualised, or translated-artifact text. Does ambient accidental contamination effectively lower the detection threshold for deliberate attacks?

11. **Is there a detection threshold for token-level corpus attacks?** The rare-token injection and Unicode steganography attacks described in §9 operate below the level of human reading. Are they detectable through statistical analysis of corpus composition? What anomaly signature would a large-scale rare-token flooding campaign produce in a corpus, and is that signature distinguishable from legitimate usage change?

## §10 — Accidental Contamination: How Normal Processes Degrade the Commons

### 10.1 The non-malicious pollution problem

Sections §8 and §9 describe harmful corpus dynamics driven by either emergent incentive structures or deliberate adversarial strategy. A third category is both more mundane and, in aggregate, potentially more consequential: contamination that occurs through the normal operation of content production, aggregation, and scraping systems with no harmful intent on the part of any individual actor. The corpus is polluted not because someone wanted to pollute it but because the machinery of assembling it at scale is imprecise and the imprecision compounds.

The concept maps directly onto accidental environmental contamination: industrial effluent entering a waterway is not intended to damage the ecosystem, but the damage is real and cumulative. Individual contributions are harmless at the source; the aggregate in the substrate is toxic.

### 10.2 Context collapse: irony, satire, and quotes stripped of framing

Web scraping strips page structure. What remains after scraping is typically the main text content of a page, with headers, navigation, metadata, and visual cues removed. This stripping is a catastrophic loss of context for a specific class of text.

**Satire and irony.** Satirical articles — from *The Onion*, national equivalents, and user-generated parody — are written to be read in the context of a recognised satirical publication. The framing ("This is *The Onion*") is what triggers the reader's interpretive mode. Stripped of that framing and scraped as text, a satirical headline reads as a factual assertion. Models trained on scraped corpora that include satirical content without framing learn the satirical assertions as part of the factual distribution. The result is not random noise but systematic bias toward the specific kinds of claims that satire produces: exaggerated, emotionally heightened, politically charged, and structurally plausible.

**Quoted speech and attributed positions.** A news article critiquing a politician's statement typically quotes the statement and then rebuts it. Scraped as a document, the article may be learned as an endorsement of the quoted position, because the rebuttal is in a different syntactic position from the quote and the model learns the association between topic and claim, not the argumentative structure that frames the claim as disputed. At scale, this produces systematic bias in model representations of contested political positions.

**Fictional and illustrative examples.** Academic papers, textbooks, and journalism routinely generate synthetic examples to illustrate points: "imagine a user who says X", "consider a scenario where Y". These invented examples, written to make arguments, are scraped as data points. A model trained on a corpus containing many academic papers learns the illustrative examples as real instances. The fictional becomes embedded as training signal.

### 10.3 Translationese and machine-translation artifacts

A significant share of multilingual training corpora consists of text that has been machine-translated rather than originally authored in the target language. Before the current generation of high-quality neural machine translation, large volumes of text were translated using statistical and phrase-based MT systems that produced characteristic artifacts: unnaturally literal word order, calque constructions that reproduce source-language idioms in the target language, and systematic errors at morphological boundaries.

These artifacts are systematic, not random. A model trained on a corpus containing large volumes of translated text learns the artifact patterns as plausible text in the target language. For high-resource languages (English, German, French), the dilution is manageable because the volume of originally authored text is high. For low-resource languages — the languages already underrepresented in training corpora for the reasons Draft W identifies — translated content may constitute a majority of the corpus, and the model's output in those languages is substantially shaped by MT artifacts. The accidental contamination is worst where the underlying corpus is already thinnest.

The current generation of LLM-produced translations has better surface quality than earlier MT, but introduces its own characteristic patterns: certain phrasings, hedging constructions, and paragraph structures that are identifiable as LLM outputs. As LLM-produced translations enter the corpus, these patterns become self-reinforcing.

### 10.4 Benchmark contamination: the evaluation collapse

A specific and well-documented form of accidental contamination is the inadvertent inclusion of evaluation benchmark data in training corpora. Evaluation benchmarks — standardised test sets used to measure model performance — are typically drawn from the same public text sources that training corpora are scraped from: Wikipedia, web text, books. If a benchmark is constructed from text that is also present in the training corpus, the model's performance on that benchmark measures memorisation at least as much as generalisation.

This is not a hypothetical: Brown et al. (2020) acknowledged possible contamination in GPT-3 evaluations; subsequent analysis of major benchmarks has found contamination across multiple datasets and model families (Magar & Schwartz, 2022). The contamination is accidental — benchmark designers did not intend to include training data, and model trainers did not intend to include benchmark data — but its consequence is epistemically serious: the metrics used to compare model generations may not measure what they purport to measure, and performance improvements attributable to better generalisation may be partially contamination-driven memorisation.

For the purposes of this chapter, benchmark contamination is a case of accidental corpus pollution with direct consequences for the hybrid intelligence system's epistemic infrastructure: the feedback loop from model evaluation to model improvement — the mechanism supposed to drive capability advances — is corrupted at its measurement point.

### 10.5 Temporal contamination: outdated claims as current ground truth

Training corpora are assembled at a point in time but models are deployed over extended periods. Text that was accurate when scraped becomes inaccurate as the world changes. This is a known limitation — the "knowledge cutoff" problem — but its character as contamination is underemphasised.

The contamination is not uniform. Some claims have long half-lives (mathematical truths, historical facts about completed events, scientific consensus on settled questions). Others have short half-lives (population statistics, technology specifications, political configurations, prices, personnel). A model trained on a corpus from 2022 has been contaminated, from a 2026 perspective, with a specific pattern of outdated short-half-life claims that it cannot distinguish from its long-half-life claims. It does not know what it does not know has changed.

The temporal contamination problem is structurally different from the others in this section: it requires no scraping error, no missing context, no translation artifact. The text was accurate at the time. The contamination is introduced by the passage of time and the static nature of the corpus. Continuous training and retrieval-augmented generation are partial responses, but they address the symptom (outdated retrieval) rather than the underlying structure (the model's inability to represent its own temporal uncertainty).

### 10.6 Self-referential loops: AI describing AI becoming training data

A structurally novel contamination source has emerged with the proliferation of AI-generated and AI-discussing text: a growing share of web content, forum discussion, academic papers, and even code documentation now consists of text that describes, quotes, critiques, or imitates AI system behaviour. This text is scraped into training corpora and the next generation of models learns from it.

The contamination is self-referential. When a forum post quotes a ChatGPT response in order to discuss it, the scraped version may learn the quoted response as an example of how to respond to that type of query. When a paper about AI hallucination quotes hallucinatory model output as examples of the failure mode, the model trained on that paper learns the hallucinations as part of the distribution of scholarly discourse. When a security blog demonstrates a prompt injection attack by including the malicious prompt as a code example, the training corpus inherits the attack pattern.

This creates a closed loop: models produce text that describes model behaviour; that text becomes training data; subsequent models learn the described behaviour as a norm. The loop does not require any single actor to intend it. It is an emergent consequence of the combination of large-scale scraping and the increasing centrality of AI in public discourse.

### 10.7 The composite effect: ambient contamination and signal degradation

Each of the contamination sources described above is individually tolerable. A corpus with a small share of satirical text scraped without framing, some translationese, some benchmark leakage, some temporal inaccuracies, and some self-referential loops is a workable corpus. The concern is their composite effect: a corpus that simultaneously has all of these contamination sources at scale is a corpus with substantially degraded signal-to-noise ratio across multiple dimensions. The model trained on such a corpus inherits a systematically imprecise representation of the world — not incorrect in any single identifiable way, but unreliable across many dimensions simultaneously.

The composite contamination problem also interacts with the deliberate attack scenarios in §9: a corpus with high ambient accidental contamination is a corpus in which deliberate poisoning is harder to detect. Statistical anomaly detection for rare-token flooding (§9.2) or gradient-based poisoning (§9.3) is calibrated against a baseline of normal corpus composition. If the baseline is itself contaminated, the detection threshold rises. Accidental contamination provides cover for deliberate attack.

The governance implication is that corpus curation cannot focus exclusively on either quality (remove low-quality text) or security (remove deliberately poisoned text) in isolation. It requires a composite framework that addresses the full spectrum of contamination sources — accidental and deliberate, content-level and structural — as a unified problem of training substrate integrity.

## §9 — Deliberate Infection of the Commons: Adversarial Attacks on Future Training Substrates

### 9.1 The training corpus as attack surface

Sections §8 describes harmful patterns that propagate through emergent, uncoordinated dynamics — bias amplification, misinformation laundering, discourse normalisation. Each scenario involves actors pursuing local goals (generate content, publish papers, moderate at scale) whose aggregate effect produces a harmful corpus shift. No actor intends the outcome.

This section addresses a qualitatively different threat class: *deliberate, strategic attacks* on the training corpus as an attack surface, by actors who understand that today's text production shapes tomorrow's AI behaviour and who exploit that understanding to achieve specific objectives. The distinction matters for governance: emergent harms require design responses (architecture, incentives, transparency); deliberate attacks require security responses (detection, attribution, adversarial hardening).

The training corpus is a novel attack surface that existing security frameworks do not adequately address. It is not a database that can be locked; it is a distributed, continuously growing, and necessarily open collection of human-generated text from public sources. Its openness is not a bug — it is the property that makes it representative. But openness is also what makes it attackable.

### 9.2 Token-level attacks: rare characters and vocabulary manipulation

The tokenization layer — the mapping from raw text to the discrete symbols that language models process — is a structural vulnerability that most discourse about AI safety ignores. Two attack vectors operate at this level:

**Rare token flooding.** Language models learn representations for all tokens in their vocabulary, but representations for rare tokens are poorly calibrated — they are learned from fewer examples, so the model's internal representation of a rare token is more sensitive to the distribution of its limited occurrence in the corpus. If an adversary can systematically over-represent a rare token in the corpus, they can disproportionately influence how the model represents it relative to the investment required. The attack is asymmetric: a small volume of deliberately crafted text containing rare tokens can produce larger model behaviour changes than the same volume of text using common tokens.

The *SolidGoldMagikarp* phenomenon, first documented by independent researchers in early 2023 and subsequently formalised as the *under-trained token* class by Land & Bartolo (2024, EMNLP), illustrates the mechanism without deliberate attack: a cluster of anomalous tokens — tokens that were present in the GPT tokenizer vocabulary but had very low or zero frequency in the training corpus — caused erratic, destabilised model behaviour when prompted directly, including repetition loops, incoherence, and apparent hallucination. Land & Bartolo demonstrate that the phenomenon is not GPT-specific but generalises across major open-source model families (Llama, Mistral, Gemma, Phi) and that under-trained tokens can be automatically detected from embedding-weight signatures alone, prior to any behavioural probing. The anomalous behaviour emerges from the tokenizer/corpus mismatch, not from deliberate manipulation. A deliberate attacker who understands this mechanism can engineer the conditions rather than stumble upon them: create a token frequency distribution in a corpus contribution that causes a target token to behave as a trigger.

**Unicode steganography and homoglyph injection.** Boucher et al. (2022, IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy) demonstrate the canonical class of *imperceptible* NLP attacks: Unicode contains thousands of characters that are visually identical or near-identical to common ASCII characters but map to different code points — homoglyphs, directional override markers, zero-width joiners, variation selectors. Text that reads normally to a human reader may tokenize differently than visually equivalent text, because the tokenizer operates on code points, not visual appearance. Boucher and colleagues show that single-character Unicode substitutions can degrade the accuracy of production NLP systems (Google Cloud Translation, Microsoft Azure Translator, IBM Watson, Facebook fairseq) by 20–95% across translation, toxicity classification, and named-entity recognition tasks while remaining visually indistinguishable from the unperturbed input. The attack generalises to training-time corpus contributions: an adversary can inject Unicode-encoded content into GitHub commit messages, academic papers submitted to arXiv, or Wikipedia edits that is invisible to human review but affects tokenization and thereby affects what a model trained on the corpus learns.

The invisibility is the key property. Standard corpus review processes — which are human review processes — cannot detect Unicode steganography systematically. Automated detection requires specific tooling that is not currently standard in training data pipelines (Boucher et al., 2022, recommend NFKC normalisation and visual-confusable detection as minimum defenses). The attack surface therefore exploits the mismatch between human-readable text (which corpus curators review) and machine-processed text (which the model trains on).

**Vocabulary gap insertion.** A related attack: flooding the corpus with neologisms or rare technical terms in specific, misleading contexts. If a term that has an established technical meaning in one domain (e.g., a medical term) is systematically used in a different context in a large volume of corpus contributions, the next model generation learns a skewed representation of the term. This is a semantic rather than syntactic attack, operating on the meaning layer rather than the token layer, but its mechanism — corpus frequency manipulation — is the same.

### 9.3 Gradient-based data poisoning

Machine learning security research has established that training corpora are vulnerable to *data poisoning* attacks: the crafting of specific training examples designed to cause targeted misbehavior in the trained model (Schuster et al., 2021; Wallace et al., 2021). The poisoned examples look like normal training data to human reviewers; their effect is encoded in the gradient updates they produce during training.

For language models trained on public text corpora, the attack scenario is: an adversary contributes to a source that will be scraped into the training corpus — publishes papers on arXiv, commits code to GitHub, posts on Reddit — and crafts contributions that look legitimate but contain the poisoned pattern. The required volume of poisoned contributions is surprisingly small: Schuster et al. (2021) demonstrate that injecting fewer than 100 poisoned code examples into a corpus comparable to GitHub is sufficient to cause a code completion model to suggest insecure patterns in specific security-sensitive contexts. The attack is not random degradation but targeted: the model behaves normally in most contexts and produces the harmful behaviour only when specific trigger conditions are met.

The implications for the domains covered in this chapter are severe:

- **GitHub:** An actor with the ability to contribute code to public repositories — a capability available to any registered user — can inject poisoned code examples that cause future code models to suggest specific vulnerabilities in specific contexts. The attack is patient: the poisoned code enters the corpus when the repository is next scraped, takes effect when the next model generation is trained, and manifests when a developer triggers the targeted context in a deployed code assistant.

- **arXiv:** An actor with the ability to submit preprints — also available to any researcher with an institutional affiliation — can inject poisoned text that causes future language models to associate specific concepts, entities, or claims in targeted ways.

- **Web forums:** The barrier is lowest here. Forum contributions require no credentials and can be automated at scale. The poisoning capacity is lower per contribution but the volume available is higher.

### 9.4 Slow semantic drift: long-horizon manipulation

A distinct threat class exploits the temporal dimension of corpus construction. Training corpora are typically scraped over extended periods; the temporal composition of the corpus determines what historical period of human text the model learns from. An actor with a long-horizon strategy can attempt to shift the semantic distribution of a specific concept or term gradually across a corpus contribution period — not a sharp injection but a slow drift.

The mechanism: systematically contribute text to corpus sources over months or years in which a target concept is contextualised differently from its established use. If the contributions are individually plausible and constitute a sufficient share of the total corpus contribution for that concept, the model's learned representation of the concept drifts. The attack is hard to detect precisely because it is slow: no individual contribution is anomalous; the anomaly only becomes visible when the aggregate distribution is analysed against historical norms.

Candidate targets for slow semantic drift attacks include:
- **Legal and regulatory terms** (shifting the apparent meaning of "consent", "harm", "reasonable" in corpus text to affect AI-assisted legal interpretation)
- **Scientific concepts under active political contestation** (manipulating the apparent consensus around contested empirical questions)
- **Organisational and institutional names** (associating a target institution with specific framings across a large corpus contribution)

The attack is particularly suited to well-resourced state actors or large commercial organisations with the capacity to sustain a long-horizon operation without detection.

### 9.5 Supply-chain attacks on curated sources

The curation turn documented in Draft AX — the shift from "harvest everything" to quality-filtered, high-provenance training data — creates a new attack vector: *high-value curated sources become high-value targets*. If a model developer relies heavily on Wikipedia, arXiv, and the Linux kernel mailing list as high-quality anchors, those three sources become the adversarial chokepoints.

**Wikipedia as target:** Wikipedia's edit history and governance structure make it more resistant to rapid bulk manipulation than unmoderated sources. But persistent, patient manipulation of specific article content — the kind that exploits Wikipedia's processes rather than fighting them — has been documented in political and commercial contexts without AI amplification (Famiglietti, 2015; the Wiki-PR scandal). With AI-assisted editing, the cost of sustained manipulation drops and the detection challenge grows: AI-assisted edits are harder to attribute to coordinated campaigns because they do not share the stylistic fingerprints that human-coordinated campaigns produce.

**arXiv as target:** arXiv's moderation is light — it filters for basic scientific plausibility and institutional affiliation, not for subtle corpus-poisoning patterns. A coordinated campaign to flood arXiv with papers in a specific domain, crafted to establish a false empirical baseline, is operationally feasible for well-resourced actors.

**The open-source code commons:** The Linux kernel, CPython, and similar high-reputation repositories are specifically targeted by training data curators as high-quality code sources. A long-term contributor to these projects — one who has earned commit access through years of legitimate contribution — is in a position to introduce subtle poisoning that is protected by their established reputation. The attack exploits the trust infrastructure of the open-source community.

### 9.6 Feedback poisoning through model output re-indexing

A final attack vector exploits the fact that AI model outputs are themselves increasingly indexed and re-scraped. Perplexity AI responses appear in search results. ChatGPT answers are copied to Stack Overflow, Reddit, and personal blogs. Model outputs are cited in academic preprints. This creates an indirect attack path: rather than poisoning the training corpus directly, an attacker manipulates the outputs of a currently deployed model — through adversarial prompting, jailbreaking, or API manipulation — so that the poisoned outputs are re-indexed and appear in the next training cycle.

This attack is structurally analogous to the cache poisoning attacks known in DNS and web security: the legitimate infrastructure (the DNS resolver, the web cache, the training data pipeline) is used to propagate the adversarially injected content. The attack does not require access to the training process itself; it only requires the ability to generate content that will be scraped.

### 9.7 Governance implications

These threat scenarios share a common governance gap: the attack surface is distributed across the open web, and no single actor owns or controls it. The training data pipeline is an aggregation of contributions from millions of actors across thousands of sources; securing it against deliberate attack requires coordinating defences across all of those sources simultaneously.

Partial responses available with current technology:
- **Provenance tracking and cryptographic signing** for high-value curated sources (arXiv, major code repositories) — establishes a chain of custody for corpus contributions but does not prevent attacks from actors with legitimate signing credentials
- **Statistical anomaly detection** for token frequency distributions and Unicode composition in corpus contributions — detectable for large-scale attacks but computationally expensive at corpus scale and ineffective against slow drift attacks
- **Red-teaming the training pipeline** analogously to red-teaming model outputs — explicitly attempting to identify and exploit corpus vulnerabilities before deployment

The governance response ultimately requires reconceptualising training corpora not just as data infrastructure but as security-critical infrastructure with adversarial threat models. This is a significant shift from current practice, in which training data governance focuses on quality, diversity, and legal compliance rather than adversarial hardening.
