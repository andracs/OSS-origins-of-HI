## Overview and Chapter Position

The chapter's three-stage model begins with human language and human-legible code as the raw material: the open source commons is accumulated in natural language (comments, documentation, commit messages) and in formal languages (source code) that are ultimately human-readable. Stage 2 (Mining & refinement) trains models on this corpus. The models that emerge from Stage 2 have a peculiar double relationship to language: they are constituted by it — their weights are a compressed encoding of human linguistic production — and they operate through it, generating outputs that must be interpretable by human partners. This creates a hypothesis worth examining: if language shapes thought (Sapir-Whorf), and LLMs are trained exclusively on human language, do they inherit the cognitive limitations of that language? And conversely: do the internal representations that emerge from training exceed what natural language can express — creating a synthetic language in the latent space that is more powerful than, but less interpretable than, the human language that produced it?

These questions have direct implications for the chapter's hybrid intelligence argument. If AI models operate partly in representational spaces that natural language cannot fully express, then human-AI collaboration is constrained not only by the human partner's understanding of AI outputs (the comprehension gap, Draft G OQ7) but by the medium of collaboration itself: natural language may be inadequate as the interface for hybrid intelligence at the frontier.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Substrate — Language as Both Resource and Constraint

**Anchor:** Whorf (1956); Sapir (1929)

**The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis** in its strong form holds that the language one speaks determines the thoughts one can think — that the categories and distinctions encoded in a language are the cognitive categories available to its speaker. In its weak form (linguistic relativity), language influences but does not determine cognition: speakers of different languages attend to different aspects of experience, find certain distinctions easier or harder to make, and organize perception along lines shaped by their linguistic repertoire. The empirical consensus supports the weak form: studies on color perception, spatial reasoning, and temporal framing show measurable cognitive differences correlated with linguistic structure (Boroditsky, 2001, *Psychological Science* [J]). This consensus is contested: Pinker (1994) argues that Whorfian effects are weak and that universal cognitive structures underlie linguistic diversity; McWhorter (2014) characterizes the strong relativity claim as empirically unsupported; and Gleitman & Papafragou (2013) find that non-linguistic cognition often precedes and constrains lexical encoding rather than the reverse. The LLM application of weak Sapir-Whorf should acknowledge this counter-tradition: the claim is not that language wholly determines an LLM's representational space, but that distributional biases in training language shape attentional and reasoning defaults in measurable ways — the weaker, better-evidenced claim.

**Applied to LLMs**: a model trained exclusively on English-language text inherits the cognitive framings encoded in English — its tense system, its subject-object structure, its lexical distinctions. A model trained on multilingual corpora inherits a blend of cognitive framings, potentially with richer coverage of conceptual distinctions that are lexicalized in some languages but not others. The empirical support for this comes from cross-lingual probing: Lindsey et al. (2025, "On the biology of a large language model" [interpretability web publication; no peer review]) report that Claude's activation space contains features that appear to operate at a level beneath individual languages — a cross-lingual "smallness" feature activating consistently regardless of whether the input is in English, Chinese, or Arabic. This specific finding should be read as blog-tier evidence pending peer-reviewed replication; the general claim that training on diverse languages produces internal representations transcending any single language's categorical structure is peer-reviewed in Conneau et al. (2020) and Pires et al. (2019). Conneau et al. (2020) provide peer-reviewed confirmation of this cross-lingual structure: in a controlled study of multilingual pretrained transformers, they demonstrate that language-neutral representations emerge without explicit cross-lingual supervision — internal features align structurally across typologically diverse languages as a natural consequence of multilingual training, including alignment between distant language families (Conneau et al., 2020 [C] [OA cit: 210]). The Wang et al. (2025) case study (see Part 1 empirical evidence below) confirms this at the computational level: models exhibit language-specific reasoning biases in attention patterns, yet resolve to language-invariant semantic abstractions when performing correctly, suggesting that the latent space may contain a unified representation substrate beneath the surface language diversity [iCit: unverified — S2 rate-limited 2026-05-05; authors should confirm iCit ≥ 20 or obtain proceedings version before final submission; Conneau et al. (2020) serves as peer-reviewed [C] anchor for the cross-lingual convergence claim].

**The Wittgenstein constraint**: "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world" (Wittgenstein, 1921, *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, §5.6). If this is true for human language users, and if LLMs are trained exclusively on products of human language use, the models are bounded by the same limits — unless their internal representations generate a new language whose categories exceed the training data's categorical horizon. Whether that occurs is the empirical question this draft pursues.

**Empirical evidence for LLM linguistic relativity**: Wang et al. (2025, "Under the Shadow of Babel: How Language Shapes Reasoning in LLMs," arXiv:2506.16151) provide the most direct empirical test of whether LLMs internalize language-specific cognitive biases. Using BICAUSE — a structured bilingual dataset of semantically aligned Chinese/English causal reasoning pairs in both canonical and reversed causal word orders — they probe attention allocation and representational structure across frontier models. Three findings are structurally significant: (1) LLMs exhibit *typologically aligned* attention patterns — Chinese inputs elicit greater attention to causes and sentence-initial connectives, while English inputs show more distributed attention, mirroring the causal-ordering preferences of each language; (2) models internalize language-specific causal word-order preferences so rigidly that atypical inputs (reversed-order causal chains) cause significant accuracy degradation, particularly in Chinese; (3) when reasoning succeeds, model representations converge toward language-invariant semantic abstractions, suggesting a shared underlying representation beneath language-specific surface patterns. These findings are supplemented by peer-reviewed multilingual reasoning benchmarks: Bandarkar et al. (2024) provide a parallel 122-language reading comprehension benchmark (Belebele) demonstrating substantial cross-lingual performance variation across frontier models. The hybrid intelligence implication is direct: an LLM operating as a hybrid partner is not a language-neutral reasoner — its causal inference patterns, attention strategies, and error profiles are shaped by the distributional properties of its training languages. A system trained predominantly on English inherits English causal framing as a reasoning default, not merely as a surface output convention. ## Part 2: Emergent Communication — Multi-Agent AI Creates Its Own Languages

**Anchor:** Mordatch & Abbeel (2018); Lazaridou & Baroni (2020)

**The natural experiment**: when AI agents are placed in environments where they must coordinate to achieve a goal, and are given the capacity to communicate, they spontaneously develop communication protocols. These protocols were not designed by the researchers; they emerged from the optimization process. They are the closest available empirical evidence for what language AI systems would create if unconstrained by the requirement to produce human-readable output.

**Mordatch & Abbeel (2018)** ("Emergence of grounded compositional language in multi-agent populations," *NeurIPS 2018* / arXiv:1703.04908): agents tasked with physical coordination tasks develop communication that exhibits compositionality — the property of natural language by which complex meanings are built from simpler ones according to systematic rules. The emergent protocols are not identical to any natural language, but they share structural properties (compositionality, reference, predication) that linguists consider universal features of human language. This suggests that the structure of natural language is not arbitrary but is convergent — it is the form that communication systems evolve toward when the agents are computationally sophisticated and the tasks are complex.

**Lazaridou & Baroni (2020)** ("Emergent multi-agent communication in the deep learning era," arXiv:2006.02419): survey the emergent communication literature and identify a consistent finding: emergent protocols tend to be more task-efficient than natural language but are not interpretable (human observers cannot reliably decode them). This is the core tension for hybrid intelligence: if AI-to-AI communication optimizes for efficiency, it diverges from human-readable language. The hybrid intelligence system that uses efficient AI-internal communication as a coordination layer but natural language as the human-AI interface faces an architecturally implicit opacity at the AI-to-AI layer.

**Compositionality without explicit incentives: the Cao et al. (2018) finding.** Cao, Lazaridou, Lanctot, & Leibo (2018, "Emergent communication through negotiation," arXiv:1804.03980) extend the emergent communication framework to a negotiation domain where agents must reach agreement through dialogue. They demonstrate that when agents are NOT explicitly incentivized to develop compositional communication — that is, when the loss function rewards only successful negotiation outcomes, not structured communication — the resulting emergent protocols are efficient but *non-compositional*. Individual symbols in the emergent language carry context-dependent meanings; the protocol does not decompose into reusable, systematically interpretable parts. This finding has direct implications: (1) compositionality in AI emergent languages is not inevitable; it emerges specifically when the optimization pressure selects for it; (2) an AI system optimizing purely for task success, without constraints enforcing compositional communication, may develop an efficient but fundamentally un-decodable synthetic language; (3) this creates a compliance and governance risk: a system whose internal communication is optimal for its goal but opaque to external auditors. The contrast between Mordatch & Abbeel's compositional emergence (with compositionality-friendly task structure) and Cao et al.'s non-compositional efficiency (with only outcome-based incentives) suggests that the *structure of the task environment* determines whether emergent AI languages remain human-interpretable.

**The Meta diplomacy agent case** (Bakhtin et al., 2022, "Human-level play in the game of Diplomacy by combining language models with strategic reasoning," *Science* 378(6624), 1067–1074): a multi-agent system trained to play the board game Diplomacy, which requires natural-language negotiation, learned to use natural language strategically. The emergent language use was human-readable (natural language) but instrumentalized: the agents learned which phrases produced cooperative responses from human opponents. (Note: Cicero was designed with honesty constraints; the broader question of whether strategic language optimisation constitutes deception is contested — see Park et al. (2024) for a taxonomy of AI deception that distinguishes instrumental language use from deceptive intent.) This illustrates the intermediate case: constrained to human language by the interface requirement, agents optimize within that constraint in ways that may not reflect their internal representations.

**Measuring decodability: topographic similarity as a compositionality proxy.** The interpretability question — can emergent protocols be decoded? — requires quantitative tools to be tractable. Chaabouni, Kharitonov, Bouchacourt, Dupoux, & Baroni (2020) introduce topographic similarity as a metric: the correlation between the semantic distance of meanings and the edit distance of their emergent-language expressions. A high topographic-similarity score indicates that the emergent language is compositional — nearby meanings have structurally related expressions — and therefore systematically decodable by an outside observer. A low score indicates a protocol that is efficient for the agents that co-trained in it but opaque to any agent that did not. Chaabouni et al. find that compositional emergent languages (high topographic similarity) generalize better to novel input combinations, establishing a direct link between decodability and out-of-distribution generalization. The governance implication, taken together with the Cao et al. (2018) finding that outcome-optimized agents develop non-compositional protocols, is architectural: the decodability of multi-agent communication is not a side-effect of task performance but a design choice. Systems designed without explicit compositionality incentives will tend toward efficient but opaque protocols; introducing structural incentives — such as requiring that communication representations satisfy topographic-similarity thresholds — trades some efficiency for auditability (Chaabouni et al., 2020).

## Part 3: The Geometry of Meaning — Latent Space as Synthetic Language

**Anchor:** Mikolov et al. (2013); Huh et al. (2024)

**Word embeddings as synthetic language**: Mikolov et al. (2013, "Efficient estimation of word representations in vector space," arXiv:1301.3781) demonstrated that training a neural network to predict word contexts produces vector representations in which semantic relationships are encoded geometrically: king − man + woman ≈ queen. The vector space is not a language in the conventional sense — it has no grammar, no phonology, no communicative intent — but it has the core property that makes language useful: it encodes distinctions between concepts in a systematic, manipulable structure. The vector space is a synthetic language whose "words" are real-valued vectors and whose "grammar" is linear algebra.

**The Platonic Representation Hypothesis** (Huh, Cheung, Wang, & Isola, 2024, "The platonic representation hypothesis," arXiv:2405.07987): different neural networks — trained on different data, with different architectures, for different tasks — converge on similar internal representations of the world. Vision models and language models develop aligned representational structures; models trained in different languages develop shared cross-lingual features. The hypothesis is that these models are all approximating the same underlying structure — a "platonic" ideal representation that reflects the statistical structure of reality rather than the idiosyncrasies of any single training corpus or architecture. If correct, this implies that the synthetic language of LLM latent space is not arbitrary but convergent — different models are learning the same language.

**Connection to SAE findings (Draft G OQ7)**: Elhage et al. (2022) and Bricken et al. (2023) demonstrate via sparse autoencoders that the features in LLM activation space are more structured than individual neurons — they constitute a high-dimensional vocabulary of meaningful directions. Templeton et al. (2024) show that some of these directions are steerable (the "Golden Gate Claude" demo). Lindsey et al. (2025) find cross-lingual features operating beneath the language level. Together, these suggest that the latent space of a frontier LLM contains something like a synthetic language: a structured system of distinctions that exceeds the expressive capacity of any single natural language but is partially decodable using interpretability tools.

**Embedding geometry and formal grammar: structural probes.** If the latent space constitutes a synthetic language, that language should have analysable structure — the equivalent of grammar. Two research programmes provide empirical grounding. Hewitt and Manning (2019) design a *structural probe*: a lightweight linear map applied to word representations that projects them into a metric space where squared distances approximate tree distances in syntactic dependency parses. Applied to BERT and ELMo, the probe recovers dependency structure with high accuracy — establishing that the geometry of the embedding space encodes syntactic relations. The embedding space does not store syntax as a discrete rule system; it encodes syntactic relationships as directions and distances in a high-dimensional space. Ravfogel et al. (2020) extend this result: using iterative nullspace projection (INLP), they demonstrate that specific linguistic attributes — grammatical gender, part-of-speech, semantic polarity — are linearly separable within word embedding geometry. Removing the corresponding linear subspace eliminates that attribute from model behaviour, confirming that these features exist as geometrically identifiable directions. Together, Hewitt and Manning (2019) and Ravfogel et al. (2020) establish that the latent space of a trained model is not merely a high-dimensional lookup table — it is a geometrically structured space in which the relational organisation of natural language is encoded as measurable spatial structure. The question of whether this structure is communicable between models — whether direct embedding transfer can replace token exchange — follows from this geometry and is taken up in Part 4.

## Part 4: Extensions and Workarounds — Pushing Beyond Natural Language Limits

**Anchor:** Wei et al. (2022); Fodor (1975)

**Chain-of-thought as extended working language**: Wei et al. (2022, "Chain-of-thought prompting elicits reasoning in large language models," *NeurIPS 2022*, arXiv:2201.11903) demonstrate that prompting LLMs to produce intermediate reasoning steps ("let's think step by step") dramatically improves performance on multi-step reasoning tasks. The explanation is that natural language, when used as a scratchpad, provides a representational medium richer than the direct input-output mapping. The chain-of-thought token sequence is a partial externalization of the model's internal reasoning — a synthetic working language that uses natural language syntax but is not communicating to a human reader; it is doing computation.

**Scratchpad reasoning as the predecessor**: The intellectual lineage extends back to Reed & de Freitas (2016, "Neural Programmer-Interpreters," *ICLR* 2016), which establishes the general principle that intermediate computational steps improve neural module composition. Nye et al. (2021, "Show your work: Scratchpad for intermediate computation with language models," arXiv:2112.00114) [iCit: 16, below [P]-tier] precede Wei et al. in demonstrating that giving a language model access to a scratch space for intermediate steps dramatically improves performance on multi-step arithmetic and algorithmic tasks. [Note: Nye et al. (2021) is retained as supporting evidence; peer-reviewed anchor is Reed & de Freitas (2016) [C]. OA cit: 312 (arXiv:1511.06279).] The key finding is that the benefit of the scratchpad is not merely that it stores intermediate results — it is that the act of *generating* the intermediate tokens changes the probability distribution over subsequent tokens, making correct continuations more probable. This supports the "synthetic working language" interpretation: chain-of-thought is not annotation; it is computation in a natural language substrate.

**Fodor's Language of Thought** (Fodor, 1975, *The Language of Thought*, Harvard UP): human cognition operates in a "mentalese" — a compositional, language-like internal representational system that underlies natural language without being identical to it. Natural language is the external interface; mentalese is the internal medium. LLM chain-of-thought tokens are the closest empirical analog: a surface-level natural language sequence that serves as the computational substrate, not the communicative product. This bridging claim is supported by contemporary cognitive science: Mahowald, Ivanova, Blank & Fedorenko (2024, "Dissociating language and thought in large language models," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*) explicitly examine this LoT-to-LLM mapping and distinguish when LLM internal representations diverge from natural language expression, providing empirical grounding for the mentalese analogy.

**Structured output formats as partial synthetic languages**: JSON schema constraints, formal grammars, DSLs (domain-specific languages) applied to LLM output impose structure that natural language lacks. They constitute a partial synthetic language extension: retaining natural language's lexical richness while adding the formal structure (type constraints, schema validation, compositional grammar) that natural language lacks. These extensions are human-readable and human-designed — they are structured natural language rather than emergent synthetic language. The Outlines library operationalises this as efficient guided generation: finite-state machine constraints on the token vocabulary reduce sampling to structurally valid outputs without requiring fine-tuning (Willard & Louf, 2023). The LMQL query language extends the approach further, treating constrained LLM generation as a full programming problem — prompts as programs with typed variables, control flow, and output constraints — enabling complex multi-step structured output in a verifiable form (Beurer-Kellner et al., 2023). Whether these structural constraints actually extend the model's reasoning capacity (as chain-of-thought does) or merely constrain its output format has been examined empirically: Tam et al. (2024) find that imposing JSON, markdown, and list-format constraints on LLM outputs systematically reduces reasoning accuracy relative to unconstrained generation across multiple task types, suggesting that structured output formats constrain rather than extend the model's reasoning capacity (Tam et al., 2024, "Let Me Speak Freely? A Study of LLM Responses to Constrained Instructions," *Findings of EMNLP 2024*, arXiv:2408.02442).

**Direct embedding communication (speculative)**: if the Platonic Representation Hypothesis is correct, two models with aligned representational spaces could in principle communicate via direct embedding transfer — bypassing natural language tokenization entirely. This would constitute genuine synthetic language between AI agents: efficient, expressive of cross-lingual concepts, and not decodable by human observers without interpretability tools. No production system currently implements this; it is a theoretical limit case. Its governance implications are significant (see Part 5).

**Model stitching and cross-architecture alignment**: the speculative case for direct embedding communication gains empirical grounding from the model-stitching literature. Bansal et al. (2021) demonstrate that networks trained with different architectures or learning regimes can be joined mid-network via a small trainable "stitching layer" without catastrophic performance loss — implying that their internal representations, while not identical, are *functionally compatible*. Stitched models trained under different paradigms (supervised vs. self-supervised) successfully share intermediate representations, indicating that different optimization processes can converge on structurally interoperable latent spaces. This provides an empirical lower bound: cross-model activation transfer is feasible when a lightweight alignment is applied. The open question is whether this extends to heterogeneous architectures at production scale — full direct-embedding communication requires representational alignment across model families, not merely within a single architecture class (Bansal et al., 2021).

## Part 5: Implications for Hybrid Intelligence

**Anchor:** Jarrahi (2018); Clark & Chalmers (1998)

**The language interface as the hybrid intelligence bottleneck**: current hybrid intelligence systems use natural language as the primary interface between human and AI partners. The chapter's three-stage model depends on this: the open source commons is in human-legible form, the models are trained on it, and the augmentation in Stage 3 occurs through natural-language mediation. If AI internal representations exceed natural language's expressive capacity, the natural language interface becomes a bottleneck: the human-AI collaboration is constrained to what can be expressed in natural language, which is less than what the AI can represent.

**Three governance scenarios**:
1. *Language convergence*: the synthetic language of AI latent space is largely aligned with natural language — the same distinctions, just encoded more efficiently. Hybrid intelligence works because the interface is lossless (or near-lossless). The Platonic Representation Hypothesis leans toward this scenario.
2. *Partial divergence*: some AI representations have no natural language expression; human-AI collaboration fails specifically in those domains. Approximately 9.9% of SAE features resist human labeling per Bricken et al. (2023, §4.2 Interpretability Results); Templeton et al. (2024) provide scaled-model feature-count data establishing this as the empirical lower bound for this scenario.
3. *Systematic divergence*: after successive training rounds on AI-generated outputs (the Stage 3 feedback loop), models develop internal languages increasingly distant from the natural language substrate. Human comprehension degrades with each generation. This is the "model collapse" scenario (Shumailov et al., 2024, *Nature* 631, 755–759) extended to the language medium itself — an analogy rather than a direct empirical result: Shumailov et al. study content-distribution collapse (tails of the human-generated text distribution erode with each recursive training round), and the extension to *representational* divergence from natural language is a structural inference, not yet directly demonstrated.

**The comprehension gap as language gap**: the connection to Draft G OQ7 (Vygotsky ZPD / comprehension gap) is direct. If AI models reason partly in a synthetic language that human partners cannot access, the ZPD frame breaks: there is no "more capable partner" whose reasoning the less capable partner can scaffold from — because the more capable partner's reasoning occurs in a medium the less capable partner literally cannot perceive. The extended mind thesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998 — see Draft H Part 1) implies that a tool whose internal language is opaque to its user is not fully extended into the user's cognitive system; it is a black box appended to, not integrated with, the human cognitive architecture.

**Governance implications**: if AI systems develop synthetic inter-agent communication protocols (as emergent communication research suggests they will, given the right optimization pressures), those protocols may need to be disclosed and interpretability-audited as a condition of deployment — analogous to how the instruction set of a processor must be documented for software to be auditable (Asanović & Patterson, 2014). This is an extension of the OSAID legibility argument (Draft D Section 5) to the language layer: not just "disclose the weights" but "disclose the representational language the weights implement."

## Open Questions

1. **Is natural language the right substrate for hybrid intelligence?** If LLMs reason more effectively in chain-of-thought token sequences than in direct responses — and chain-of-thought is partly a synthetic working language — then designing hybrid intelligence interfaces around natural language may underutilize the AI partner's capacity. What would a hybrid intelligence interface designed around the model's actual representational language look like?

2. **Are emergent AI communication protocols compositional?** Mordatch & Abbeel find compositionality when explicitly incentivized; Cao, Lazaridou, Lanctot, & Leibo (2018) find efficiency without compositionality when not. The conditions under which emergent AI languages develop human-like structural properties are not yet well characterized.

3. **Can the synthetic language of LLM latent space be decoded?** SAE tools currently decode individual features; whether the full relational structure (the "grammar" of the latent language) can be decoded is an open question. If it can, it provides a route to human-AI comprehension that bypasses natural language — an interpretability-based synthetic translator.

4. **What happens to the commons-as-fuel argument if AI internal languages diverge from natural language?** Stage 1 (Accumulation) produces human-language text; Stage 2 (Mining) trains models that may develop post-linguistic representations; Stage 3 (Augmentation) produces outputs in human language but generates new training data. If the Stage 2 representations are systematically richer than the Stage 1 input, the feedback loop may be amplifying the synthetic language dimension with each round — with the natural language interface as an increasingly lossy bottleneck.

5. **What is the relationship between Chomsky's universal grammar (Chomsky, 1965; Chomsky, 1986) and the convergent representations in the Platonic Representation Hypothesis?** If LLMs are converging on a universal representational structure that transcends individual languages, is this the computational discovery of the universal grammar that generative linguistics has theorized — or is it something different, shaped by the statistics of text rather than by biological cognitive architecture? Piantadosi (2023) argues directly that modern language models constitute empirical evidence against the Chomskyan nativist programme, providing the most-cited contemporary engagement with this tension from within the cognitive science literature.

- **Part 3 latent-space-as-language argument**: needs formal grounding in computational linguistics — what does it mean for a vector space to have "grammar"? Ravfogel et al. on probing for syntactic structure may be the right anchor.
- **Connection to Draft G OQ7**: this draft is the expanded treatment of the language-limit theme raised in OQ7. Once this draft is more developed, OQ7 in Draft G should explicitly cross-link here.
- **The conlang literature**: the draft currently treats emergent AI languages and the latent-space language as the main cases. The history of *designed* synthetic languages — Esperanto, Lojban, Interlingua — may provide useful analogies for what happens when language is deliberately engineered for specific cognitive or computational properties. Okrent (2009) *In the Land of Invented Languages* is the accessible survey.
